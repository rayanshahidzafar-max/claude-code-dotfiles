# Contact and Collision

Use this whenever two peoples, factions or civilizations meet: trade, conquest, first contact, a border war.

## Analyze the collision like Cajamarca

Pose the question as a chain of whys, from the moment of contact backward. [Diamond ch3]

1. **Why did one side win the encounter?** List the proximate advantages: weapons, armor, mounts or vehicles, organization, surprise.
2. **Why was the loser there, in that state, on that day?** Look for disease, civil war, or a succession crisis that a prior plague set off.
3. **Why did the winner get there at all, instead of the reverse?** Look at transport technology and a centralized power able to fund the venture.
4. **Why did the loser walk into the trap?** Information: who knew what about whom, and how they came to know it.
5. **Then push to ultimate causes** with `causal-chain.md`.

Remember that central power can be a weakness too. Take the god-king and the whole chain of command falls into the enemy's hands intact. [Diamond ch3]

## Information asymmetry

- One side may know almost nothing about the other. Its picture may come from a single scout who saw the strangers on a bad day. The other side may carry recorded knowledge of many past encounters, and may even copy a strategy that worked before. That gap decides who springs the trap. [Diamond ch3]
- In story terms, the reader's position (mystery, suspense or dramatic irony) can mirror the characters' information gap. [McKee ch16]

## Contagion in contact

- The side that has lived with dense crowds and close animals carries plagues it has become resistant to. The other side has neither the plagues nor the resistance. The disease can run ahead of the invaders and empty the land before they arrive. [Diamond ch11]
- Exchange can be lopsided: nothing deadly may travel back. [Diamond ch11]
- Disease can defend as well as attack. A region's native fevers can keep invaders out for centuries. [Diamond ch11]
- From the germ's point of view, symptoms are strategies for spreading itself: coughing, sores, madness. Build any invented contagion by asking how its symptoms help it spread. [Diamond ch11]

## Technology transfer

- **Borrowing beats inventing.** Most new technology in a connected society arrives from elsewhere. How much a society invents for itself depends on how hard the thing is to invent and how close its neighbors are. [Diamond ch13]
- **Four things decide whether an invention is taken up.** [Diamond ch13]
  1. Its economic advantage *here*: a wheel is worthless without draft animals.
  2. Its prestige: people will pay more for status.
  3. Whether it fits vested interests: a worse standard, once entrenched, survives.
  4. How easy the advantage is to see: once someone watches it work, they want it.
- **Innovativeness is scattered.** With so many independent factors at work, receptive and conservative societies exist side by side everywhere. Across a large enough region, some society will always be innovating. [Diamond ch13]
- **Technology can be lost or refused.** An isolated people can forget skills. A proud warrior class can ban a weapon that threatens its prestige. One court faction can scrap an entire fleet. Refusal lasts only where neighbors can't punish it. [Diamond ch13]
- **Recombination.** Big leaps come from combining existing pieces. The same idea can appear early in a place without the supporting pieces and go nowhere. [Diamond ch13]

## Writing as a weapon

- Written orders, maps and reports let a distant power act as one, and published accounts of a conquest bring more conquerors. [Diamond ch12]
- Where writing belongs only to the elite, the official record says what the rulers need it to say. [Diamond ch12]
