# Worldbuilding

A world built from causes: every institution, weapon, belief and grudge in it traces back through a chain to geography and starting resources. It produces a **world dossier** (`references/world-dossier.md`) that holds only what the story needs, and it ends with the pressure points where the world squeezes the characters. It never produces an encyclopedia of trivia.

**Important:** never quote or reproduce text from the source books. Paraphrase the ideas, and build the person's own world, not Earth's history renamed. Diamond offers his factors as broad tendencies with a large unexplained residue. Use them as a generator for fiction, not as settled fact about real peoples.

## The core ideas

- Big differences between societies come from differences in their environments and starting conditions, not from innate differences between their peoples. [Diamond, Prologue]
- Separate proximate causes from ultimate ones. Guns, germs, steel, ships and states are proximate: they are *why* one side won. The ultimate question is why that side had them. Always push one more "why" back. [Diamond, Prologue]
- Keep asking why, like a therapist tracing a marriage's breakdown past the slap, past the affair, to the cause underneath. Institutions that look like causes are often effects. [Diamond, Afterword]
- One-sided outcomes on a large scale need deep explanations, not the luck of a single battle. [Diamond, Prologue]
- Explaining how something happened is not justifying it. Understanding a chain of causes is how characters, and readers, learn to break it. [Diamond, Prologue]
- Causal chains are long and cross fields: an asteroid for the dinosaurs, a cold century for a colony. From inside the chain, nobody sees the end coming. [Diamond, Epilogue]
- History runs on statistical tendencies, not laws. You can predict the pattern across many societies, never the fate of one. [Diamond, Epilogue]
- Cultural quirks and individual people are wild cards. They can nudge events, but they don't explain the broad pattern. That makes them perfect material for a protagonist. [Diamond, Epilogue]

## How to use this in practice

1. **Scope it.** Decide what the story needs: one city, a border between two peoples, or a whole world's history. Build only that deep. [McKee ch3]
2. **Run the causal chain** in `references/causal-chain.md`, from geography through starting resources, surplus, density, society type, legitimacy, writing, technology and contagion. Write down one sentence of cause for each step.
3. **Place the societies** on the band → tribe → chiefdom → state ladder with `references/societies.md`, including how each keeps its elite in power.
4. **If peoples collide,** analyze the collision with `references/contact.md`: whose advantages are proximate, where they came from, and who knows what about whom.
5. **Consider a natural experiment.** Split one founding culture into different environments and let centuries pass. Siblings who have forgotten each other make a built-in comparison for the reader (see `societies.md`, the Polynesian gradient).
6. **Add deep time.** Decide who once led and lost the lead: through ecological self-destruction, or through a single unified decision that shut a door for everyone (see `causal-chain.md`, deep time).
7. **Add the wild cards:** the cultural quirk nobody can explain, and the individual whose choice bent a local outcome. Put your protagonist in this space.
8. **Fill in the dossier** and end with pressure points: the places where the world's causes land on one person's life.

## Turning the world into story

- **One person, one scene.** Diamond opens nearly every chapter this way: one person in one concrete scene, before widening to the principle. Do the same. Let the world arrive through one character's specific problem. [Diamond, Prologue]
- **Let witnesses carry the drama.** Put the analysis after the scene. The people who were there tell it in fear, pride and self-justification. [Diamond ch3]
- **Victors write the record.** Official accounts frame conquest as virtue or destiny. A world's own chronicles should be as slanted as that, and the gap between record and event is a story engine. [Diamond ch3]
- **Setting has four dimensions:** period, duration, location, and level of conflict (inner, personal, institutional, environmental). The cast is part of the setting. [McKee ch3]
- **Keep the world small and knowable.** A world small enough to know completely lets you answer any question about it at once. The bigger the world, the thinner the knowledge and the more clichéd the story. [McKee ch3]
- **Rules bind the writer.** Once a world's rule is established, it can't be broken. The fantastic gets one great leap from reality, then must obey strict probability. [McKee ch2]
- **Ask the world questions.** How do people make a living, play, pray, love? Who holds power, arranged as a pyramid even inside families? What are the rituals, the values, the line between legal and right? What would people die for? [McKee ch8]
- **Authenticity is consistency plus the telling detail,** not accuracy to the real world. A convincing impossibility beats an unconvincing possibility. [McKee ch8]
- **Clichés come from not knowing your world.** Research it from memory, from imagination (live a day of a character's life), and from fact. [McKee ch3]
- **Only what bears on the struggle.** World detail earns its place only when it shows through a character's struggle; description with no story purpose is sightseeing. Make every abstract force concrete in one person's specific trouble. [Cron ch6]
- **Theme sets the limits.** A story's theme decides what's possible in its universe: whether the world is loving, indifferent or cruel. The causes you choose should make that world. [Cron ch2]
- **No portable stories.** An honest story belongs to one place and one time, and couldn't be moved without becoming a different story. If your plot would work unchanged in any setting, the world isn't doing its job yet. [McKee ch3]
- **Know the genre's world.** List what the worlds of your genre always contain: settings, roles, events, values. Keep the conventions and find a fresh way to meet each one. [McKee ch4]
- **Invent more than you use.** Generate five to twenty times more world than the story shows, and choose. [McKee ch3]
- **History as backstory.** World history is a garden of past events to harvest for revelations at the story's turning points. It isn't a preamble to recite. [McKee ch8]
- **Widen the stakes through a role.** Protagonists are so often soldiers, doctors, judges and officials because their choices ripple into society. Give your protagonist a role that connects their private trouble to the world's forces. [McKee ch12]
- **Give the reader a yardstick.** Early on, the reader needs just enough of the big picture to measure what's happening against it, and no more. [Cron ch1]
- **Only the history the story needs.** A character needs only the backstory this story uses, and so does a world. [Cron ch5]
- **Name things.** Real worlds are densely named: trades, tools, streets, weather. Invent the names your people would actually use, and use them exactly. [Klinkenborg]
- **Research stays in the back.** Most of the dossier never reaches the page directly. It flavors the story like spice; it isn't the dish. [King, On Writing]
- **Bring real knowledge.** Even in a strange world, the texture of real work and real life makes it believable. [King, On Writing]
- **Find the truth inside the invention.** The job of fiction is to find the truth inside a web of made-up things. The invented world has to tell the truth about people. [King, On Writing]
- **Invent now, verify later.** When drafting, make up the fact you need and check it against the dossier in revision. Never stop the draft to worldbuild. [King, On Writing]

## Where the books disagree

- **How much world before story?** Diamond's engine could fill volumes. King puts research in the back, and Cron allows only what bears on the protagonist. Resolution used here: run the whole chain in notes, but let the dossier's final section, the pressure points, decide what reaches the page. Everything else stays in the drawer as the iceberg below the waterline, as McPhee would put it.
- **Big forces or individual choice?** Diamond explains broad patterns and treats individuals as wild cards. McKee says story is individual choice under pressure. Resolution: the world supplies the pressure, and the character supplies the choice. Use Diamond to make the pressure real and inevitable, and McKee to make the choice free and costly.
- **Realism or consistency?** Diamond reasons from real-world evidence. McKee values internal consistency over resemblance to reality. Resolution: borrow Diamond's causal logic, which works in any world, and keep McKee's consistency test as the judge. No real-world fact has to be true in the story world, but every story-world fact must follow from its causes.
