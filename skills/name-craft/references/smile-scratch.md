# SMILE, SCRATCH and the Scorecard

## SMILE in detail

**Suggestive**
- Suggest something about the thing through metaphor rather than description. A store named for the world's biggest river could grow from books to everything without outgrowing its name, which a "book barn" never could. Coined names can work too, when they marry two words cleverly and stay easy to spell and say, though most coinages fail that test. [Watkins ch1]
- Mine the personality adjectives for metaphors. Rugged vehicles take the names of wild places and expeditions. [Watkins ch1]
- Don't cram "trust" or "quality" into a name; it sounds disingenuous. Add weight with a sturdy second word (Group, Robotics, Global), and earn credibility with proof elsewhere. [Watkins ch1]

**Meaningful**
- The name has to mean something to the audience, because most of the time you won't be around to explain it. [Watkins ch1]
- Don't name a business after yourself. Strangers don't know you, your name may be hard to spell or say, and it says nothing about what you do. If your name invites good wordplay, use the wordplay, and think about what happens if you ever sell the company. [Watkins ch1]

**Imagery**
- A name you can picture is a name you remember years later. Picture a flower; now picture an invented drug-sounding word. [Watkins ch1]
- Any category can have imagery, even dog food flavours. [Watkins ch1]
- Verbs and concrete nouns put a picture in the head; abstract states don't. [Edwards, Q&A]

**Legs**
- Ask what the name can extend to: taglines, product lines, titles, rooms, newsletters, sign-offs, merchandise. If people would wear it on a shirt, it has legs. [Watkins ch1]
- Version names can be plain numbers (clear but dull), a theme (fun, like desserts), or an initial letter with plenty of words behind it. Avoid starting names with X. [Watkins ch1]

**Emotional**
- Buying is about half emotion. Shoppers pick wine for a label that makes them smile. [Watkins ch1]
- Renaming plain services with emotionally engaging names lifted a hotel's wedding sales by about a quarter, with nothing else changed. A dish sells better when "soup" becomes grandma's soup. [Watkins ch1]
- Emotion lives in what the thing means to someone, not in what it is. Ask "so what?" until you reach a feeling. [Edwards S10]

## SCRATCH in detail

**Spelling-challenged**
- If you have to spell it aloud, voice dictation mangles it, or it reads like a typing error, cut it. [Watkins ch2]
- Don't swap numbers for words; every mention becomes "the numeral two". The voice test catches most spelling trouble: say the name into a phone's dictation and see what appears, then ask a voice assistant to search for it. [Watkins ch2]

**Copycat**
- Riding a competitor's name is lazy, breeds distrust and invites trademark claims. One frozen-yogurt success spawned a dozen look-alike "-berry" names. Tired patterns are copycats too: animal-plus-word trends, rocket and daddy names, i/e/u prefixes, double-O spellings, fruit names, "cloud", and a random colour plus a noun. [Watkins ch2]
- Unrelated brands can share a name when context prevents confusion, as soap and a chocolate bar do, but never borrow an iconic one. [Watkins ch2]

**Restrictive**
- A name tied to one product, price, place or opening hours will be outgrown, and the tagline ends up apologizing for the name. [Watkins ch2]
- Don't give the company and its product the same name; the company name must leave room for every future product. Launching both at once? Name the product first, because buyers remember one name. [Watkins ch2]

**Annoying**
- Mashups that don't sound natural, vowels bolted onto real words, and trendy suffixes (-ology, -topia, -palooza and friends) all sound forced. [Watkins ch2]
- Don't be mysterious. A random name leaves people unable to guess what you do, and uninterested in finding out. Founders' initials and ungrammatical names annoy customers too, and set a bad example. [Watkins ch2]
- Don't overstate. A superlative in a name (best, ultimate, number one) makes people doubt everything else you claim. [S&W V.7]
- Avoid fancy words. A plain, lively word beats a grand one. [S&W V.14]

**Tame**
- Descriptive names take no imagination, sound like everyone else, and are hard to trademark. A crowd of "cloud something" names blurs into one. [Watkins ch2]
- Descriptive names do earn their place where people must pick quickly among options, as in shipping speed tiers. [Watkins ch2]
- A name must still signal its category where buyers browse. A punchy one-word series name that could have belonged to any genre lost out to a longer one that said science fiction. [Tomlinson ch24]

**Curse of knowledge**
- Insiders can't unlearn what they know. Code names, acronyms, chemistry and model numbers mean nothing outside. [Watkins ch2]
- Turn model numbers into names people can feel. A bedroom speaker dock became a name about moonlight. [Watkins ch2]
- A confused mind says no. Jargon that proves how smart you are makes buyers feel stupid. [Edwards S13]
- For anything aimed at teens or young adults, look the name up in the slang dictionary, and drop it if the bad definitions are popular. [Watkins ch2]
- The foreign-word horror story you heard in business school is a myth, but real slips happen; get a professional check for a global brand. [Watkins ch2]

**Hard to pronounce**
- If people aren't sure how to say a name, they won't say it at all, because nobody wants to say it wrong in front of others. [Watkins ch2]
- Don't set a name in all capitals, rely on punctuation, colour or odd capitals to show how it's said, or spell it backward, and avoid prefixes with two common pronunciations ("eco-"). The name has to survive being printed plainly in a newspaper, with no colours or tricks. [Watkins ch2]
- Use a name in public and keep any acronym internal; people remember one name, not two. [Watkins ch2]
- Spell out initials unless everyone already knows them. [S&W V.19]
- Don't sprinkle foreign words to sound sophisticated. [S&W V.20]
