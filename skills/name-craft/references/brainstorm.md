# Brainstorming Names

## The method

- Group brainstorms fail. Extroverts dominate, introverts stay quiet, people defer to the boss, nobody applies an objective filter, and the winner is whatever met the least resistance. [Watkins ch5]
- The best brainstorming team is one person at a computer. The internet holds everything you need, and its side paths lead to ideas you'd never reach in a meeting room. [Watkins ch5]
- Open your mind and play: flip the idea over, look from above, pair unlikely things, and don't shoot anything down yet. [Watkins ch5]
- Write everything down, even near-misses, sorted into Spot On, Maybe and Sparks. A spark one person writes can become another person's winner. [Watkins ch5]
- Warm up with at least twelve spark words drawn from the brief, then run every tool on one spark before moving to the next. [Watkins ch5]
- Push past the obvious. Force ten answers to every question; the first few are easy and shared by everyone, and the useful ones come after. [Edwards S3]
- Expect weeks and a hundred-plus names for anything important. [Watkins ch5]

## The tools

- **Thesaurus.** Read the synonyms of one spark, and look at each for a second meaning that fits the brand. A word for the chill of cold can also be the shiver of a teenage crush. [Watkins ch5]
- **Dictionary.** It is the destination, where the thesaurus is only a rest stop: it separates the shades of near-synonyms, and the definition of a weak word often contains the strong one. [McPhee, Draft No. 4]
- **Image search.** Pictures throw up words you'd never type: snow play leads to snow sports, which leads to their lingo. [Watkins ch5]
- **Glossaries, lingo and slang** of related worlds. Many terms are too insider, but some work whether or not you know the jargon, like a boarding term for a shaky ride that also suggests chattering teeth and chattering friends. [Watkins ch5]
- For a metaphor, search the glossary of something that shares the quality. To suggest speed, look at racing vocabulary. [Watkins ch5]
- **Idioms and clichés** are among the richest sources, because familiar phrases arrive pre-loved. Trim them to fit. [Watkins ch5]
- Ration the puns. Wordplay comes too cheaply, and a writer who once turned out a thousand pun headlines gave them up for good. [McPhee, Omission]
- **Folk tales and ballads** travel without owners, and their patterns repeat with small changes, so names drawn from them feel familiar and new at once. [Uncanny, Dean]
- **Searching around the topic** ("coldest places on earth") turns up places, people and facts that make funny, apt names. [Watkins ch5]
- **Pop culture, film, book and song titles** make sticky names because they are already familiar. Titles of books and songs generally can't be trademarked, which makes them open sources, though franchises are another matter. Sort songs by popularity, since well-known ones carry the emotion. [Watkins ch5]
- **Rhyme, idiom and word-graph sites** widen the net. [Watkins ch5]
- **Real trade vocabulary.** Every craft has exact names for its tools and moves. Learn them; they are more vivid than anything you could invent. [Klinkenborg]
- **Etymology.** Dig out a word's old root and it can come back with its teeth. [Uncanny, Jarvis]
- **A swipe file.** Collect names that made you look twice, from any industry, and reread them before a session to warm up. [Edwards S30]
- Study the names that made you spend your own money. You are usually a member of your own audience. [Edwards S5]

## How a winner turns up

- The winning name for a frozen-yogurt chain came from an image search of spoons in cups: a playful phrase that passed SMILE and SCRATCH and gave the brand years of jokes for signage and shirts. You can't predict where the winner will come from, so keep searching. [Watkins ch5]
- Pop-culture names work even on people who never saw the original. [Watkins ch5]
