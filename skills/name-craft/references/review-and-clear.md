# Reviewing, Clearing and Renaming

## Before the review

- Add a line or two explaining why beside each name to help it land, and head the list with the acid-test sentence. [Watkins ch6]

## The twelve review rules

1. Review the list independently first, not in a meeting, so nobody echoes the boss. [Watkins ch6]
2. Ask "Is it right?" rather than "Do I like it?" [Watkins ch6]
3. Comment only on what works. Negative remarks never build consensus. [Watkins ch6]
4. Remember a name can't say everything. Let it hint at the offer or one good experience. [Watkins ch6]
5. Read the list on paper, several times and in both directions, over a few days. [Watkins ch6]
6. Don't survey friends, family or strangers. Asking invites criticism and shows a lack of confidence, and the result is the least-resisted name. Announce the name; don't ask about it. [Watkins ch6]
7. Imagine each name in context: on caller ID, a badge, a sign, a website, a card, the product on the shelf. Names rarely appear alone. [Watkins ch6]
8. Don't be afraid of different. The best names often feel unfamiliar at first, and a little discomfort means you're doing something new. [Watkins ch6]
9. Don't check domains yet, and don't drop a name for lack of the exact .com. [Watkins ch6]
10. Each reviewer picks at least ten names. [Watkins ch6]
11. Don't fall for any name before trademark screening. Expect 30–50% to be taken; trendy industries are the most crowded. [Watkins ch6]
12. Collect the lists, find the overlap, then meet to discuss the strongest, rank the top five, and start screening. [Watkins ch6]

## Focus groups and outside readers

- Don't focus-group names. Groups drift to the safest name, and many famous brand names would have died in one, because every bold name invites an easy objection. [Watkins ch6]
- An outside reader is useful for clarity alone. Ask someone outside the field what they think the thing is, and whether the name confused them, never whether they like it. [Edwards S13]
- Take a stand. A name that pleases everyone moves nobody. [Edwards S21]

## Domains

- An available .com should never be the main criterion. Desperation for one produces misspellings and silly extensions. People who land on the wrong site simply search again. [Watkins ch3]
- Add a word: a verb or noun modifier around the name (get, try, my, the; app, co, hq, store). It also helps search engines find you. [Watkins ch3]
- Use a phrase: a short sentence as the address can be friendlier and more memorable than the name itself, and it can redirect to the main site. [Watkins ch3]
- Other extensions such as .net are fine. Don't use a country code to spell the name, and don't use .org unless you're a nonprofit. [Watkins ch3]
- Not every good name is taken; parked names can be bought with a low offer, and common misspellings of hard words are worth buying and redirecting. Longer addresses are fine, because browsers fill them in and meaning beats brevity. [Watkins ch3]
- Before you buy, read the words run together to check they don't spell something unintended. [Watkins ch3]

## Trademarks

- Owning a domain does not give you the trademark. Clear the name before you buy or build on it. [Watkins ch3]
- Search first for free (the web, registration-search sites, the government database), then use a professional screening service, then go to a trademark attorney for full clearance. [Watkins, Resources]
- Google every finalist, including as a person's name. A good restaurant name was killed the day a man of that name made the news. [Watkins, Resources]
- Protect the mark from becoming a generic word. Use it as an adjective with the generic noun, mark it with ™ or ®, keep its form exact, use it across a line, and object to misuse. [Watkins, Resources]
- Invest in professional identity design; nothing cheapens a good name like a cheap logo. [Watkins, Resources]

## Renaming

- It's never too late to rename. Email, redirects and social media carry customers across. [Watkins ch7]
- Weigh the case for renaming (a fresh brand, no more apologizing, a reason to contact every customer, more years ahead than behind) against the case against (attachment, buy-in, hurt feelings, reprinting costs, admitting the old name failed). Most future customers will only ever know the new name. [Watkins ch7]
- A name can carry the hook (curiosity) or the point of difference, but rarely both. Decide which job the name does and let the tagline do the other. [Edwards S29]
