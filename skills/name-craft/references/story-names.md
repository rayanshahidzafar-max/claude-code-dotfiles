# Names Inside a Story

Characters, places, ships, factions, spells and the things people in the story use. Run SMILE and SCRATCH with the story's world as the brief: who coined the name, who says it, and what the reader must feel on meeting it.

## Make them sayable and memorable

- A reader meets a name alone on the page, with nobody to explain it. If they can't tell how to say it, they stumble every time it appears. [Watkins ch2]
- Names that carry a picture stick. People remember others whose names call up a thing they can see. [Watkins ch1]
- Shorten a galumphing name. A shorter surname saves lines and reads better aloud. [King, And Furthermore]
- A working name is a placeholder. Expect a better one to arrive as the draft goes on. [King, And Furthermore]
- Name things specifically. A generic noun gives the reader nothing; a specific name gives a picture. [Cron ch6]
- Description comes alive through the exact names of things: the right tool, plant or move rather than a vague category. [McKee ch18]

## Who gets a name

- Don't name a minor character unless they speak for a while or matter to the plot. Every name asks the reader to remember someone. [Scheuerer ch11]
- Three or more newly named characters in one scene are hard to follow. Bring people in a few at a time, each with a strong first impression. [Tomlinson ch10]
- Who is named and who isn't is itself a signal. A tale can name the man and leave the woman nameless, and the gap says something about power. [Uncanny, Emelumadu]

## Names that do quiet work

- A name can be a slightly wrong detail: a professional whose name sounds made up, or a guest house whose name begs the question of who would choose it. [Uncanny, Moore]
- Name places exactly, not "a one-way system" but that one. A real-sounding specific place does more than a generic one. [Uncanny, Budden]
- Naming an event without explaining it plants a question the reader carries forward, which is a light form of foreshadowing. [Scheuerer ch12]
- Old tales and ballads are open sources. One famous story kept its demon's name from the ballad it borrowed. [Uncanny, Emelumadu]
- Names can carry a world's history. Newcomers keep the earlier inhabitants' place names, especially for rivers and capes, so a map can show who lived there first. [Diamond ch20]
- Old shared words reveal what a people had long ago, and new inventions get unrelated names in each daughter language. Let old things share roots across your cultures and new things not. [Diamond ch17]

## Keep a names list

- Record every name in the series bible: full names, nicknames, childhood and former names. [Tomlinson ch17]
- A bible's minimum contents include names, places and world specifics, so later books stay consistent. [Rosett, Practicalities]
- Give a family of names a shared pattern when they come from one culture, the way a brand's sub-brands share a theme. Families make a world feel coherent. [Watkins ch1]
