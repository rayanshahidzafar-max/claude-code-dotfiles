# Name Craft

This skill names things so that people get them and like them. It produces:
- **a naming brief** (`references/naming-brief.md`);
- **a long list** sorted into Spot On, Maybe and Sparks;
- **a SMILE and SCRATCH scorecard** for the shortlist (`references/smile-scratch.md`);
- **a recommendation:** three to five finalists, each with a one-line rationale and the acid-test sentence, plus the checks still to run.

For titles of books, chapters, series, articles and posts, use `title-craft`. It borrows this skill's tests.

**Important:** never quote or reproduce text from the source books. Paraphrase the ideas, use your own examples, and apply them to the person's naming job.

## The core ideas

- A name should earn a smile. If it leaves people puzzled, cut it, however clever it felt at the desk. [Watkins ch2]
- The names that move people are made from familiar words and ideas they already understand and like. A good name works like a good headline, by making an emotional connection; names built from word fragments or "linguistic engineering" make none. [Watkins, Preface]
- People like to feel smart, not lost. A name they get flatters them, so they repeat it. [Watkins ch1]
- Every time you have to spell, pronounce or explain a name, you are apologizing for it, and the apology costs the brand. Different is not the same as good: a distinctive name can still be a bad one. [Watkins ch2]
- A name can't say everything. The brief may run to pages, but the name is one to three words, so let it suggest one thing well. [Watkins ch4]
- Generate far more names than you need: dozens at least, often over a hundred, because screening will kill many. Never get attached to a single candidate. [Watkins ch5]
- Judge by "Is it right?", not "Do I like it?", and don't hand the decision to polls or focus groups. [Watkins ch6]
- Name for one specific person. Know the words they use and what is on their mind; a narrow, well-understood audience beats a broad vague one. [Edwards S8]
- The world is densely named. Learn the real names that people in the trade or world use before inventing your own. [Klinkenborg]
- A name positions its audience before they meet the thing. It tells them what kind of experience to expect, and a mismatch disappoints even people who would have loved the real thing. [McKee ch4]
- Don't dress up the vocabulary. The plain word that fits beats its fancy cousin. [King, Toolbox]

## How to use this in practice

1. **Write the brief** with everyone who will decide, and get agreement on it before anyone names anything. → `references/naming-brief.md` [Watkins ch4]
2. **List at least twelve word sparks** from the brief: the experiences, the personality, the world around the thing. They are fuel, not names. [Watkins ch5]
3. **Mine each spark** with every tool, one spark at a time: thesaurus, image search, glossaries, dictionaries and idioms, clichés, searches around the topic, and film, book and song titles. → `references/brainstorm.md` [Watkins ch5]
4. **Write everything down** in three columns (Spot On, Maybe and Sparks), so half-ideas can grow into names. [Watkins ch5]
5. **Score the shortlist** with SMILE and SCRATCH, and cut anything that commits a SCRATCH sin. → `references/smile-scratch.md` [Watkins, Intro]
6. **Test for legs.** For each finalist, sketch three extensions: a tagline, a product or package name, and a sign or sign-off. [Watkins ch1]
7. **Review by the rules:** independently first, on paper, imagined in context, with no polls. → `references/review-and-clear.md` [Watkins ch6]
8. **Screen the top five:** search the web and the slang dictionary, run the voice test, search the trademark databases, then go to an attorney. Look at domains last. → `references/review-and-clear.md` [Watkins, Resources]
9. **Present the finalists** with a sentence of rationale each and the acid-test sentence at the top of the list. [Watkins ch6]

If the name lives inside a story (a character, place, ship, faction or spell), run the same steps with the story's world as the brief, and use `references/story-names.md`.

## SMILE: five qualities of a sticky name

| Quality | Meaning | Quick check |
|---|---|---|
| Suggestive | evokes something about the thing, by metaphor rather than description | Does it hint at the essence without spelling it out? |
| Meaningful | resonates with the audience, not just the founder | Would a stranger get it without you there to explain? |
| Imagery | paints a picture, which aids memory | Can you see it? |
| Legs | carries a theme that can stretch a long way | Can you spin ten extensions from it? |
| Emotional | moves people | Does it make someone feel something, ideally smile? |

- Aim for all five. A name that suggests, means, pictures, extends and moves people sells itself before anyone has used the product. [Watkins ch1]
- A meaningful long name beats a short meaningless one, and the imagery must be in the name itself, not borrowed from decades of advertising. A founders'-surname bank only pictures the Wild West after a century of ads. [Watkins ch1]

## SCRATCH: seven deadly sins

| Sin | What goes wrong |
|---|---|
| Spelling-challenged | spelled differently from how it sounds; reads like a typing error |
| Copycat | close to a competitor or a tired trend |
| Restrictive | locks you into one product, place, price or promise |
| Annoying | forced, random, ungrammatical or try-hard |
| Tame | flat, descriptive, sounds like everyone else |
| Curse of knowledge | means something only to insiders |
| Hard to pronounce | not obvious how to say it, so people avoid saying it |

- One sin is enough to cut a name. These are deal breakers, not points to weigh against strengths. [Watkins ch2]
- Use orthodox spelling. A clever misspelling forces every customer to learn your exception. [S&W V.10]

## Families, versions and legs

- A name with legs carries a whole theme: job titles, package names, meeting rooms, sign-offs, merchandise slogans. A fire-themed PR firm can have a chief, a firehouse and packages named for ways of burning. Rich themes (space, nature, music, travel, art, food) stretch furthest, and families can share a prefix, a verb pattern, a play on the parent name, or just the brand's personality. [Watkins ch1]
- Leave room for the next product. A suffix family that fits two products may have no room for a third. [Watkins ch2]
- Choose a pattern you can keep, and keep it consistent, but not one so rigid it can't survive an extension or a change of direction. [Scheuerer ch27]
- Use the same words everywhere the name appears. Repetition across every touchpoint builds familiarity. [Edwards, Q&A]

## Where the books disagree

- **Puns and wordplay.** Watkins builds names from puns and clichés, and her firm's own name is one. McPhee wrote a thousand pun titles early on, then gave puns up because wordplay comes too cheaply. Resolution used here: wordplay must carry meaning (the pun says something true about the thing), pass SCRATCH, and fit the tone. Brands and products can use more; fiction and serious nonfiction ration it.
- **Test or trust?** Watkins says never poll outsiders or run focus groups on names. Edwards split-tests everything. Resolution: a name is a permanent identity, judged against the brief by the people who own it. Use an outsider only to check understanding ("What do you think this is?"), never preference. Save split tests for headlines, subject lines and subtitles.
- **Describe or suggest?** Watkins calls descriptive names tame. Edwards' headlines state the promise outright, and McKee wants a title to name something solid. Resolution: brand and product names suggest, the tagline or subtitle describes, and fiction titles name something solid in the story (see `title-craft`).
- **Long or short?** Watkins says a meaningful long name beats a short meaningless one. King shortens a galumphing character name so it reads aloud better. Resolution: length is fine while every word earns meaning; cut syllables that carry none.
- **Insider words.** Watkins warns against the curse of knowledge, while Klinkenborg and Watkins's own glossary method mine a trade's real vocabulary. Resolution: use a trade word only when it also works for someone who has never heard it, the way a snowboarding term for a shaky board also suggests chattering teeth.
