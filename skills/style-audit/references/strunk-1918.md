# Strunk's 1918 Original

The spine of `style-audit` is the later, illustrated edition of *The Elements of Style* (Strunk & White). This file checks that edition against William Strunk Jr.'s own 1918 classroom text, the root of the book. It adds what the original says that later editions merged, trimmed or dropped. Its tags carry the year: S&W 1918, then the rule or chapter. The audit keeps citing R1–R22 (the later numbering); use the map below to translate.

## What the original is for

- The book covers only the rules writers break most often. Three comma rules and one semicolon rule handle the internal punctuation of nearly every sentence. Master the four punctuation rules (R3–R6) until applying them takes no thought. [S&W 1918 Intro]
- Once past the essentials, a writer improves fastest by working on the problems in their own drafts. That is why this audit reports on the person's text instead of lecturing on rules. [S&W 1918 Intro]
- Strong writers sometimes break a rule, and when they do the sentence repays the reader with some gain that makes up for it. Until you can be sure of doing as well, follow the rule. Once plain English comes easily, go to the masters for the rest of style. [S&W 1918 Intro]

## Punctuation the original spells out

**Paired commas (1918 R3):** [S&W 1918 R3]
- When an aside follows "and" or "but," the first comma goes before the conjunction: *She stood, and, though nobody had asked, began to sing.*
- To tell a nonrestrictive clause from a restrictive one, try splitting the sentence in two. If the clause only adds a fact, the sentence splits into two independent statements, so use commas. If the clause tells you which one, it can't be split, so no commas: *The lighthouse, which was built in 1871, still works* versus *The lighthouse that still works is the northern one.*
- Set off the year in a full date (*May 4, 1990, was the day*) and a date that follows a weekday.
- Set off introductory phrases and clauses that come before or after the main clause.

**Comma before a conjunction (1918 R4):** [S&W 1918 R4]
- "And" only says that two clauses are related, not how. When the relation is cause, concession or time, say so with *as*, *although* or *when*, or with a phrase: *As the road had flooded, we turned back.*
- Don't overcorrect. A page made entirely of tight, subordinated sentences turns stiff, and an occasional loose sentence gives the reader relief.
- "So" as a joining word is colloquial on the page. Recast with *as* or *since*.
- When one subject serves two verbs, use a comma before "but" and none before "and" if the link is close: *He read the contract but refused to sign*; *She trained for a year and won.*

**The semicolon (1918 R5):** [S&W 1918 R5]
- A semicolon between two complete clauses shows a close relation, usually cause or consequence. It is briefer than a conjunction and tighter than a full stop, which makes it one of the writer's best tools for showing how statements connect.
- A conjunctive adverb (*therefore*, *accordingly*, *besides*, *then*, *thus*) between clauses still needs the semicolon.
- Very short clauses of the same shape may take commas (*I came, I saw, I left*). The chatty comma splice belongs only in dialogue or a familiar letter.

**Fragments (1918 R6):** [S&W 1918 R6]
- An emphatic word or phrase may stand as its own sentence, as long as the emphasis is earned and nobody could mistake it for a blunder.

**Openers (1918 R7):** [S&W 1918 R7]
- The rule covers more than participles. A phrase that starts with a preposition, an appositive, or an adjective at the head of the sentence must also belong to the subject.
  - *A nervous flyer, the turbulence terrified her* → *A nervous flyer, she was terrified by the turbulence.*
- Violations are often unintentionally funny, which is a good way to spot them.

## The paragraph (1918 R8–R9)

**Subdividing (1918 R8):** [S&W 1918 R8]
- A short subject (one incident, one idea, a brief description) usually wants a single paragraph. After drafting it, check whether splitting would help.
- Plan a longer piece as a list of topics, one paragraph or group of paragraphs each. A story critique might run premise, structure, characters, prose.
- A single sentence shouldn't stand as a paragraph except as a transition, or in guidebook-style writing that covers many small topics.

**The expository paragraph (1918 R9):** [S&W 1918 R9]
- Put the topic sentence first, or close to first.
- The body explains it, proves it, or develops it.
- The last sentence drives the topic home or states a consequence.
- Never end on a digression or a minor detail.
- Tie the paragraph to the whole with a word or phrase in its topic sentence (*again*, *for the same reason*). If the transition needs more than one sentence, give it its own paragraph.
- Ways to develop a topic sentence:
  - restate it;
  - define its terms;
  - deny its opposite;
  - give instances;
  - prove it;
  - trace what follows from it.

**Narrative and description (1918 R9):** [S&W 1918 R9]
- A paragraph may open with a brief summary statement that holds the details together. Used too often, though, this becomes a mannerism.
- More often the opening's subject simply names what the paragraph is about.
- Short paragraphs of fast narrative need no topic sentence at all. The break works as a pause that throws one detail into relief.

## Sentence rules: the finer points

**Voice (1918 R10):** [S&W 1918 R10]
- Choose the voice by deciding which word should be the subject, usually the paragraph's topic.
- Don't stack one passive on another (*He was reported to have been seen* → *Witnesses reported seeing him*).
- Don't build a passive whose subject is a noun naming the whole action: *An inspection of the hull was carried out* → *The hull was inspected.*

**Negatives (1918 R11):** [S&W 1918 R11]
- Use "not" to deny or to set up a contrast, never to avoid committing to a claim.
- Negative words other than "not" (*never*, *nothing*, *nobody*) are usually strong.

**Concrete language (1918 R12):**
- In exposition and argument, keep hold of the concrete. Every general principle needs at least one particular instance. [S&W 1918 R12]
- An abstract word makes the reader translate it into a picture; a specific word hands the picture over directly and saves the reader the effort. [S&W 1918 R12]

**Needless words (1918 R13):** [S&W 1918 R13]
- "Who is" and "which was" are often padding: *her sister, who is a pilot* → *her sister, a pilot*.

**Parallel form (1918 R15):** [S&W 1918 R15]
- Don't vary the form of parallel items for variety's sake. Varying the wording is right only when you restate one point to emphasize it.
- If you have too many like items to keep parallel, group them, or put them in a table.

**Related words (1918 R16):** [S&W 1918 R16]
- A phrase between subject and verb is acceptable when it is a short relative clause or an appositive. It is also acceptable when a periodic sentence delays the verb on purpose, for suspense.
- When the antecedent is a whole phrase, put the relative pronoun after the entire phrase, unless that creates an ambiguity.
- Watch "all … not." *All the guests did not arrive* says none came; *Not all the guests arrived* says some didn't.

**Summaries (1918 R17):** [S&W 1918 R17]
- Present tense for the action of a play, and preferably for a story too. Put earlier action in the perfect (*has fled*), and leave reported speech in the past.
- Signal once that what follows is summary. Don't keep inserting "he said" or "the author adds."
- In criticism, don't slide into retelling. Write a discussion supported by evidence. When the subject spans several works, build toward general conclusions instead of marching through the works one at a time.

**Emphasis (1918 R18):** [S&W 1918 R18]
- To make the subject itself emphatic, move it into the predicate's position at the end: *Out of the fog came the ferry.*

## Matters of form (1918 IV)

A checklist for manuscripts and reports. [S&W 1918 IV]
- **Numerals:** write dates and serial numbers in figures (*Chapter 9*, *the 14th Battalion*).
- **Parentheses:**
  - Punctuate the sentence outside the parentheses exactly as if they weren't there.
  - Inside, punctuate the parenthetical as if it stood alone, but drop its final full stop. Keep a question mark or exclamation mark.
  - A wholly separate sentence in parentheses keeps its full stop inside.
- **Quotations:**
  - A formal quotation offered as evidence is introduced by a colon and set in quotation marks.
  - A quotation that serves as an object or appositive takes a comma.
  - Full lines of verse start on a new line and need no marks.
  - A quotation after "that" is indirect and takes no marks.
  - Proverbs, familiar literary phrases, colloquialisms and slang need no quotation marks.
- **References:**
  - In scholarly work, keep references out of the running text; use parentheses or footnotes.
  - Abbreviate titles you cite often and list the full forms at the end.
  - Write act, scene and page numbers compactly.
- **Titles:**
  - Italicize the titles of works.
  - Drop a leading *A* or *The* when a possessive comes before the title: *Hardy's Return of the Native*.
- **Line-end hyphens:**
  - Divide a word by how it is built.
  - Never strand a single letter.

## Spelling (1918 VI)

- Spelling rests on general agreement. A nonstandard or simplified spelling makes readers stumble and spend attention on the form instead of the sense, so the writer defeats their own purpose. Use the standard spelling. [S&W 1918 VI]
- Keep a list of the words you personally misspell (*separate*, *definitely*, *accommodate*, *occurred*, *privilege*, whatever yours are), and search every draft for them before it goes out. [S&W 1918 VI]

## Drill mode (1918 VII)

- The original closes with exercises:
  - punctuating an unpunctuated passage;
  - explaining how two versions differ in meaning;
  - correcting faulty sentences.
- When a writer keeps making the same fault, build a short drill of that kind from their own sentences, and show the corrected answers after. [S&W 1918 VII]
