# Rules 1–22

Each rule is paraphrased, followed by what to flag and an original example. Cite rules in the audit report by number: R1–R22.

## Part I — Usage (R1–R11)

- **R1. Possessive singular.**
  - Rule: add 's to a singular noun, whatever its final letter: the boss's desk, Charles's friend.
  - Exceptions: a few ancient names ending in -es or -is. Pronoun possessives (its, hers, theirs, yours, ours) never take an apostrophe.
  - Flag: "it's" used for "its," and the reverse. "It's" always means "it is." [S&W R1]
- **R2. The serial comma.**
  - Rule: in a series of three or more with one conjunction, put a comma after every term but the last: *rust, salt, and time*. [S&W R2]
- **R3. Parenthetic expressions.**
  - Rule: enclose them in commas, both commas or none. Commas also set off nonrestrictive clauses, the kind that add a fact rather than identify which one.
  - Flag: a missing closing comma: *The pilot, who had flown the route twice had no charts.* [S&W R3]
- **R4. Comma before a joining conjunction.**
  - Rule: put a comma before and, but, or, nor, for, so or yet when it introduces an independent clause: *The gate opened, but no one came through.* [S&W R4]
- **R5. No comma splices.**
  - Rule: don't join two independent clauses with only a comma. Use a semicolon, a period, or a conjunction.
  - Flag: *The storm passed, the streets were empty.* → *The storm passed; the streets were empty.* [S&W R5]
- **R6. Don't break sentences in two.**
  - Rule: don't put a period where a comma belongs, leaving a fragment by accident.
  - Flag: *She reached the tower at dusk. Having walked all day.* A deliberate fragment in fiction is a separate question; see SKILL.md. [S&W R6]
- **R7. The colon.**
  - Rule: use it after an independent clause to introduce a list, an appositive, an amplification or an illustrative quotation.
  - Flag: a colon wedged between a verb and its object: *The crew needed: water and fuel.* [S&W R7]
- **R8. The dash.**
  - Rule: use a dash for an abrupt break or interruption, or to announce a long appositive or summary. It's stronger than a comma, less formal than a colon, and more relaxed than parentheses.
  - Use it only when the simpler marks won't do. [S&W R8]
- **R9. Agreement.**
  - Rule: the number of the subject decides the number of the verb, whatever words come between them.
  - Flag: *The list of names were long.*
  - "Each," "either," "everyone" and "nobody" take a singular verb. In "one of those who…," the verb goes with "those," so it's plural. [S&W R9]
- **R10. Pronoun case.**
  - Rule: use the right case for the pronoun's job: *between you and me*; *taller than I* (am); *whom* when it's the object. [S&W R10]
- **R11. Dangling participles.**
  - Rule: a participial phrase at the start of a sentence must refer to the grammatical subject.
  - Flag: *Rounding the corner, the tower came into view.* The tower did not round the corner. [S&W R11]

## Part II — Composition (R12–R22)

- **R12. Choose a design and hold to it.**
  - Rule: decide the shape of the piece in advance and follow it. Even loose forms have a skeleton.
  - Flag: pieces that change purpose partway through. [S&W R12]
- **R13. The paragraph is the unit of composition.**
  - Rule: one topic per paragraph, and a new paragraph marks a new step.
  - In dialogue, each speech, even a single word, gets its own paragraph.
  - Short paragraphs suit animated narrative, but don't chop everything into one-liners. [S&W R13]
- **R14. Use the active voice.**
  - Rule: it's more direct and vigorous: *The crew sealed the hatch*, not *The hatch was sealed by the crew*.
  - Replace "there were … to be heard" constructions with a real verb.
  - The passive is right when the receiver of the action is the true subject. [S&W R14]
- **R15. Put statements in positive form.**
  - Rule: say what is, not what isn't: *forgot* for *did not remember*; *ignored* for *did not pay attention to*.
  - A contrast of negative and positive can be strong: *not mercy, but justice*.
  - Save "might," "could" and "may" for real uncertainty. [S&W R15]
- **R16. Use definite, specific, concrete language.**
  - Rule: *It snowed for nine days*, not *A period of inclement weather ensued*.
  - Give the significant details, not all of them. [S&W R16]
- **R17. Omit needless words.**
  - Flag padding:
    - "the question as to whether" → "whether"
    - "there is no doubt but that" → "no doubt"
    - "he is a man who" → "he"
    - "in a hasty manner" → "hastily"
    - "the reason why is that" → "because"
  - Always hunt "the fact that."
  - Watch for a single complex idea spread thin across several sentences that could be one. [S&W R17]
- **R18. Avoid a run of loose sentences.**
  - Flag: several sentences in a row made of two clauses joined by "and," "but," "which" or "while." The monotony dulls the reader.
  - Recast some as simple, periodic or three-part sentences. [S&W R18]
- **R19. Express parallel ideas in parallel form.**
  - Rule: matching ideas take matching grammar.
  - Articles and prepositions go before the first term only, or before every term.
  - Correlatives (both/and, not/but, either/or) are followed by the same construction. [S&W R19]
- **R20. Keep related words together.**
  - Rule: word order shows what relates to what.
  - Flag: modifiers away from what they modify (*She only found two errors* → *She found only two errors*), and relative pronouns far from their nouns.
  - Don't split subject and verb with a long phrase that could move. [S&W R20]
- **R21. In summaries, keep to one tense.**
  - Rule: a summary of a plot or an argument stays in one tense, usually present. Drifting between tenses looks undecided. [S&W R21]
- **R22. Put the emphatic words at the end.**
  - Rule: the end of a sentence is its position of stress. Put the new or important element there.
  - The start of a sentence is the other strong spot, for any element that isn't the subject.
  - The same principle holds for sentences in a paragraph and paragraphs in a piece. [S&W R22]

## A narrative note from the misused-words chapter

- **Habit to incident.** When narration moves from what someone habitually did ("would get up early…") to what happened on one particular day, signal the change clearly, e.g. "One morning in March…". Otherwise the reader can't tell habit from incident. [S&W IV]
