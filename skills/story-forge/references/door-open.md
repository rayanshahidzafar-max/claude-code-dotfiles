# Door Open: Reread, Second Draft, Feedback

Use this after the first draft exists. Here the story stops being only yours: the work now is to make it right for the reader.

## The reread (station 6)

- Come back to the draft after enough distance to see it fresh. King's rule is at least six weeks in a drawer. It should read as though someone else wrote it. [King, On Writing]
- Read it in one sitting if you can, with a notepad. Don't fix anything on this pass except obvious typos. [King, On Writing]
- **Housekeeping to note:**
  - Misspellings, names that drift, inconsistent timelines.
  - Truck-sized holes in the plot.
  - Motivation that doesn't hold: the "she would *never* do that" moments. [King, On Writing]
- **The big questions to answer in the notes:**
  - Is the story coherent?
  - If it is, what would make it sing?
  - What keeps recurring?
  - Do those recurring elements add up to a theme? [King, On Writing]
- Aim for resonance, not a moral. Theme should make the story echo in the reader's mind, not deliver a lesson. [King, On Writing]
- Chart who knows what, when, for the reader and each major character. Contradictions show up here that a straight reread misses. [Cron ch12]

## The second draft (station 7)

- The formula: the second draft equals the first draft minus ten percent. Every story has slack in it. Failing to find a tenth to cut, while keeping the story and its flavor, usually means not looking hard enough. [King, On Writing]
- Take out everything that isn't this story: side trips, scenes that serve some other book, explanations the reader didn't need. [King, C.V.]
- Bring out the theme you found. Add a scene or detail that sharpens it; cut ones that point elsewhere. [King, On Writing]
- Polish recurring images so they carry weight without announcing themselves as symbols. Symbolism should decorate and focus the story, not manufacture depth it hasn't earned. [King, On Writing]
- Work in passes, three or four times over. Each pass looks for different things. Run `revision-passes` for the full sequence. [McPhee, Draft No. 4]
- When cutting, take whole lines and passages from the middle, not the endings that land them, so nobody notices anything is gone, and never cut in a way that damages the tone. [McPhee, Omission]
- A draft on the page resists real change. Reread every sentence as still open to revision, as though you'd stopped everywhere at once. [Klinkenborg]
- When a sentence won't come right, don't just shuffle the words it already has. Imagine it again from scratch. [Klinkenborg]
- Keep the earlier version beside the new one while you experiment, so you can compare and fall back. [S&W V.5]

## The kinds of edits King made on a real story

When line-editing, look for each of these. They come from King's own marked-up draft of the opening of "1408." [King, And Furthermore]

1. Replace the placeholder title with the real one.
2. Shorten names that clog the line or read badly aloud.
3. Stop doing the reader's thinking. Cut the sentence that explains what the scene already showed.
4. Cut stage directions, belaboring of the obvious, and clumsy backstory.
5. Plant important objects early. If it matters in the third act, it must appear in the first; otherwise it arrives from nowhere.
6. Cut the obvious, like a character sitting down in a chair, and the trite.
7. Cut to the bone. Stamp out adverbs.
8. Keep a rule-breaker only when it's doing real work. An adverb that shows a character mocking another can stay.
9. Remove repetition. Add the one detail that clarifies a character.

## Feedback (station 10)

- Every story is a letter to one person, the Ideal Reader. Give them the draft first, then four to eight trusted readers. [King, On Writing]
- Keep the Ideal Reader in the room even while drafting. Imagine where they would laugh, and where they'd get bored and set it down. [King, On Writing]
- Ask for specifics. "It didn't work for me" is weather, not criticism. [King, On Writing]
- Friends and family praise the writer, not the pages. Choose honest, capable readers. [Cron ch12]
- Once the story is right, it belongs to anyone who wants to read or criticize it. That's the point of opening the door. [King, C.V.]
