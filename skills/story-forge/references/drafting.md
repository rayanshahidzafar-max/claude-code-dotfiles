# Drafting With the Door Closed

Use this while writing the first draft. It covers the craft of putting a story on the page: narration, description, dialogue and character. Keep moving. Anything here that tempts you to stop and fiddle belongs in the second draft instead.

## What writing is

- Writing is telepathy across time and space. You send a picture; the reader receives it. [King, What Writing Is]
  - Precise instructions don't make the picture land. The one specific, telling detail does, the thing everyone sees the same way.
  - The reader supplies the rest.
- Don't come lightly to the blank page. Take the work seriously even when the story is playful. [King, What Writing Is]

## The three parts of a story

- Every story is narration (moving it from A to Z), description (making the reader see and feel) and dialogue (bringing characters to life through speech). Plot is not on the list. [King, On Writing]
- Start with a situation and characters, and narrate what they do. Characters can be sketchy at first; they fill in as you watch them try to get free. [King, On Writing]
- If you're the story's first reader and you can't guess the ending, the reader won't either. [King, On Writing]
- A drill for drafting without a plot: take a familiar situation and invert the roles, then narrate what happens without planning it. [King, On Writing]

## Description

- Description starts with seeing it clearly in your own head and ends with translating that into words. The reader completes it: give them the seed and let their imagination grow the rest. [King, On Writing]
- Too little description leaves the reader squinting. Too much buries them. Pick a few details, usually the first ones that come to mind, and let them stand for the whole. [King, On Writing]
- Skip the inventory of a character's looks and clothes. Place and texture usually matter more than faces. Don't use appearance as a shortcut to character, such as eyes that "look intelligent." [King, On Writing]
- Boredom sets in when the writer falls in love with describing and forgets to keep the story moving. See it, set it down, move on. [King, On Writing]
- A good simile is a pleasure of recognition. A far-fetched or stale one is worse than none. [King, On Writing]
- Use the names of things. A carpenter hammers a spike, not a big nail. Vividness comes from exact nouns and verbs, not from adjectives. [McKee ch18]

## Dialogue

- Dialogue shows who people are, often without them knowing it: smart or dim, honest or evasive. Never tell the reader something about a character that their talk can show. [King, On Writing]
- The test is honesty. Each character speaks the way that person would, including the words you'd rather they didn't use. Good dialogue feels like overhearing. [King, On Writing]
- Use "said" for attribution, and don't hang adverbs on it. Don't swap in showy verbs for "said" either. If the line needs an adverb to explain how it was said, fix the line. [King, Toolbox]
- To write dialogue well, talk to people yourself, then shut up and listen to how they talk. [King, On Writing]
- Dialogue is not real conversation. It's compressed, and every exchange moves the scene toward its turn, while still sounding like talk. [McKee ch18]

## Character

- Watch how real people behave and tell the truth about what you see. Every character is part you, part observation, part invention. [King, On Writing]
- Nobody is the villain of their own life. Write every antagonist as someone who believes they're right. Give villains moments of doubt and heroes moments of wanting to turn away. [King, On Writing]
- Show mood and nature through behavior. If you have to tell the reader what a character is like, you've lost. [King, On Writing]
- If a character is dangerous, let the reader see it early, in one act that shows it. [King, On Writing]
- True character comes out in the choices made under pressure. The harder the choice, the deeper the reveal. [McKee ch5]

## Backstory and research

- Everyone has a history, and most of it isn't interesting. Include only the parts that affect this story. [King, On Writing]
- Bring backstory in early, but gracefully: through gesture and consequence, not announcement. [King, On Writing]
- Research belongs in the background. If you don't know a fact, invent it now and check it in revision. Never let research stop the draft. [King, On Writing]
- Bring in what you know from your own work and life. It gives even strange settings the texture of truth. [King, On Writing]

## Toolbox basics while drafting

- Use the vocabulary you actually have. Don't dress it up. The first word that comes to mind is usually right, if it fits and has some color. [King, Toolbox]
- A noun and a verb make a sentence. When a sentence gets tangled, fall back on simple ones. [King, Toolbox]
- Prefer the active voice. The passive puts the doer offstage and usually reads as timid. [King, Toolbox]
- Paragraphs show intent. Short ones speed things up and let the eye breathe. Don't plan paragraph breaks in the first draft; fix them later. [King, Toolbox]

## Craft from the supporting books

- **Openings.** The first sentence is an audition. Try several and keep the one with the most promise for the sentences after it. Begin the story itself; don't introduce it or clear your throat. [Klinkenborg]
- **Openings.** The lead is a promise and a flashlight into the story. It must not promise anything the story won't deliver. [McPhee, Structure]
- **Physical action.** Readers take in action with their bodies as well as their minds. Write it so the reader can actually picture each move in order. [Klinkenborg]
- **Every beat.** The protagonist reacts to everything that happens, and the reader can see the cause of each reaction. [Cron ch3]
- **Show the inner logic.** "Show, don't tell" means dramatizing *why* a character feels what they feel, including their train of thought. Showing the tear isn't enough. [Cron ch3]
- **Scene rhythm.** Each scene runs action, then reaction, then decision, and the decision triggers the next scene. [Cron ch8]
- **Subtext.** Let characters talk about one thing while the scene is about another. People rarely say exactly what they mean. [McKee ch11]
- **Exposition.** A character reveals a fact only when they need it as a weapon in the scene's conflict. [McKee ch15]
- **Finding the move.** Ask what you would do in the character's place. Then ask what the opposite would be, and look for the gap between them. [McKee ch7]
- **Dialogue tags.** Put the attribution where the speaker would naturally pause, so the reader always knows who is talking. [S&W V.13]

## When you finish the draft

- Tell yourself you're done, then put it away. Don't show it to anyone yet, because opinions now will bend a story that isn't finished becoming itself. [King, On Writing]
- Note any holes you already know about at the top of the file, and don't fix them yet. [King, On Writing]
