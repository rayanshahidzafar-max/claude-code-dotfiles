# Story Forge

The whole road from a blank page to a finished story, as a sequence of stations. Every station leaves a file in the story folder (layout in `references/story-folder.md`), so the work can be picked up, audited, or re-run from any point. The output is a finished, revised story plus the paper trail that produced it. Never skip to the last station.

**Important:** never quote or reproduce text from the source books. Paraphrase the concepts and apply them to the person's own story and world.

## The one-line version

First, tell yourself the story with the door closed. Then, with the door open, take out everything that isn't the story. [King, C.V.]

## The stations

0. **Brief** (`00-brief.md`).
   - Form, target length, genre, and one Ideal Reader: a specific person the story is written for. [King, On Writing]
   - Pick the kind of story you love to read, not the one you think you should write. [King, On Writing]
1. **World** (`01-world.md`). Run `worldbuilding`, but only as deep as this story needs. Research and world facts belong in the background, like spice in a sauce. The story always comes first. [King, On Writing]
   - Open the world through one person in one concrete situation, and let the larger forces show through that person's problem. [Diamond, Prologue]
2. **Design** (`02-design.md`).
   - Start from a situation, often a what-if, put characters in it, and ask how they'll get free. [King, On Writing]
   - Then run `story-design` to fix only the load-bearing events: desire, inciting incident, crisis, climax. [McKee ch19]
   - Leave the route between those events open for the draft to discover. The story is a found thing, dug up with care. It isn't assembled. [King, On Writing]
3. **Reader check** (`03-reader-check.md`). Run `reader-brain` on the design: the first-page test and the one-sentence focus. Fix the design, not the prose. [Cron ch2]
4. **First draft, door closed** (`04-draft-1.md`).
   - Write it straight through, as fast as is comfortable, to stay ahead of doubt.
   - Don't stop to polish, research or decide what it means. The first draft is the wrong place to think about meaning. [King, On Writing]
   - Drafting craft (narration, description, dialogue, character) is in `references/drafting.md`.
5. **Rest** (the drawer). Put the draft away and work on something else, long enough to reread it as a stranger would. For Claude inside one session: finish the reader and design notes first, then reread the draft cold, in one pass, as a reader, before changing a word. [King, On Writing]
6. **Door-open reread** (`05-reread-notes.md`).
   - Read the whole draft in one sitting and take notes on a pad.
   - Look for holes, inconsistencies and unbelievable motivation.
   - Look for the recurring elements that point to the theme. [King, On Writing]
   - See `references/door-open.md`.
7. **Second draft** (`06-draft-2.md`, `07-revision-log.md`).
   - Rewrite to make the story's point clearer. Cut what belongs to some other story. [King, On Writing]
   - Hit the formula: the second draft equals the first minus ten percent. [King, On Writing]
   - Run `revision-passes` for the structural, lead, frame-of-reference, continuity and omission passes.
8. **Sentence pass.** Run `sentence-craft` on the second draft.
9. **Polish** (`08-final.md`). Run `style-audit` as the last filter, then write the final title. Placeholder titles get replaced here. [King, And Furthermore]
10. **Door open to readers.** Give the final draft to the Ideal Reader first, then to a few trusted readers. If one reader dislikes something, the tie goes to the writer. If everyone flags the same spot, fix it. [King, On Writing]

Two drafts and a polish is the default shape. [King, On Writing] A draft that's still broken after the second pass goes back to station 2, not forward to station 8.

## Standing orders while a project is open

- Read a lot and write a lot. There's no shortcut. Reading is the creative center of a writer's life. You can't sweep a reader away until someone has done it to you. [King, On Writing]
- Write every day while a project is live, to a quota. King's rule is about 1,000 words a day for a beginner, with a day off a week. Stall and the characters go stale. Aim to finish a first draft within a season. [King, On Writing]
- Work behind a door you're willing to shut, at the same time each day. The routine trains your mind to show up. [King, On Writing]
- Fear is behind most bad writing: fear of plain words, fear of being judged. Let it go and write in plain words. [King, Toolbox]
- Tell the truth. Honesty in how people act and talk makes up for many faults; lying is the one readers don't forgive. [King, On Writing]
- The story is the boss. When a character or scene wants to go somewhere the plan didn't, follow it if it's truer, then update the design. [King, On Writing]
- Leave out the parts readers skip. Pace is a matter of what's missing as much as speed. [King, On Writing]
- Symbolism and theme are found in the second draft and brought out there, never bolted on in the first. [King, On Writing]
- Run `reader-brain`'s checkpoints at the end of each writing day. Treat each draft as a little better than the last, not as "the one." [Cron ch12]

## When the draft stalls

- Stop forcing it. Go for a walk and let the problem sit. The answer often arrives from above the level of conscious thinking. [King, On Writing]
- Look for the knot. When the plot has tangled, a single drastic event that wipes the board can free it. King's example is the bomb in the closet in *The Stand*. [King, On Writing]
- Check whether the good guys have started to act like the bad guys, or whether the story has drifted off its situation. [King, On Writing]
- Write a letter to someone you trust about how stuck you are. Mention the story's facts along the way, then delete the complaining and keep the facts. [McPhee, Draft No. 4]
- Remember that the first draft takes most of the total time and most of the dread. Once any draft exists, your mind keeps working on it between sessions. [McPhee, Draft No. 4]
- Don't wait for special conditions. Needing the perfect desk, pen or coffee turns into a ban on writing whenever they're missing. [Klinkenborg]

## Where the books disagree

- **Fast draft or finished sentences?** King drafts fast with the door closed and fixes later. McPhee agrees: blurt out the first draft, then work it over three or four times. Klinkenborg says accept no provisional sentences, and revise at the point of composition. Resolution used here: draft fast at the level of scenes and events, so no stopping to research or restructure. But write each sentence as a real sentence, not a note to yourself. The polish comes later.
- **Plan or discover?** McKee writes a full step-outline and treatment before any prose, and dialogue last. King distrusts plotting and lets characters talk freely in the first draft. Resolution: station 2 fixes only the load-bearing events. Dialogue is drafted freely at station 4, and McKee's subtext test is applied in revision.
- **Where to begin?** King prefers A to Z and dislikes flashbacks. McKee and McPhee start late, near the action, and flash back. Resolution: start on the day the balance breaks. Use a flashback only as a scene that turns, delivered once the reader wants it.
