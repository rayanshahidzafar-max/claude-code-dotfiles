# Reader Report

Use this template whenever you give feedback on a draft or outline. Every finding quotes the text it's about. A note without a location is noise: vague critique can't be acted on. [King, On Writing]

## Template

```
READER REPORT — <title>, <draft number>, <date>

1. FIRST-PAGE TEST
   Whose story:            <answer or "unclear">
   What's happening:       <the event on page one>
   What's at stake:        <answer or "not yet visible">
   All is not as it seems: <the anomaly, or "missing">

2. FOCUS SENTENCE
   <protagonist> + <trait> + <inner issue> + <goal> + <what the plot throws at them>
   Verdict: holds / doesn't hold yet (why)

3. CHECKPOINT FINDINGS (worst first)
   #  | where (scene/para) | quote | checkpoint | what the reader experiences | fix
   1  | ...

4. WHO KNOWS WHAT, WHEN
   scene | what really happened | what <protagonist> believes | what <other> believes | what the reader believes
   ...
   Mismatches that confuse: ...
   Mismatches that could create suspense or irony but are wasted: ...

5. MYTH CHECK (list any that apply, with the passage)

6. WHERE THE IDEAL READER PUTS IT DOWN
   <page/scene> — <why>

7. TOP THREE FIXES (in order)
```

## The myth check

Test the draft against these misbeliefs. Each line is myth → reality.

- Beautiful writing wins → story wins, every time. A beautifully written story nobody cares about is still one nobody finishes. [Cron ch1]
- The plot is what it's about → the story is about how the plot affects the protagonist. [Cron ch1]
- Write what you know → write what you know emotionally. [Cron ch1]
- Outside troubles add drama → only if she must confront them to overcome her issue. [Cron ch2]
- A full biography helps → only the backstory this story needs. [Cron ch5]
- Sensory detail brings it to life → only when it carries information the story needs. [Cron ch6]
- A withheld reveal hooks the reader → it usually robs the story of what hooks. [Cron ch7]
- "Show, don't tell" means show the tears → it means show *why* she's sad: the events and the inner logic. [Cron ch3]
- Literary fiction doesn't need plot → it has as much plot, if not more. [Cron ch8]
- Real writers break the rules → successful writers follow them. [Cron ch12]

## Questions for outside readers

Hand these to a reader at the end of each scene or chapter. Their answers show where your intent and their experience part ways. [Cron ch12]

1. What do you think will happen next?
2. Who seems important?
3. What do they want?
4. What leaped out as a setup?
5. What information seemed important?
6. What were you dying to know?
7. What confused you?

## Using outside readers well

- Friends and family are unreliable judges. Their pleasure in knowing you gets mistaken for pleasure in the pages. Choose capable, honest readers. [Cron ch12]
- Show the draft first to your Ideal Reader, the one person you picture while writing, then to a small circle of trusted readers. When their opinions split, the tie goes to the writer. When most of them flag the same spot, there's a real problem there. [King, On Writing]
- Watch the moment a reader puts the pages down. It's the most honest pacing data you will get. [King, On Writing]
- Test any ambiguous sentence against the literal reader described in SKILL.md, the one who takes every ambiguity the wrong way.
