# The Twelve Checkpoints

Run these on an outline before drafting, at the end of each writing day, and at the end of each scene or chapter. For every "no," quote the passage and write the fix.

## 1. Hook — does the reader want to know what happens next?
*Questions from* [Cron ch1]

- Is it clear whose story this is?
- Is something happening on page one: the event itself, not the warm-up?
- Is the conflict on page one tied to what the protagonist wants?
- Is something at stake, and does the reader know what?
- Is there a sense that all is not as it seems?
- Is there enough of the bigger picture that the reader can measure what's happening against it?

## 2. Point — what is this story about?
*Questions from* [Cron ch2]

- What do you want the reader to walk away believing about people?
- Is the theme reflected in how the world treats the protagonist?
- Does every twist force her to deal with her inner issue?
- Can you sum the story up in a short paragraph?
- Watch for the signs a story is off the rails.
  - No clear protagonist.
  - No goal.
  - A goal with no inner issue behind it.
  - A protagonist who gets what she wants by luck.
  - Events that don't touch her goal.
  - Reactions that aren't believable.

## 3. Emotion — does the reader feel it?
*Questions from* [Cron ch3]

- Does the protagonist react to everything that happens? Is the reaction one the reader understands instantly, with the cause visible?
- Do we know what she expects, so we can tell when it's met or thwarted?
- In first person, is everything filtered through the narrator's slant?
- Is the author's editorializing kept out?
- Is body language used only when it tells us something we don't already know?
- *Support:* Emotion comes from a change in value, and only when the reader empathizes, knows the desire and understands the stakes. Mood-setting can't stand in for that change. [McKee ch10]
- *Support:* The reader feels the gap exactly when the character does: the moment the world answers differently than expected. [McKee ch7]

## 4. Goal — what does she want, and why?
*Questions from* [Cron ch4]

- What does she want most, and why?
- Is the external goal specific, not a vague bad situation she wants out of?
- What would getting it mean about her? Is that inner goal at odds with the outer one?
- Does pursuing the goal force her to face a long-standing problem or fear?

## 5. Inner issue — why now?
*Questions from* [Cron ch5]

- Why does the story start on this day? What clock started?
- Are her fears and desires rooted in specific past events?
- Do you know how the issue has thwarted her right up to page one?
- Is the backstory limited to what this story needs, and specific enough to picture?
- Do you know roughly where the story is going, so page one can plant the seeds?
- *Support:* Backstory goes in early but gracefully, as a gesture rather than a speech: a thumb rubbing the place a ring used to be, not a character announcing a divorce. [King, On Writing]
- *Support:* Make the reader want a fact ("why?") before you hand it over. [McKee ch15]

## 6. Specifics — can the reader see it?
*Questions from* [Cron ch6]

- Is every generic line turned into something specific?
- Are reasons, reactions and memories on the page, not hidden behind actions?
- Can every metaphor be grasped at reading speed?
- Does every sensory detail have a story reason? Description with no story purpose is sightseeing.
- *Support:* Description is completed by the reader: supply the seed, not the whole garden. A few well-chosen details beat an inventory. [King, On Writing]
- *Support:* Few details are essential alone, but together they add up to one impression. Choose them for how they set the scene, what they suggest underneath, and how they sound. [McPhee, Structure]
- *Support:* What the writer notices is a sign of what matters. Get the words close to the actual thing and its real name. [Klinkenborg]

## 7. Conflict — is change forced?
*Questions from* [Cron ch7]

- Are the seeds of conflict there from page one?
- Is the protagonist caught between a specific rock and hard place?
- Does conflict force her to act, either rationalizing or changing?
- Have you imagined what she'd most want to avoid, and made her face it?
- Does the story make sense without the big reveal, and more sense after it?

## 8. Cause and effect — does each scene follow?
*Questions from* [Cron ch8]

- Is each scene triggered by the one before and testing the last decision?
- Does everything revolve around the story question?
- Can you trace every decision, especially every change of mind?
- Does each scene run action, reaction, decision?
- Ask "And so?" of every scene. Where you can't answer, a digression lurks.

## 9. Trouble — are you being nice?
*Questions from* [Cron ch9]

- Does everything that can go wrong go wrong?
- Are her deepest secrets and flaws exposed?
- Does she earn what she gets and pay for what she loses?
- Do her efforts to fix things make them worse?
- Is the opposition personal, present, active, specific, and on a clock?
- *Support:* Does the opposition believe it is right? Nobody is the villain of their own life. [King, On Writing]

## 10. Setups and payoffs — can the reader see the road?
*Questions from* [Cron ch10]

- Are there no accidental setups: nothing that promises a payoff that never comes?
- Is there a visible trail of dots from each setup to its payoff?
- Do the dots let the reader anticipate?
- Is every payoff possible given what came before?
- *Support:* A setup should mean one thing when planted and something deeper when it pays off. Payoffs fail when the obvious is over-prepared and the unusual under-prepared. [McKee ch10]
- *Support:* Never let a coincidence pay off the ending. [McKee ch16]

## 11. Subplots, flashbacks, foreshadowing — do they illuminate?
*Questions from* [Cron ch11]

- Does each subplot supply information, make the quest harder, or deepen our understanding of the protagonist?
- Is the reason for leaving the main line clear on the page? Do we return seeing the story differently?
- Is every out-of-character act foreshadowed, so the reader says "aha," not "give me a break"?
- Does the reader never have to flip back to find something they missed?
- Do mirror subplots arc the opposite way, asking "is this what you really want?"
- Do all subplots merge into the main line by the end?

## 12. Rewriting — is this draft better than the last?
*Questions from* [Cron ch12]

- Is this draft aimed at being a little better than the last, not "the one"?
- Is the who-knows-what-when chart updated for this draft?
