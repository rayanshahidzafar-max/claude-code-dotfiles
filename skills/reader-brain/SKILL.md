# Reader Brain

A way of reading a story as the reader's brain reads it: hunting for what matters to one person's struggle, and dropping everything else. It should produce a **reader report** (`references/reader-report.md`): specific places where attention will drop, with a fix for each. Not a general verdict.

**Important:** never quote or reproduce text from the source books. Paraphrase the concepts and apply them to the person's own pages.

## The working definition

- A story is how what happens affects someone pursuing a hard goal, and how that person changes as a result. The plot is what happens; what the story is *about* is the change inside the protagonist. [Cron ch1]
- The reader doesn't come for the events. They come to find out what it's like to face those events, and they judge every line by that. [Cron ch1]
- Focus is three things working together. [Cron ch2]
  - The protagonist's inner issue.
  - The theme: what the story says about how people behave when things are beyond their control. It must be a specific claim, not a topic word like "love."
  - The plot, which exists to force that issue.
- The theme is never stated on the page. The world's treatment of the protagonist shows it. [Cron ch2]

## The twelve things the reader's brain demands

Condensed from the book's paired findings about cognition and story. [Cron ch1–12]

1. From the first sentence, the reader has to want to know what happens next. Something must be at stake on page one. [Cron ch1]
2. Everything on the page is on a need-to-know basis. Anything the reader can't connect to the protagonist's struggle is noise. [Cron ch2]
3. Emotion is how the reader decides what matters. If they aren't feeling, they stop reading. [Cron ch3]
4. The reader tracks agendas, so the protagonist needs a clear goal. Without one there is nothing to figure out. [Cron ch4]
5. The writer must know exactly when, and why, the protagonist's view of the world was knocked out of true. [Cron ch5]
6. The brain thinks in specific images. Anything abstract has to be made concrete in the protagonist's particular struggle. [Cron ch6]
7. People resist change, so change must come from conflict the protagonist can't avoid. [Cron ch7]
8. The brain links cause and effect. Every scene must follow from the last and set up the next. [Cron ch8]
9. Story is rehearsal for trouble, so put the protagonist through tests she doesn't think she can pass. [Cron ch9]
10. To the reader, everything is a setup, a payoff, or the road between them. [Cron ch10]
11. Flashbacks, foreshadowing and subplots must shed instant light on the main line, or they stall it. [Cron ch11]
12. Skill becomes instinct only after long, conscious rewriting. Writing is really rewriting. [Cron ch12]

## How to use this in practice

1. **Run the first-page test.** Whose story is it? What's happening, the actual event rather than the preamble? What's at stake, and does the reader know it? Is there a sense that all is not as it seems? [Cron ch1]
2. **Write the one-sentence focus.** Protagonist + defining trait + inner issue + goal + what the plot throws at them. If it won't fit in a sentence, the story isn't focused yet. That's a finding, not a formatting problem. [Cron ch2]
3. **Walk the checkpoints** in `references/checkpoints.md`. Quote the passage behind each finding. [Cron ch1–12]
4. **Chart who knows what, when.** Keep three timelines: what really happened, what each major character believes scene by scene, and what the reader believes scene by scene. Confusion and flat reveals show up as mismatches between them. [Cron ch12]
5. **Check the myths.** Test the draft against the common misbeliefs listed in the reference. Beautiful writing doesn't beat story. A sensory detail with no story reason clogs the page. [Cron ch1, ch6]
6. **Test the reader's grip, not just their curiosity.** Interest has two parts: curiosity (wanting the answer) and concern (caring how it turns out for someone). [McKee ch16] Readers look for the center of good in a story and attach to it, so make sure it's where you want it. [McKee ch16]
7. **Deliver the report** using the template. Rank fixes by what will lose the reader soonest.

## Pacing and timing

- Pacing is the length of time between moments of conflict. Unbroken conflict turns into the new normal and stops registering. After a peak, ease off so the reader can take it in; that's often where a subplot belongs. [Cron ch8]
- Backstory has a clock. Brought in too soon, it's a digression the reader has no reason to care about. Brought in too late, it's a groan. Deliver it at the moment the reader needs it to understand a choice. [Cron ch5]
- Readers owe the writer nothing. They're warm-hearted toward characters and ruthless toward pages that waste their time. [Cron ch1]
- Writing is communication, not self-expression. If the reader didn't get it, it isn't on the page yet. [Cron ch12]

## Supporting lenses

- Decide what the reader knows relative to the characters. [McKee ch16]
  - Mystery: the reader knows less.
  - Suspense: the reader knows the same.
  - Dramatic irony: the reader knows more.
- A withheld fact that only exists to fake a mystery is a cheat, and so is a jolt with no insight behind it. [McKee ch16]
- Write for one Ideal Reader. When that reader would put the pages down, a stretch is too slow or too thin. Leave out the parts readers skip. [King, On Writing]
- Every reader is two readers. [Klinkenborg]
  - A literal one who takes every ambiguity the wrong way.
  - A curious one who will follow anywhere if the prose is clear.
  - Write for both.
- Trust the reader. Don't over-explain, over-describe or over-signal. [Klinkenborg]
- A reader doesn't need to be grabbed. They need to feel the writer's interest in each sentence. Authority is granted by the reader, who can close the book at any time. [Klinkenborg]
- The opening is a promise. It lights the way into the story and must not promise what doesn't follow. [McPhee, Structure]

## Where the books disagree

- **Grab them or earn them?** Cron wants urgency and stakes from the first sentence. Klinkenborg says the reader doesn't need grabbing, only the writer's evident interest and clarity. Resolution used here: the first page must put something at stake. It earns attention through a specific, strange, clearly seen situation, never through a trick or a false alarm. McPhee's warning agrees: a blaring lead is a promise the story can't keep.
- **Know the theme first, or find it?** Cron wants the point known early, to filter material. King finds theme after the first draft. Resolution: hold a working focus sentence before drafting, and treat a mismatch after drafting as information. Either the story found a truer point (update the sentence), or it drifted (cut back to the point).
- **How much physical "showing"?** Cron argues that "show, don't tell" is widely misread. Showing a character crying tells us little; show *why* they're sad, including their inner logic. King and McKee push toward behavior on the surface. Resolution: dramatize the cause and the inner reasoning, and use a gesture only when it tells the reader something new.
