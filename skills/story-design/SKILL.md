# Story Design

A design tool for the events of a story: what happens, in what order, and why it has to happen that way. When applied, it should produce a **design sheet and a step-outline** (see `references/design-sheet.md`) or a concrete diagnosis with fixes, not a lecture on structure.

**Important:** never quote or reproduce text from the source books. Paraphrase every concept in your own words and build examples from the person's own story, not the books' film examples.

## The core model

- A story is a chain of events, and an event only counts when it changes the charge of a value in a character's life (alive/dead, loyal/betrayed, free/enslaved) and the change is won through conflict. Weather and luck don't count unless someone's will meets them. [McKee ch2]
- The units nest, and every unit must turn a value. A beat is one action and one reaction. A scene turns one value. A sequence of 2–5 scenes turns harder than any single one of them. An act ends on a major reversal. The story's climax is the one change that can't be undone. [McKee ch2]
- The scene test: note the value and its charge (+/−) at the top of the scene and at the bottom. If nothing changed, the scene is exposition in disguise. Cut it, or fold its information into a scene that does turn. [McKee ch2]
- The engine is the gap: a character acts expecting one reaction from the world, and the world answers differently or harder. Every gap forces a bigger, riskier next action. [McKee ch7]
- Nothing moves forward except through conflict. It can come from three levels: inner (mind, body, feelings), personal (intimates), and extra-personal (institutions, other people, environment, time). [McKee ch7, ch9]
- Structure and character are the same thing seen from two sides. Structure applies steadily rising pressure. Character is what the choices under that pressure reveal. The harder the choice, the deeper the reveal. [McKee ch5]

## Before designing: choosing what to write

- Write the kind of story you love to read, not the kind you think will impress or sell. [King, On Writing]
- Draw on long-held interests. Subjects you have cared about for years give the richest material. [McPhee, Progression]
- Bring your own knowledge of work and life into even the strangest setting: plumbers in space. Use it to enrich the story, not to lecture. [King, On Writing]
- Tell the truth about how people behave. Honesty makes up for many faults; lying is the one readers don't forgive. [King, On Writing]

## How to use this in practice

1. **Find the protagonist's desire before anything else.**
   - Look for a conscious object of desire and, ideally, an unconscious desire that contradicts it. [McKee ch7]
   - The protagonist must be willful, able to pursue the desire, have a real but slim chance, and be empathetic (the reader recognizes something human), not necessarily likable. [McKee ch7]
   - Name the inner issue or false belief the plot will attack. The external goal should force a confrontation with it. [Cron ch4]
2. **Run the risk test.** What is the worst thing that happens if the protagonist fails? If the answer is "life goes back to normal," the story isn't worth telling yet. Raise the stakes until failure costs something the character can't afford. [McKee ch7]
3. **Design the inciting incident.**
   - It must be one on-page event that radically upsets the balance of the protagonist's life, positively or negatively. [McKee ch8]
   - It must arouse the desire to restore that balance, launch a quest, raise the story's central question, and plant the promise of the obligatory scene: the confrontation the reader will now insist on. [McKee ch8]
   - One generator: ask what is the worst thing that could happen to this person, and how it could turn out to be the best. Or run it the other way. [McKee ch8]
4. **Build progressive complications.**
   - Each action must cost more will, capacity and risk than the one before. A story may never retreat to actions of lesser magnitude, and a sagging middle is almost always Act One–sized actions being recycled. [McKee ch9]
   - Invent many more scenes than you will use; you choose among them. [McKee ch9]
5. **Chart the value's declension.** Take the story's primary value and plot its negative forms: contrary, contradictory, then the negation of the negation, where the value turns against itself (justice → unfairness → injustice → tyranny). A story that never reaches that depth satisfies but won't be remembered. See `references/antagonism.md`. [McKee ch14]
6. **Design the crisis as a true dilemma.**
   - Good-versus-evil is no choice at all. Make it irreconcilable goods, or the lesser of two evils. [McKee ch10]
   - Put it at the moment the protagonist has tried every lesser action. Hold it still on the page instead of skimming past it. [McKee ch13]
7. **Design the climax, then work backward.**
   - The climax is an action that needs no explanation and changes things absolutely and irreversibly. Ideally it gives the reader what they wanted but not in the way they expected. [McKee ch13]
   - Every earlier scene must be justified by it, thematically or structurally, or it goes. [McKee ch13]
   - Close with a brief resolution so the reader can leave the story with their dignity. [McKee ch13]
8. **Name the controlling idea.**
   - One sentence: the value as charged at the climax, plus the main cause of that charge ("Loyalty survives because she chooses her crew over her record").
   - Find it by reading the climax backward. Don't impose it forward. [McKee ch6]
   - The story argues it by swinging between the idea and its counter-idea, and it must give the counter-idea full, honest strength. [McKee ch6]
9. **Write the step-outline before any prose.**
   - One or two sentences per scene, saying what happens and how it turns.
   - Write each scene's function on the back of the card (inciting incident, act climax, setup for X).
   - Then do the treatment, then the draft. Dialogue comes last, because early dialogue makes you fall in love with lines. [McKee ch19]
10. **Check the outline against the reader.** Can you state the whole story in one sentence (protagonist, trait, issue, goal, plot)? If not, redesign until you can. Then hand the outline to `reader-brain`. [Cron ch2]

## Design rules worth checking every time

- **Exposition is ammunition.**
  - Characters reveal facts only when they need them as weapons in the current conflict. Never have them tell each other what both already know. [McKee ch15]
  - Withhold everything except what the reader needs right now. Save secrets and painful backstory for the major turning points. [McKee ch15]
- **Setups and payoffs.** Plant a setup that means one thing at first and something deeper at the payoff. Logic is retroactive: design the payoff first, then go back and plant. [McKee ch10] Make the chain of dots visible enough that the reader can anticipate. [Cron ch10]
- **Diminishing returns.** The same emotional charge twice in a row loses force, and a third time can turn into laughter. Alternate positive and negative scenes. [McKee ch10]
- **Act rhythm.** The last two act climaxes should carry opposite charges. You can't set up an up-ending with another up-beat. [McKee ch9]
- **Subtext.** A scene that is literally about what it's about is dead. Give characters a surface action and let the real conflict run underneath. [McKee ch11]
- **Reader position.**
  - Choose deliberately what the reader knows compared with the characters.
    - Mystery: the reader knows less.
    - Suspense: the reader knows the same.
    - Dramatic irony: the reader knows more.
  - Most stories run on suspense with the other two mixed in. [McKee ch16]
- **Motivation.** Melodrama comes from events that are under-caused, not from big emotion. Build enough causes to carry the event. [McKee ch16]
- **Coincidence.** Bring it in early and let it gain meaning. Never let it turn an ending; a rescue from outside is a lie about how life works. [McKee ch16]
- **Dimension.** Dimension means consistent contradiction: inside the deep character, or between the surface and the depth. It doesn't come from a list of quirks. The protagonist must be the most dimensional person in the cast. [McKee ch17]
- **Cast.** Polarize the cast. If two characters would react to the same news the same way, merge them or cut one. [McKee ch8]
- **Genre.** Keep the genre's conventions and avoid its clichés. Cure a clichéd scene by listing 5–15 ways it could go and picking the truest one the reader hasn't seen. [McKee ch3, ch4]
- **Reveals.** Don't withhold the key fact just to spring a big reveal. The story must make complete sense without the reveal, and even more sense once it lands. [Cron ch7]
- **Subplots and flashbacks.** Each one must bear on the protagonist's pursuit, and give the reader a reason on the page for leaving the main line. Every subplot has to rejoin the main story in the end. [Cron ch11]
- **Foreshadow the surprising act.** If a character will do something out of character, plant why earlier, so the reader says "of course" instead of "come on." [Cron ch11]
- **Need to know.** Everything on the page must be something the reader needs in order to follow the protagonist's struggle. [Cron ch2]

## Sequencing tools

- **Juxtaposition.** Put two scenes side by side and let the white space between them say what neither says outright. [McPhee, Structure]
- **Flashback order.** Start at a later, stronger moment, flash back, then run forward past where you began. Use verb tense to keep the reader oriented. [McPhee, Structure]
- **Slow build.** Mention an important thing briefly, return to it later with more, then later still, until the reader is desperate for it. Only then deliver the full scene. [McPhee, Structure]

## Where the books disagree

- **Design first or discover?** McKee designs the whole event chain before drafting. King distrusts plot and prefers to put characters in a situation and watch them work free. Resolution used here: design the load-bearing events (desire, inciting incident, crisis, climax) and leave the path between them open during the draft. McPhee works the same way: he knows his ending before he starts but lets the structure come out of the material. When the draft discovers a better path, update the design sheet and re-check everything before it. Cron makes the same point: when the map changes, the earlier chapters must change too.
- **When is theme decided?** McKee names the controlling idea from the climax. Cron wants the point known early, to filter material. King says theme belongs to the second draft. Resolution: hold a provisional controlling idea while designing, and confirm or rewrite it after the draft's climax exists.
- **Where does it start?** McKee says start as close to the climax as possible. King prefers telling it from A to Z. Resolution: start at the inciting incident's day, not before, and bring in history only as ammunition.
