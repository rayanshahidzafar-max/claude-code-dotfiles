# Design Sheet and Step-Outline

Use this when designing a new story or rebuilding one. Fill the sheet top to bottom, then write the step-outline. Keep both in the story folder (e.g. `02-design.md`) and update them whenever the draft discovers something better.

## The design sheet

```
WORKING TITLE:   (a placeholder is fine until the real one arrives)
FORM & LENGTH:   (short story ≈ one act; novella ≈ two; novel/feature ≥ three)
PREMISE:         What if ...?
WORLD:           period · duration · location · level of conflict   (from worldbuilding)

PROTAGONIST
  Conscious desire (object):
  Unconscious desire (if any, and how it contradicts the first):
  Inner issue / false belief the plot will attack:
  Why the story starts today (what clock started):
  Capacity to pursue it / real but slim chance:
  Why a reader will recognize themselves in them:

VALUE AT STAKE:  positive / contrary / contradictory / negation of the negation
INCITING INCIDENT (on-page event):
STORY QUESTION the reader now carries:
OBLIGATORY SCENE it promises:
SPINE (the deep desire to restore balance):

FORCES OF ANTAGONISM (inner / personal / extra-personal):
PROGRESSIONS (each costlier than the last):
CRISIS DILEMMA (irreconcilable goods or lesser of two evils):
CLIMAX (action that needs no explanation) + KEY IMAGE:
RESOLUTION (brief):
CONTROLLING IDEA (value + cause):
COUNTER-IDEA (argued at full strength):
SUBPLOTS and the job each does:
```

Why each field is there:

- **Title.** A working title is a placeholder. A real title names something solid in the story: a person, a place, an object, a theme, or the genre. [McKee ch18]
- **Premise.** A premise is a what-if question, not a plot. Many strong stories start as a situation ("what if X happened to Y here?") that you then put characters into. [King, On Writing] Don't treat the premise as sacred. If the story turns elsewhere, follow it. [McKee ch6]
- **Setting.** Setting has four dimensions: period, duration, location, and level of conflict. Name all four, and keep the world small enough to know completely. [McKee ch3]
- **Desire.** The protagonist needs a conscious desire. The most memorable protagonists also carry an unconscious desire at odds with it. [McKee ch7]
- **Inner issue.** Trace it to specific past events and know how it has blocked the character right up to page one. Keep only the backstory this story needs. [Cron ch5]
- **The antagonist.** Nobody is the villain of their own life. Write the antagonist's desire as something that makes sense and feels right to them. [King, On Writing]
- **Acts.** Act One usually runs about a quarter of the length. The last act is the shortest and accelerates. [McKee ch9]
- **The high moment.** In a long piece, the high moment tends to land about three-fifths of the way through. Use that as a check, not a mold. [McPhee, Structure]
- **The ending.** Know where you will end before you begin. The lead and the ending shape everything between them. [McPhee, Structure]
- **Subplots.** Every subplot needs a job:
  - contradict the controlling idea (irony);
  - echo it (variation);
  - set up a late central plot; or
  - complicate the central plot, which is the most useful job.

  A subplot that just runs alongside splits the story. [McKee ch9]

## The step-outline

One entry per scene. Each entry says who wants what, what they expect, and what happens instead, and it names the value's charge at the start and end.

```
S01  [safety: + → −]  Mara returns to the depot expecting the night shift's
     handover log, but finds the log wiped and her mentor's badge in the scrap bin.
     FUNCTION: inciting incident.
S02  [trust: + → −]  ...
     FUNCTION: first complication; plants the tracer bead (payoff S14).
```

Rules for the outline:

- Invent five to twenty times more material than you will use. Choosing among many options is where creativity happens. [McKee ch3]
- Each entry is one or two sentences of event, never dialogue. Add a FUNCTION line for each scene: its job in the design. [McKee ch19]
- When a scene's charge is the same at both ends, mark it CUT or MERGE. [McKee ch2]
- Once the outline holds, pitch the story aloud in a few minutes. Hunt for the places where the listener's attention drops, and redesign those beats before drafting. [McKee ch19]
- Title each sequence by what it achieves (e.g. "NO ALLY → ALLY"). A sequence that achieves nothing can't be titled. [McKee ch2]
- When a draft finds a better route, rewrite the outline from that point, then re-check every earlier setup that the new route depends on. [Cron ch12]
- Structure should not be imposed on the material. It should come out of it and stay as invisible as bones. If the outline reads like a formula, look again at what the material wants. [McPhee, Structure]
