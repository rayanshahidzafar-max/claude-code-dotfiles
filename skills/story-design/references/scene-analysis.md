# Scene Analysis

Use this to check whether a single scene works, or to rebuild one that doesn't.

## The five-step analysis

1. **Define the conflict.** Who drives the scene, and what do they want in it? Write it as an infinitive ("to get the codes"). What does the opposition want ("to stall until dawn")? The two desires must collide head-on, not glance off each other. [McKee ch11]
2. **Note the opening value and its charge.** Freedom (−), trust (+), and so on. [McKee ch11]
3. **Break the scene into beats.**
   - A beat is one action and one reaction. Name each with an active verb in the -ing form that describes the *subtext*, not the words: "baiting her," "pretending not to hear."
   - A new beat starts only when behavior clearly changes. [McKee ch11]
4. **Note the closing value and compare.** If it's the same charge, the scene is a non-event. [McKee ch11]
5. **Find the turning point.** Survey the beats and locate the one where the gap between expectation and result opens widest. That beat is the scene's hinge. If there isn't one, you've found the problem. [McKee ch11]

## What a good turn does to the reader

- A real turning point runs in four steps:
  1. surprise;
  2. curiosity about why;
  3. insight, as the reader rethinks earlier scenes and the setups click into a new shape;
  4. a new direction for the story.

  Information alone doesn't turn a scene. Insight does. [McKee ch10]
- A scene can turn on only two things: action or revelation. The strongest revelations come from the backstory, so save them for the major turns. [McKee ch15]
- Beats run as action, then reaction, then decision, and each scene should grow out of the decision that ended the one before it. [Cron ch8]
- Ask "And so?" of every scene. If you can't say how it changes the protagonist's pursuit, it's a digression. [Cron ch8]

## Text and subtext

- The text is what's seen, heard and said. The subtext is what's really going on. [McKee ch11]
  - If the characters say exactly what they mean, the scene goes flat.
  - A love scene can play as two people changing a tire.
  - A confession can be a request for help.
- Put the emphasis on reactions. When a knock gets answered politely and nothing changes, the beat is dead. [McKee ch7]
- To find the character's move, ask what you would do in their place, in their circumstances. Then ask what the opposite would be. That's where the gap is. [McKee ch7]
- Characters speak honestly, as themselves. If a person would swear, let them swear. False dialogue is the fault readers forgive least. [King, On Writing]

## Rhythm between scenes

- Rhythm is the length of a scene; tempo is how much happens inside it. [McKee ch12]
  - Speed up toward an act climax with shorter scenes and faster tempo.
  - Then let the climactic scene breathe; it has earned the pause.
- After a peak, ease off so the reader can take it in. Pacing is the interval between moments of conflict. Constant conflict becomes the new normal and stops registering. [Cron ch8]
- Link scenes through something they share or oppose: an object, a word, a sound, a quality of light. [McKee ch12]

## Quick scene checklist

- [ ] Someone wants something here, now, and something opposes it directly. [McKee ch11]
- [ ] The value's charge changes between the first line and the last. [McKee ch2]
- [ ] The turn comes from action or revelation, not from a character explaining. [McKee ch15]
- [ ] The scene grows out of the last scene's decision and forces the next one. [Cron ch8]
- [ ] Nobody tells anyone something they both already know. [McKee ch15]
- [ ] Say what the scene needs and move on; a scene is not a place to show off what you researched. [King, On Writing]
