# Antagonism, Stakes and Depth

Use this when the villain is weak, the stakes feel low, or the ending lands without depth.

## The principle

- A story is only as compelling as the forces working against the protagonist. Those forces include every opposition: inner, personal and extra-personal, not just a villain. [McKee ch14]
- When a story is weak, the cause is nearly always weak antagonism. Build the negative side first; the positive side will pay off on its own. [McKee ch14]
- At the inciting incident, the protagonist should be the underdog, with a chance, but only a chance. [McKee ch14]
- Make the opposition personal, present, active and specific, and put it on a clock. An abstract threat that never shows up on the page can't pressure anyone. [Cron ch9]
- Let everything that can go wrong go wrong. Make the protagonist's efforts to fix things make them worse, and make her pay for everything she gets. [Cron ch9]
- Give the antagonist doubts, and give the hero moments of wanting to turn away from the right thing. Pure villains and pure heroes don't convince. [King, On Writing]

## The negative declension (negation of the negation)

Take the story's primary value and map how far it can fall. [McKee ch14]

| Positive | Contrary | Contradictory | Negation of the negation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Justice | Unfairness | Injustice | Tyranny (might wearing the robes of law) |
| Love | Indifference | Hate | Hatred passed off as love; self-hate |
| Truth | White lie | Lie | Self-deception |
| Loyalty | Divided allegiance | Betrayal | Self-betrayal |
| Freedom | Restraint | Slavery | Slavery that believes it is free |
| Courage | Fear | Cowardice | Cowardice that calls itself courage |
| Communication | Alienation | Isolation | Madness |
| Achievement | Compromise | Failure | Selling out |

How to use it:

- Mark which level each act reaches. Usually Act One touches the contrary, the middle acts reach the contradictory, and the final act goes down to the negation of the negation. From there the story ends tragically, or climbs back to the positive changed by what it has seen. [McKee ch14]
- If the story never reaches the fourth column, it may satisfy, but it won't be remembered. Ask what the value looks like when it turns against itself in this particular world. [McKee ch14]

## Ironic shapes for endings

When the ending feels too simple, test these ironic patterns. Each depends on the character being certain about what they're doing. [McKee ch12]

- They get what they wanted, too late to have it.
- They are pushed away from the goal, and the push delivers them to it.
- They throw away what they will later find they can't live without.
- Every step toward the goal carries them further from it.
- Destroying something turns out to be what destroys them.
- They try to get rid of what turns out to be their happiness.

## The ending's charge

- **Up:** the positive wins.
- **Down:** the negative wins.
- **Ironic:** a clear double statement of what was gained and what was lost. Irony is not vagueness. It is precise, and it leans one way. [McKee ch6]
- The last two act climaxes should carry opposite charges, so a down-ending needs an up-beat before it and an up-ending needs a down-beat before it. [McKee ch9]
