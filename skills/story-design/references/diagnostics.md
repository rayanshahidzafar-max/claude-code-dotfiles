# Story Diagnostics

Use this when a draft or outline "doesn't work" and you need to name the cause before fixing it. Find the symptom, confirm the cause in the text, and apply the fix. Report findings as symptom → evidence (quote the scene or line) → cause → fix.

| Symptom | Likely cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| The middle sags | The same size of action repeats; complications don't escalate [McKee ch9] | Rule out the easy actions one by one so each new attempt costs more. For a long middle, add a mid-act climax |
| The ending falls flat | No true dilemma at the crisis, or the climax gets explained [McKee ch13] | Rebuild the crisis as a choice between irreconcilable goods, and make the climactic action speak for itself |
| The ending feels cheap | A coincidence or outside rescue decides it [McKee ch16] | Let the protagonist's own choice decide it, and plant any necessary coincidence early |
| Events feel melodramatic | Big events have too few causes [McKee ch16] | Add causes, not volume. Build motivation in earlier scenes |
| Scenes are "fine but boring" | Expectation equals result; nothing turns [McKee ch7] | Open a gap in every scene. The world answers the character's action differently or harder than expected |
| Characters explain things to each other | Table-dusting exposition [McKee ch15] | Cut it, or turn the fact into a weapon someone uses in the scene's conflict |
| The protagonist drifts | No conscious desire, or no risk in failing [McKee ch7] | Name the object of desire and the worst thing that happens without it |
| Too many people and places | Complication without complexity [McKee ch9] | Fewer characters and locations; conflict on all three levels at once |
| A plot hole | Logic gap in the event chain [McKee ch16] | Close it. Or, if nobody will notice at reading speed, leave it. Or have a character name it and move past |
| The reader is confused | They lack information at the moment they need it [Cron ch12] | Chart who knows what, when, scene by scene; move facts to the moment they're needed |
| Events happen but mean nothing | The plot doesn't touch the protagonist's inner issue [Cron ch2] | Make each twist force the protagonist to deal with the issue |
| The theme feels preachy | The meaning is explained instead of dramatized [McKee ch6] | Let the climax carry the meaning; cut any dialogue that states it |
| The structure shows like scaffolding | A shape imposed from outside, not grown from the material [McPhee, Structure] | Re-derive the order from the material's own strongest moments |
| The planned ending won't work | The story may have ended earlier than planned [McPhee, Structure] | Look back upstream for the real ending and cut the rest |
| Characters act like chess pieces | Plot is overriding what these people would honestly do [King, On Writing] | Put them in the situation and let them act as they would; change the plot, not the people |
| A subplot drags | It runs alongside instead of complicating the main line [McKee ch9] | Wire it into the central conflict or cut it |

## Order of operations when several symptoms appear

1. Fix desire and stakes first. Everything else depends on them. [McKee ch7]
2. Then the crisis and climax. The whole story is rewritten backward from there. [McKee ch13]
3. Then the progressions and scene turns. [McKee ch9]
4. Only then exposition, subtext and pacing. Prose problems go to `revision-passes` and `sentence-craft`, not here. Readers follow a gripping story through plain prose, but beautiful prose can't save a story nobody cares about. [Cron ch1]
