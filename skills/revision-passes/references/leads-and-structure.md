# Leads, Structural Shapes, Endings and Details

Use this when the opening, the overall order or the ending needs work.

## The lead

- The lead is the hardest part. Once you've found it, the structure follows and you're free to write. When stuck on structure, stop studying your notes and write the lead. [McPhee, Structure]
- A lead lights the way into the story and makes a promise. It must be sound, and it must never promise what doesn't follow. [McPhee, Structure]
- Avoid the blaring lead: a big announcement that introduces something small. Use a "blind" lead, one that hides who or what it's about, rarely and only when it pays off at once. [McPhee, Structure]
- A lead can be a few words or several pages, as long as it sets a scene and implies the dimensions of what's coming. It's good when it belongs completely to what follows. [McPhee, Structure]
- Start in the middle of things. Skip the throat-clearing. [McPhee, Structure]
- Test the lead against the first-page questions from `reader-brain`: whose story, what's happening, what's at stake. [Cron ch1]

## Structural shapes

Choose the shape the material suggests. Don't pour material into a mold. [McPhee, Structure]

- **Chronological.** The default, because readers follow it without effort. [McPhee, Structure]
- **Later-first with flashback.** Open at a later, stronger moment. Flash back. Run forward past the opening point. Use verb tense to keep the reader oriented. [McPhee, Structure]
- **Cyclic.** Start partway through, in the present tense, and close with a flashback that stays back and almost closes the circle. [McPhee, Structure]
- **Two drawstrings.** A chronological strand at the start and one at the end pull tight a sack of thematic sections in the middle. [McPhee, Structure]
- **Thematic portraits.** Separate portraits of two people or forces, alternating and converging on the point where they meet. [McPhee, Structure]
- **Juxtaposition.** Put two segments side by side with nothing between them and let the white space make the argument. Place a character's admitted fear beside the record of their daring; the gap says what you'd rather not say outright. [McPhee, Structure]
- **Slow build.** Mention something briefly, amplify it much later, add more later still, until the reader is desperate. Then deliver it in full. [McPhee, Structure]

## Endings

- Know where you'll end before you've gone far into the draft. [McPhee, Structure]
- Endings are often found in the material. If the planned ending isn't working, look back upstream: you may have finished before you thought. [McPhee, Structure]
- A piece needs a starting point, a journey, and a place to stop. When the story is over, stop. [McPhee, Structure]
- Good lines announce themselves in the material. Hold on to them as candidates for the lead, the ending or the title. [McPhee, Omission]

## Choosing details

- Many small details combine into a single impression. Few are essential alone; together they are. [McPhee, Structure]
- Keep a detail when it:
  - sets the scene,
  - hints at an undercurrent in the people or place, or
  - simply sounds right. [McPhee, Structure]
- Too much decoration cheapens the whole. [McPhee, Structure]
- The one criterion for what goes in: it interests you. Never research the market to decide what to write. [McPhee, Omission]
- A detail you noticed is usually a detail that matters. Use the one detail you need, where you need it, rather than a clump. [Klinkenborg]
