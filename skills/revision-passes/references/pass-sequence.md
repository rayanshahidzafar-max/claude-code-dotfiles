# The Pass Sequence in Full

Each pass has an aim, a checklist and an output line for the revision log. Don't mix passes: when you hunt for one kind of problem, you see it; hunting for everything at once, you see nothing clearly.

## Pass 1 — Structure

**Aim:** make the order of the piece come out of its material, and make the structure invisible.

- List every segment of the draft on its own line or card, with two or three code words naming what it covers. Lay them out in order and look at the shape. [McPhee, Structure]
- The segments tied to fixed points (scenes that must come in a certain order) are easy. The free-floating ones are where the piece's character is made, so place them deliberately. [McPhee, Structure]
- Chronology usually wins, because it's the order readers most easily follow. Break it only for a reason: a stronger opening moment, or themes that want to gather together. [McPhee, Structure]
- Check the high moment. In a long piece it usually sits about three-fifths of the way through. If the peak comes much earlier, the back of the piece will feel like a long goodbye. [McPhee, Structure]
- Check the ending against the material. If the planned ending doesn't land, look back upstream. The piece may already have ended earlier. [McPhee, Structure]
- Readers shouldn't notice the structure, any more than they notice someone's bones. [McPhee, Structure]
- Before moving a scene, check what the reader will know at each point after the move. [Cron ch12]
- Ask "And so?" of every segment. If you can't say how it bears on the story question, it's a digression, however good the writing. [Cron ch8]
- Test each scene's value at its start and end. If the charge didn't change, the scene is a candidate to cut or merge. [McKee ch2]
- Spread exposition through the whole piece. Nothing goes in that the reader can easily assume, and the critical secrets come last. [McKee ch15]

**Log:** the old order and the new order (by code words), and the reason for each move.

## Pass 2 — Read aloud

**Aim:** let the ear find what the eye misses.

- Read the whole piece aloud, plainly, as if to one listener. Stop wherever your ear hears a disturbance, and mark it. [McPhee, Draft No. 4]
- Mark any sentence you couldn't imagine actually saying, then ask what you were trying to say. [Klinkenborg]
- Imagine your Ideal Reader listening. Mark where they'd laugh and where they'd get restless. The restless spots are where the pace is wrong. [King, On Writing]

**Log:** the number of marks, and three examples with fixes.

## Pass 3 — Static

**Aim:** remove the showy noise.

- Remove the noise: words that make a racket without adding sense. [McPhee, Draft No. 4]
- Hunt repetition with a word-frequency pass. [McPhee, Structure]
  - Fad words: pivot, iconic, reach out, next level.
  - Too many "but"s.
  - Distinctive words that should appear only once in the whole piece.
- Hunt indirection: facts slid in sideways instead of introduced. If you're introducing something, introduce it. Don't use "the" to pretend the reader already knows it. [McPhee, Draft No. 4]
- Hunt ambiguity. Read each sentence the way a literal-minded reader would and fix anything that can be taken two ways. [McPhee, Draft No. 4]
- Hunt forced logic: "in fact," "indeed," "therefore," "moreover," "thus." These words insist on connections that may not exist. Remove each one and see if the sentence still holds. [Klinkenborg]
- Hunt adverbs, especially on dialogue tags, and verbs that do the work of "said." Keep one only when it does something nothing else could. [King, Toolbox]
- Hunt qualifiers ("rather," "very," "pretty," "a little") and overstatement. One inflated superlative makes the reader doubt everything else. [S&W V.8]

**Log:** the words removed by type, and the indirections fixed.

## Pass 4 — Draft No. 4 boxes

**Aim:** make every word exactly right.

- Draw a box around every word or phrase that is wrong. Also box the words that work but present an opportunity for a better one. Don't linger while boxing; box everything, then go back one by one. [McPhee, Draft No. 4]
- Settle each box with a dictionary, mostly for words you already know. Look up the word you're replacing and read its definition; the right word is often sitting inside that definition. [McPhee, Draft No. 4]
- A thesaurus is a rest stop. The dictionary is the destination, because it tells you how near-synonyms differ in shade. [McPhee, Draft No. 4]
- Know the distinctions that careful readers notice: farther is for distance, further is for degree. [McPhee, Draft No. 4]
- Don't try to fix a sentence using only the words it already has. Reimagine it. A better sentence is often hiding inside the bad one. [Klinkenborg]

**Log:** the number of boxes, and the best five before → after pairs.

## Pass 5 — Frame of reference

**Aim:** make every comparison do its own work.

- An allusion is only useful if the reader recognizes it, and references go stale quickly. Prefer the durable over the current. [McPhee, Frame of Reference]
- Don't borrow vividness. Saying someone looks like a famous person asks that person to do your writing. If you borrow, pay it back with your own specific description. [McPhee, Frame of Reference]
- Don't explain a reference as a punch line, the way "a band called the Beatles" talks down to the reader. [McPhee, Frame of Reference]
- In invented worlds, frame of reference means the world's own shared touchstones: its legends, places and famous events. Use them the same way, and make sure each one was set up earlier.

**Log:** the references cut, replaced or paid back.

## Pass 6 — Checkpoints

**Aim:** tick every fact.

- Every word with a fact attached gets checked: names, dates, numbers, distances, and the rules of the story's world. Anything you can't verify comes out or gets fixed. [McPhee, Checkpoints]
- For fiction, this is the continuity check. Hold the text against the world notes and design sheet. Watch for timelines, who knows what, names and titles, where objects are, and whether world rules stay consistent. [McPhee, Checkpoints]
- Chase down the single doubtful item. The one anecdote you can't confirm is the one that will be wrong. [McPhee, Checkpoints]

**Log:** each fact checked and each fix made.

## Pass 7 — Omission

**Aim:** cut about ten percent so nothing seems to be missing. Full method in `greening.md`.

- Cut the author out first. To the extent a passage is about the writer's feelings rather than the story, remove it. [McPhee, Omission]
- Leave room for the reader to create. A few images can make a whole landscape. The white space between segments lets the reader supply the unwritten thought. [McPhee, Omission]
- Too many good jokes weaken each other. So do too many striking images. Keep the best, and trust fewer to do more. [McPhee, Omission]
- Trust the reader. Don't over-narrate, over-describe, over-interpret or over-signal. [Klinkenborg]
- It is seldom wise to tell everything. Let the conversation and the action reveal the manner; don't explain it afterward. [S&W V.11]

**Log:** word count before and after, and the percentage cut.
