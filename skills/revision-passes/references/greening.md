# Greening: Cutting Ten Percent So Nothing Seems Missing

The term comes from magazine layout. An editor would mark a piece "green four," meaning take out four lines so that nobody can tell anything is gone. Applied to a whole draft, the target is about ten percent. It improves nearly everything. [McPhee, Omission]

## Method

1. **Record the count.** Write down the word count and the target (count × 0.9).
2. **Cut the author.** Anything about the writer's own feelings, process or cleverness goes first. [McPhee, Omission]
3. **Cut from the middle, not the ends.**
   - Take whole sentences or passages from the middle of runs, not the endings that land them.
   - Never cut in a way that damages the tone. [McPhee, Omission]
4. **Cut what the reader already has.**
   - Explanations of what a scene has already shown.
   - Summaries of what the reader just read.
   - Stage directions nobody needs, like a character sitting down in a chair. [King, And Furthermore]
5. **Cut the iceberg's tip where it shows too much.** What you know and leave out can strengthen what remains, as long as you really know it. [McPhee, Omission]
6. **Cut widows.** A paragraph whose last line holds one or two words: trimming a few words above it wins back a whole line. [McPhee, Omission]
7. **Cut needless words last.** Replace "the question as to whether" with "whether," and "the fact that" with a simpler construction. Needless-word cuts add up to a surprising amount. [S&W R17]
8. **Recount.** If you're under ten percent, go back to steps 3–5. Word-level trimming alone rarely gets there.

## Guardrails

- Don't cut the setups a payoff depends on. Check the who-knows-what-when chart before removing any fact. [Cron ch10]
- Don't let the cut become violence to meaning. If a passage is doing work that nothing else does, it stays, and the cut comes from somewhere else. [McPhee, Omission]
- A short sentence isn't the goal. Omitting needless words doesn't mean making every sentence short or stripping out detail. It means every word must do work. [S&W R17]
- The measure is that the reader should not be able to tell. Read the greened version aloud. If a jump or gap shows, restore the bridge in fewer words. [McPhee, Omission]

## Worked example (illustrative)

Before (46 words):

> It was at that moment, as she stood there in the doorway of the depot, that Mara realized, with a sudden and terrible certainty, that the log had been wiped, and that someone who knew the codes had done it deliberately.

After (22 words):

> Mara stopped in the depot doorway. The log was blank. Whoever wiped it had known the codes — and had wanted her to see.

What went:

- the throat-clearing "It was at that moment";
- the telling "realized, with a sudden and terrible certainty";
- the redundant "deliberately," once the new ending said it better.

The emphatic idea now closes the paragraph. [S&W R22]
