# Plausibility and Research

Suspense lives on concrete facts. Get one wrong and an expert reader drops the book, and an editor sends it back.

## What to check

- A suspense writer's problems are concrete:
  - how fast the train runs;
  - police procedure;
  - how many pills kill;
  - the limits of a person's strength;
  - how stupid or clever the police can plausibly be. [Highsmith ch7]
- Geography may need adjusting, with distances shortened or lengthened to fit the plot. Decide deliberately and record it. [Highsmith ch7]
- If the plot will use a special talent or handicap (sharp hearing, a phobia), plant it early. [Highsmith ch7]
- A victim must put up the fight his situation implies. A man who goes too quietly reads false, even if he has little will to live. [Highsmith ch9]
- Know your city. If every local knows a door can't be left unlatched by accident, a plot that needs it will lose them. [Highsmith ch10]
- Check doses, salaries, legacies and sums as carefully as the big facts. An editor will ask, and is usually right. [Highsmith ch10]

## How to check

- Revisions about police procedure are the hardest. They involve technicalities, plot juggling, and sweat. Work the puzzle out on paper first: write down the facts from the book and see how they can be rearranged. [Highsmith ch9]
- When in doubt, ask. Visit a police station, or call someone who works there. [Highsmith ch9]
- Research from primary sources. A letter from someone describing their ordinary day can give what no book does; so can forty minutes spent watching a place. [Highsmith ch10]
- Learn other people's trades: their workrooms and the way they talk. Take a job if you can, and keep notes. Any new scene is fertile, a derelict street as much as a famous view. [Highsmith ch7]
- Check facts the way a magazine checker does. Every name, date, distance, dose and procedure in the log gets a source, and anything you invented is marked as invented. [McPhee, Checkpoints]
- Readers will trust a world remote from their own lives. But the moment a reader knows more about the subject than the author, the game is over. Research until that reader is rare. [Ligotti, Creating Horror notes]

## The plausibility log

Keep it in the story folder, beside the suspense sheet:

```
PLAUSIBILITY LOG
| # | Fact the plot turns on | Where (chapter/scene) | Source or check | Status (verified / adjusted / invented) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Sedative dose for a heavy adult | ch 14 | pharmacology reference + nurse consult | adjusted down |
| 2 | Last ferry time on Sundays | ch 3 | timetable | verified |
```

## The credulity check

Before the final draft, list every coincidence and every lucky break in the plot. For each one, record:
- where it falls (early, middle or late);
- what earlier detail makes it plausible;
- whether it resolves anything. A late coincidence that resolves the plot fails. [McKee ch16]
