# From Germ to Plot

Use this file at steps 1, 4 and 5: finding the germ, developing it, and thickening the plot. The examples are original.

## The germ

- A germ is anything that sets the imagination moving: a respectable man pocketing something he didn't pay for, a stranger's odd remark, or a short burst of action that arrives with no characters attached. [Highsmith ch1]
- State it in a single line. *A night nurse realizes the patient everyone calls comatose has been writing letters.* If it won't fit in a line, it isn't a germ yet.
- You know a real germ by the excitement it brings, the same lift as a good line of verse. False germs don't grow and don't stay in the mind. [Highsmith ch1]
- A "so what" germ needs complication. Vary it until it bites: make the second event a clumsy copy of the first, and let the copy expose the original. [Highsmith ch1]
- When a germ refuses to come alive, wait for a second one. Often the missing piece is a stronger motive: obsession or love will drive a scheme that money won't. [Highsmith ch1]
- Two germs can combine, and the story often turns on the object where they meet. [Highsmith ch1]
- Freshen a stock device by inverting it. The suspicious bundle that holds nothing makes the person carrying it the suspect. [Highsmith ch1]
- A story handed to you by a friend is rarely usable. It is already a story, so the imagination has nothing to do and rejects it like a graft. [Highsmith ch1]
- The best germs often come from something you felt: being followed down a dark street, coming home to a room someone has been through. Let the imagination escalate the feeling until it becomes a plot. [Highsmith ch2]
- A germ that is only a clever fact (a poison that leaves no trace, a lock that opens backwards) is a potential, not a story, until characters bring it to life. [Highsmith ch3]
- Keep a notebook. Three or four words can hold a mood. In a dry spell, browse it, because two old notes may combine. Feeling empty of ideas usually means tiredness or the wrong company, so change the scene. [Highsmith ch1]
- The strongest premises are situations. Put people in a predicament and watch them try to work free, rather than forcing them down a plotted track. [King, On Writing]

## Development

- Development is everything between the germ and the outline, and it can take weeks or years of slow brewing. Know how your characters look, dress and talk, and know their childhoods, even the parts that will never reach the page. [Highsmith ch4]
- Borrow a real person's face if you like, but never their face and personality together. The inner character has to be invented. [Highsmith ch4]
- The setting decides most of the cast, but one character who doesn't belong there can be more interesting than the typical one. [Highsmith ch4]
- Plot-first and character-first are equally good routes. Often a bare scrap of action, before anyone is cast in it, becomes the hub or the climax. [Highsmith ch4]
- Development weaves back and forth. For any germ, imagine half a dozen situations that might lead into it or follow from it. Let reason strike out the illogical ones, then wait: the survivors come alive later, usually when you aren't looking. [Highsmith ch4]
- Fill notebook pages with rambles around the germ. Most of them won't resemble the final book, but they prepare the ground for the ideas that will. [Highsmith ch4]
- List the story's elements (a miscarriage of justice, a marriage under strain, an addiction, a hardening) and arrange them in dramatic form. [Highsmith ch10]
- Let the hero's flaw explain his predicament. An over-trusting engineer signs papers a crooked contractor puts in front of him. [Highsmith ch10]
- Make a betrayal plausible by giving the rival exactly the qualities the betrayed partner admires. [Highsmith ch10]
- One character doing three jobs (bringer of bad news, keeper of a secret, instigator of the next crime) beats three characters doing one each. [Highsmith ch10]
- A goad character stokes the hero's worst feeling for his own reasons. The hero sees through it and is affected anyway. [Highsmith ch10]
- A small creature or object can carry a large turn. A pet discovered and taken away sparks the riot in which the hero's only friend dies. [Highsmith ch10]
- Don't start writing until the story wells up, the feeling of "I can't wait to tell this." [Highsmith ch2]
- Every story question is a promise. The inciting incident must open it with full force, and the climax must answer it. [McKee ch8]

## Thickening the plot

- Thickening means piling complications on the hero, or on his enemies. Surprise events work best. [Highsmith ch4]
- Even a simple plot can thicken. A woman who can't bring herself to kill her husband is a story of indecision, and interruptions and letters can complicate it without anything else happening. [Highsmith ch4]
- **A pattern of four thickeners.** For a hero suspected of a crime he didn't commit:
  1. A witness, and the means of witnessing. A neighbour with binoculars sees him carry something out at dawn, and his reaction to the binoculars deepens her suspicion.
  2. A vulnerability in that witness that turns a good deed into fresh suspicion. He brings her good news; her weak heart fails as he comes in; now he is suspected of that too.
  3. A rival who profits from the hero's ruin and paints him black to the police.
  4. The hero's own record. Notes he made for innocent reasons read like a confession when someone else finds them. [Highsmith ch4]
- Invent the most logical bolsterings, the ones that make the story stronger and more believable. Twenty or thirty make readers laugh. [Highsmith ch4]
- Thickeners should bite on the lead's inner issue. Each twist should push the lead toward the very thing she has been avoiding. [Cron ch2]
- Every complication should cost more than the last. Progressive complications build toward a point of no return. [McKee ch9]
- A protagonist and a plot are only as compelling as the forces against them. Push the opposition to its darkest form, beyond simple wrongdoing to wrong that feels right to the one doing it. [McKee ch14]
- Reveal backstory only as ammunition, when a character uses the past as a weapon in a present conflict. [McKee ch15]
