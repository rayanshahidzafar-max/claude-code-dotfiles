# Plotting, Tempo and Snags

## Outlining

- Plotting is outlining, the work that comes after development. Outline chapter by chapter, even in brief, especially early in a career, because young writers ramble. A rambling, atmospheric, purple chapter that can't carry a point should go. [Highsmith ch5]
- Keep the whole book in proportion while you draft. A writer with her nose on the page can spend two hundred pages on small matters and tell half the story. [Highsmith ch6]
- **The domino.** Picture the plot as a domino tile split by a raised line across its centre. That line is the middle of the book in pages and in plot. Spread minor climaxes on both sides, not all crowded just past the middle or at the far end. Being far off the mark in either direction means something is wrong. [Highsmith ch6]
- **Or graph it.** Draw an up-and-down line with events at the peaks, and pencil in rough page numbers to show where you should be by each event. [Highsmith ch6]
- See at least one chapter ahead of the one you are writing, and end each working day by thinking through tomorrow's events. You will start the next morning with a definite job. [Highsmith ch6]
- A chapter is a little act and needs its own emotional bang, large or small. [Highsmith ch6]
- Expect a foggy spot late in the book, near the three-quarter mark. Many books change there, often because a character refuses to behave. That may improve the book, or the character may need curbing. You are the boss. [Highsmith ch5]

## Tempo

- Tempo is partly plotting and partly natural style. Don't force a speed that feels unnatural to you. [Highsmith ch5]
- A quiet scene written in nervous prose prepares the reader for violence. A leisurely manner that doesn't prepare them at all can be still more effective. [Highsmith ch5]
- Arrange events in the most entertaining order, and the right tempo will usually follow. [Highsmith ch5]
- End a suspense paragraph or chapter on its most dangerous new element. The end of a unit is its position of emphasis. [S&W R22]
- A slow, tranquil opening lets the reader get to know the hero-criminal. A sharp opening (a crime by someone we barely know) can then hand over to a slower second lead. Both work. [Highsmith ch5]
- Dialogue is dramatic, so ration it. Summarize forty lines of argument in a sentence or two, then let a single speech stand alone in its own paragraph. [Highsmith ch6]
- One or two details establish a room. Describe everything only when the room is full of incongruities. [Highsmith ch6]
- Cut stretches of predictable events (journeys, arrivals, job hunts) down to the emotional beats inside them, plus the one action that moves the plot. [Highsmith ch10]

## Surprise without cheating

- False mystery (a fact the reader is denied only to fake curiosity) and cheap surprise (a jolt with no insight behind it) both abuse the reader. A true surprise splits expectation from result, and a rush of insight closes the gap. [McKee ch16]
- Make setups visible as a pattern of dots the reader can follow. Every payoff must be logistically possible, and nothing gets planted by accident. [Cron ch10]
- Foreshadow any act that would seem out of character, so the reader thinks "of course," not "give me a break." [Cron ch11]
- Readers spot twists coming. A gradual accumulation of detail, which makes the ending shocking and inevitable at once, beats a twist. [Uncanny, McKnight Hardy]
- A flash-forward that tells the reader the outcome (that a character will be found dead tomorrow) changes the reader from companion to watcher, and dread replaces curiosity. [Uncanny, McKnight Hardy]
- Small details of inevitability raise dread: the mark that will be redrawn if it rubs off, the form already signed in the character's own hand. [Uncanny, Moore]
- Escalate by degrees, with each odd detail building on the last. Mark the point past which nothing can be explained away (a detail the lead notices and doesn't question), and let the reader feel the floor go. [Uncanny, Dyson]
- A suspense sequence can work like a skid on a wet road: a firm grip, then a slide, then the moment where nothing feels safe or off-limits, then impact. [Ligotti, Plot]
- In a series, each mystery or suspense plot ends; a new unresolved situation, planted before the old one closes, keeps readers moving. [Tomlinson ch16]

## Locks and escalations

- **Blackmail escalates.** A threat to expose the first crime pushes the hero toward a second one. [Highsmith ch10]
- **Dovetail the guilt** to lock an ending. Two guilty people agree to alibi each other, each held by fear of exposure for their own crime. [Highsmith ch10]
- **Swap the victims** and both killers get genuine alibis, each far from the crime they wanted. The price: they must never be seen together again. [Highsmith ch10]
- With a single viewpoint, you must still work out what offstage characters are doing (who hired whom, who has been paid), even if the reader never sees it. [Highsmith ch10]
- **Turn the tables.** The intended victim learns the plan and makes a deal with the very tool meant to destroy her. [Highsmith ch3]
- **The culprit's own care can convict him.** The detail he adds to make the scene look natural is the one that hangs him. [Highsmith ch3]

## Snags

- **The cure for a big snag.** Small snags take minutes; big ones, writing yourself into a corner, come in the second half. Return to the first idea and whatever excited you about it. Ask what you want to happen, then change the plot or a character so it can. [Highsmith ch7]
- Know the effect you want (tragic, comic, melancholy) before you begin, and return to it when stuck. [Highsmith ch7]
- **Three causes of a stall:**
  - You can't see the events ahead. Stop and work out the next few chapters before writing on.
  - You are forcing a character against his grain.
  - The plot has become too illogical to convince even you. [Highsmith ch7]
- "What happens next?" is often only a problem of order. If thinking won't settle it, walk away and busy yourself with something unrelated. The mind arranges events dramatically when left alone. [Highsmith ch7]
- **A motionless tangle.** Invent solutions, even wild ones that could later be made logical. If nothing comes, step away. If you care about the book, the answer will come. [Highsmith ch7]
- Semi-mechanical work on the book itself (retyping, tidying earlier chapters) can shake an answer loose, and so can sleeping on it. [Highsmith ch7]
- Clinging to a scene you once imagined can wreck a book, especially if it leaves the hero passive. Passive heroes bore unless they are deliberately comic. [Highsmith ch10]
- Many snags are groundwork failures: something you need later was never set up early. Go back and establish it. [Highsmith ch7]
- Trust the "wrong noise." Like a mechanic hearing a fault in an engine, a writer should sense when something is off and fix it before it spreads. [Highsmith ch7]

## Short stories and novelettes

- A novel usually has:
  - a longer span of time;
  - real change in its characters;
  - more scenes;
  - a climax built on several earlier scenes;
  - room for changes of mood, pace and viewpoint.
  - A short story, by contrast, can turn on one slender fact in one scene lasting five minutes. [Highsmith ch3]
- A novelette is a telescoped novel. It carries the same action, character change and viewpoint shifts, told more briefly, and needs as much thought. [Highsmith ch3]
- An appealing, important secondary character can lift a story built on a gimmick. [Highsmith ch3]
- The memorable story ends with a thought: a comment the action doesn't need that gives the whole piece weight. [Highsmith ch3]
- Never throw away a good story line, even if it only exists as a synopsis. [Highsmith ch3]
