# Mystery Mode

Use this file when the reader knows less than the characters: the whodunit, the how-will-they-catch-him, the hidden past. Even here, keep danger close, so the mystery stays suspense.

## The two shapes

- There are two kinds of mystery. In a **closed mystery** (whodunit) the reader holds several suspects, three or more, and the question is who. In an **open mystery** the reader sees the crime and watches the investigator close in, and the question is how they will be caught. [McKee ch16]
- A third, quieter kind: no murder at all. The suspense comes from the anxiety and the judgments of the people around someone suspected of one. [Highsmith ch11]
- Dramatic irony can carry a crime story with no secret. The reader knows who did it and wants to know whether the evidence will be found, suppressed or misread. [Highsmith ch6]

## Fair play

- Information withheld arbitrarily until the last page is a gimmick, not a plot. [Highsmith ch2]
- Never hide what the viewpoint character knows just to fake curiosity. That is false mystery, and readers feel cheated when it is revealed. [McKee ch16]
- Read without the reveal, the story should still hold together. Read with it, the story should hold together better. [Cron ch7]
- Clues are setups. Plant them where the reader can see them, let them build, and make every payoff logistically possible. [Cron ch10]
- A false lead is a setup too. It must pay off later as the reason someone looked guilty, not simply vanish. [Cron ch10]
- Balance the clues: enough that the ending doesn't come from nowhere, not so many that readers guess it early. [Scheuerer ch12]
- Leave small clues for small reveals along the way, as breadcrumbs that reward the attentive reader before the big payoff arrives. [Scheuerer ch12]
- Run smaller open loops beside the main question. A question asked, a clue discovered, or something set under investigation each carries its own small arc. [Scheuerer ch13]
- The goal is the "aha": the plot makes sense in hindsight. Readers should appreciate it, not predict it. [Scheuerer ch12]
- The best clue is one the culprit leaves through his own care, the touch he added to make the scene look natural. [Highsmith ch3]

## The investigation

- The police must be neither stupid nor sharp-then-blind. If they would surely follow an obvious lead, give them a less obvious one, or a reason not to follow it. [Highsmith ch9]
- An unreliable narrator lets the reader slowly realize who really did it. The realization then makes them doubt everything else they were told, and want to read it again. [Uncanny, McKnight Hardy]
- The mystery genre's themes are justice against injustice (moral, not only legal), suspects' guilty secrets, and a disturbed equilibrium restored. [Tomlinson ch8]
- In a cozy, murder upsets a safe community's balance, and the investigator puts it right. [Tomlinson ch7]
- A detective's own past can be a slow mystery revealed piece by piece across a series. Answer small secrets now and bigger ones later; the longer one is withheld, the bigger its payoff must be. [Tomlinson ch10]

## Series mysteries

- Every novel in a mystery series must solve its own whodunit, whatever threads run on. [Rosett, Why]
- A small community with a murder every book strains belief. Plan early how the sleuth's patch widens or moves. [Rosett, Introduction]

## If you write a whodunit anyway

- Highsmith's own whodunit lost her strengths: surprise, speed, stretched credulity and intimacy with the killer. Before choosing the form, ask what you will put in their place. [Highsmith ch11]
- Keep the marks of quality: insight into minds, interest in justice, people who seem real. A puzzle alone is forgotten. [Highsmith ch11]
- In dramatic-irony scenes the reader's question shifts from what to how and why: how did they come to do what we already know they did? [McKee ch16]
