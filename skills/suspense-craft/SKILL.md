# Suspense Craft

This skill makes plots that keep danger close. It grows a germ of an idea into a suspense or mystery plot and keeps it honest:
- a danger the reader can feel on every page;
- complications that arrive as surprises;
- a deliberate choice about what the reader knows;
- credulity stretched but never broken;
- police, poisons and timetables that hold up.

It produces two things:
- **The suspense sheet** (`references/suspense-sheet.md`). It goes into the story folder's design file.
- **A plausibility log** for every fact the plot turns on.

`story-design` builds the structure around the sheet, and `story-forge` drafts it.

**Important:** never quote or reproduce text from the source books. Paraphrase the ideas, use your own examples, and apply them to the person's story.

## The core ideas

- In the trade sense, a suspense story is one where violent action, even death, stays close the whole time. [Highsmith, Foreword]
- Any complete story carries some suspense. The suspense story turns it up, and its lively frame can still carry serious thought and quiet stretches. [Highsmith ch1]
- Please yourself first. If the story keeps you amused for as long as it takes to write, readers will follow. [Highsmith ch1]
- There is no formula. The only secret is individuality: what this writer alone notices, feels and finds funny. [Highsmith, Preface]
- Build from felt experience, not gimmicks. Surprise endings, obscure technical facts, and information withheld unfairly until the end make flimsy entertainment. [Highsmith ch2]
- A plot improves by thickening: complications piled on the hero, best when they arrive as surprises. [Highsmith ch4]
- Stretch the reader's credulity as far as it will go, but never break it. The ideal turn is unexpected and still consistent with the characters. [Highsmith ch5]
- Caring isn't liking. Readers will follow a criminal they'd never trust, as long as they care whether he goes free or gets caught. [Highsmith ch8]
- Do more than describe brutality. Shed light on the minds involved, care about justice or its absence, about cowardice and courage, and make the invented people seem real. [Highsmith ch11]
- Interest runs on two engines: curiosity (the need to know what happens) and concern (fear for someone the reader cares about). Suspense uses both. [McKee ch16]
- The opening line has one job: make the reader need the line after it. [Cron ch1]

## How to use this in practice

1. **Find the germ** and state it in one line. If it leaves you shrugging, look for the complication or the second germ that brings it alive. → `references/germ-to-plot.md` [Highsmith ch1]
2. **Choose the reader's position**: suspense, dramatic irony or mystery (below).
3. **Ask the crucial questions early.** Does the hero end victor or vanquished? Is the atmosphere comic, tragic, both, or flat reporting of a cruel fate? What effect is the story for? [Highsmith ch4]
4. **Develop.** Flesh out characters, setting and atmosphere until you see them as clearly as a photograph. Think of several situations that could lead to or follow the germ, let reason cut the illogical ones, and wait for a survivor to come alive. [Highsmith ch4]
5. **Thicken.** List the complications and keep the few that make the story stronger and more believable. Twenty of them make readers laugh. [Highsmith ch4]
6. **Plot the chapters.** For each chapter, write how it advances the story and the one to three points it must make. Place the climax, then check the proportions. → `references/plotting.md` [Highsmith ch5]
7. **Log every fact the plot turns on** (doses, distances, timetables, procedure) and check each one. → `references/plausibility.md` [Highsmith ch7]
8. **Draft once the story feels alive.** Start only when the opening section has a sense of life you can see and feel. Keep the plot loose enough for characters to choose, and end each day knowing tomorrow's events. [Highsmith ch4]
9. **Clear snags** with the snag list in `references/plotting.md`.
10. **Revise.** Read as a stranger would, fix the biggest problem first, cut, clarify, then check police and procedure. [Highsmith ch8]

Fill in the suspense sheet as you go. It rides in the story folder's `02-design.md`.

## Suspense, dramatic irony or mystery

The first design decision is how much the reader knows compared with the characters.

| Mode | The reader knows | It runs on |
|---|---|---|
| Suspense | about what the lead knows | curiosity and concern |
| Dramatic irony | more than the characters | dread and compassion: how and why will they do what we know they'll do? |
| Mystery | less than the characters | curiosity alone |

- Most stories mix the three inside an overall suspense design. Choose a dominant mode, then use the others scene by scene. [McKee ch16]
- In suspense the criminal is usually known from the start, and the writer has to render his mind from inside. That takes sympathy for him, and it is the main difference from the mystery. [Highsmith ch5]
- When Highsmith hid her murderer offstage to write a whodunit, the book lost what her suspense ran on: surprise, speed, stretched credulity, and above all intimacy with the killer. Know your strong points before choosing the mode. [Highsmith ch11]
- Dramatic irony makes the reader suffer on a character's behalf. Let the reader know that the caller on the step means no harm, or that nobody will open the hiding place for months, while the character can't know it. [Highsmith ch3]
- A character who feels safe while the reader can see the danger makes the reader more afraid. [Uncanny, Royle]
- Track who knows what, and when: the real timeline, what each major character believes, and what the reader believes, scene by scene. [Cron ch12]
- In mystery mode, use `references/mystery.md` for fair clues, false leads and the solution.

## Plotting the danger

- Decide where the climax falls (the middle, three-quarters, or the very end) and whether the book needs more than one. Some climaxes must be the last thing on the page. [Highsmith ch5]
- Coincidence can open a story when it is almost, but not quite, incredible: a stranger's audacious plan, a fateful choice of messenger. [Highsmith ch5]
- Bring coincidence in early and let it gain meaning. Never let one turn the ending. [McKee ch16]
- At a fuzzy spot, the obvious solution presents itself first. It is usually the lazy one, so distrust it. [Highsmith ch5]
- Shocking the reader at the expense of logic is a cheap trick. Sensational action and clever prose can't hide a lack of invention. [Highsmith ch5]
- A situation becomes suspense the moment the reader knows the danger. A surprise inside it (a dangerous stranger the would-be killer decides to use) turns ordinary menace into a story. [Highsmith ch3]
- Make the bad outcome probable and the good one improbable; underdogs pull harder. [Tomlinson ch16]
- Set up an expected confrontation and delay it, but when it comes, put it on the page. Readers resent visible manipulation. [Tomlinson ch16]
- When two suspenseful lines are running, cut between them, leaving one hanging while you follow the other. [Tomlinson ch16]
- Make the opposition personal, present, active and specific, and wire it to a ticking clock. [Cron ch9]
- Raise the stakes by risking something vital to someone the reader cares about. A nastier threat or a longer chase doesn't do it. [Tomlinson ch16]
- Build the crisis as a true dilemma, a choice between two goods or two evils. The criminal lead's character shows in what he chooses under that pressure. [McKee ch13]
- Show what the lead expects before each move, so the reader feels it when the expectation breaks. [Cron ch3]
- Tempo, proportion, endings that lock, and snags → `references/plotting.md`.

## The criminal at the center

- Give a criminal hero pleasant qualities: generosity, kindness to some people, a love of music or cooking. They are amusing next to his crimes. [Highsmith ch4]
- Or make him wholly dark and fascinating, and balance him with a decent counterpart. A reader who needs someone to identify with can take a lesser character, preferably one he doesn't kill. [Highsmith ch4]
- Criminals are dramatically interesting because they act, they are free in spirit, and they bow to nobody. [Highsmith ch5]
- You needn't have killed anyone to write a killer. Imagine murder as anger carried past its limit. [Highsmith ch7]
- Mood and pace must fit the protagonist. A restless, frightened lead needs restless prose. [Highsmith ch6]
- Readers like a writer who seems fully in command, with strength to spare. Thinking inside an insolent character can make the prose itself more assured. [Highsmith ch6]
- Bind two characters with several forces at once: what each knows about the other, a debt, a resemblance, love mixed with hate. [Highsmith ch1]
- An antagonist pursues his own goal, not villainy for fun. In a whodunit the murderer hides and the investigator proves; in a criminal-protagonist story the hunt runs the other way. [Tomlinson ch12]

## Point of view

- Choosing a point of view is a matter of comfort and of what kind of story it is: one told from the sidelines, or one told from inside the action. [Highsmith ch7]
- First person is the hardest form. The "I" palls, the narrator seems to be sitting at a desk writing, and an introspective schemer sounds nastier than when an author reports his thoughts. [Highsmith ch7]
- A single viewpoint gives intensity. Two contrasting viewpoints give changes of pace and mood. [Highsmith ch7]
- Never break viewpoint without warrant. A narrator who reports a scene he couldn't have witnessed weakens the whole story. [Highsmith ch7]
- In first person, dramatic irony still works if you put the same evidence in front of the reader and let them reach the conclusion the narrator misses, without making the narrator look stupid. [Tomlinson ch16]
- If a draft stalls a few chapters in, you may not be identifying with the viewpoint character. Stepping back and writing as if from a distance can free it. [Highsmith ch7]
- A bystander's reactions to what they see, or think they see, can raise more dread than the action itself. [Highsmith ch7]

## Openings and first chapters

- The first page leads the reader in or loses them. Write versions, then look again with a fresh eye the next day. [Highsmith ch6]
- Open on motion: a first line where something is already moving. [Highsmith ch6]
- Give a sense of movement before the reasons. Someone convinced they're being tailed is already a situation, and nobody needs their history yet. [Highsmith ch6]
- Don't open with a flood of facts about strangers, or with a scene of passion between people the reader hasn't met. That wastes the fire. [Highsmith ch6]
- One charged word can make a flat sentence ask a question: why would a man queuing for a film be impatient? [Highsmith ch6]
- Every first chapter needs lines of potential action:
  - a wish to leave and an inability to;
  - a want not yet had;
  - a danger, from damp in the walls to a mind going wrong.
  - In suspense, action or its promise must be there from the start. [Highsmith ch6]
- By the end of page one, the reader should know whose story it is, what is happening, and what is at stake. [Cron ch1]

## Atmosphere and the senses

- A neglected atmosphere gives the writer a thin-ice feeling without knowing why. Build atmosphere through all five senses: the smell of a house, the colour of a room, a sound from another room. [Highsmith ch7]
- A calm place can still feel wrong. An estate where nothing is amiss, where the trees seem about to fall inward, does more than a storm. [Highsmith ch7]
- Let sentences imply the threat. A sentence that says less than it knows makes the reader lean in; one that explains the menace lets it out. [Klinkenborg]
- A germ usually carries its atmosphere with it: a gloomy idea about a resourceless man wants a doomed hero. [Highsmith ch1]

## Revision

- Read the whole draft as a stranger would, fast, for pace, lags, unclear spots and emotional gaps. Don't fiddle with words on this pass. [Highsmith ch8]
- Ask honestly whether the reader cares about the hero, for or against. [Highsmith ch8]
- Implied, not speechified. Complex feelings between characters (a wife who loves two men) must be implied in the crucial conversations. Write those properly in the first draft, and if they ring false, scrap them and write them fresh. [Highsmith ch10]
- Cut minor characters who don't advance the plot, along with every reference to them. [Highsmith ch9]
- When no cut seems possible, find four more pages anyway. [Highsmith ch9]
- Keep clarity at the front of your mind. In suspense it is vital. [Highsmith ch8]
- Draft fast with the door closed, to keep the story's momentum, and open the door only for the rewrite. [King, On Writing]

## Where the books disagree

- **Plot it, or discover it?** Highsmith outlines chapter points but never makes a plot rigid, and leaves a foggy spot near the three-quarter mark. King works from a situation with no plot. McKee designs backward from the climax. Resolution used here: decide the effect, the hero's fate and the nature of the climax early. Outline chapter points one chapter ahead of the draft, and leave the late middle open for the characters to surprise you.
- **Coincidence.** Highsmith loves almost-incredible coincidences. McKee allows them only early, never past the midpoint and never in the ending. They agree in practice: a coincidence may open a story, and logic must close it.
- **Mystery or suspense?** McKee treats mystery as a full mode of its own. Highsmith dislikes puzzles and secrets, and Cron warns that withholding for a big reveal usually robs the story. Resolution: in mystery mode, withhold only what an honest investigator doesn't know yet, never what the viewpoint character already knows. Keep danger close, so the mystery is also suspense.
- **Liking the lead.** Tomlinson and Cron build suspense on affinity: readers want good outcomes for people they like. Highsmith says caring isn't liking, and a dark lead can fascinate. Resolution: make readers care. If the lead is unlikable, give him competence, wit and a few pleasant qualities, and give readers a lesser character to stand beside.
- **Justice at the end.** Highsmith finds the public's passion for justice artificial and likes criminals who go free. The market prefers punishment, and McKee wants an ending earned by its causes. Resolution: the ending follows the story's logic, not market justice. If the criminal goes free, let the reader feel the cost; the ending is ironic, not tidy.
- **Explaining motives.** Highsmith says a writer must know why characters act, but explaining too much can be artistically bad. Cron wants cause and effect visible. Resolution: the writer knows every why. The reader gets the actions and enough cause to follow them, and infers the rest.
