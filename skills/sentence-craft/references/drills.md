# Drills and Worked Examples

Use these for practice, for teaching, or to break a stuck passage open. The examples are original, written for this skill in the spirit of the book's own worked corrections.

## Drills

1. **Return after every period.** Retype a page of your own prose one sentence per line and look at the lengths. Then make three sentences shorter and one longer, and read the run aloud. [Klinkenborg]
2. **The deletion test.** Remove one word at a time from a sentence, including "the," "that" and "just," and read it after each removal. Keep a word only if losing it changes the meaning or breaks the rhythm. [Klinkenborg]
3. **Remove the logic words.** Delete every "but," "however," "so," "therefore" and "in fact" from a page. Restore only the ones whose absence makes the turn disappear. [Klinkenborg]
4. **Audition the first sentence.** Write fifteen first sentences for the same piece. Choose the one that promises most for the sentences after it, not the one that tries hardest. [Klinkenborg]
5. **Noticing without meaning.** Spend ten minutes noting what you actually see, without assigning significance, then write five sentences of pure description with the real names of things. Look up any name you don't know. [Klinkenborg]
6. **Reading like a writer.** Take a passage by a writer you admire and ask:
   - Does anything look peculiar?
   - Is there a phrasing you didn't expect, or a rhythm stronger than its surroundings?
   - How tightly are the sentences bound together?
   - Can you feel something going unsaid between them?
   - Which sentence works hardest?

   [Klinkenborg]
7. **Look it up.** Pick five familiar words from your draft and read their dictionary entries: origin, pronunciation, and how they differ from near-synonyms. [McPhee, Draft No. 4]

## Worked examples

Each one shows before → after, and why.

**1. The noun that should be a verb**

- Before: *His gaze was fixed on the ground, with an occasional glance at the ridge for a brief inspection.*
- After: *He watched the ground. Now and then he glanced up at the ridge.*
- Why: "Gaze," "glance" and "inspection" were verbs frozen into nouns. "Was fixed" was the only verb, and a weak one. "With" was pretending to join two actions. [Klinkenborg]

**2. The "as" collision**

- Before: *As the crew filed off the transport, they moved into the hangar.*
- After: *The crew filed off the transport and into the hangar.*
- Why: "As" forced two moments into one. Put the events in order and use one verb. [Klinkenborg]

**3. The volunteer sentence**

- Before: *Little did she know that her life was about to change forever.*
- After: *She signed the transfer without reading the second page.*
- Why: The first arrived pre-shaped; it's someone else's thinking. The second is a specific act that implies the change without announcing it. [Klinkenborg]

**4. The throat-clear**

- Before: *It was on a cold morning in the eastern district that Kade first saw the tower.*
- After: *Kade first saw the tower on a cold morning in the eastern district.*
- Why: "It was … that" delays the subject. Now the sentence starts with its actor and ends on the place. [Klinkenborg]

**5. The strained stand-in**

- Before: *The archive resides in the lower levels and constitutes the city's only record.*
- After: *The archive is in the lower levels. It is the city's only record.*
- Why: "Resides" and "constitutes" were dodging "is." Plain "is," used twice with purpose, reads as more certain. [Klinkenborg]

**6. The hedged claim**

- Before: *She was not very happy about the decision, which seemed rather unfair.*
- After: *She hated the decision. It was unfair.*
- Why: "Not happy" becomes a positive statement. The qualifiers "very" and "rather" come out, and the claim gains authority. [S&W R15]
