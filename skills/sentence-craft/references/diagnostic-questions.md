# Diagnostic Questions

Put the passage one sentence per line, then run these questions down the list. For each sentence that fails a question, note the question number and a rewrite.

## The per-sentence questions

These questions all come from Klinkenborg. [Klinkenborg]

1. Does it begin with its subject? Or does it open on a phrase, or on "When," "Since," "While," "Because"?
2. Does it begin with "There is/are" or "It is/was"? Those openers push the real subject out of the way.
3. Are the nouns the names of things, or abstractions ending in -ion, -ity, -ment?
4. Is the subject an actor that can actually perform the verb?
5. How far apart are the subject and the verb? What is in the way?
6. How many "to be" verbs are there?
7. Are the verbs active and energetic, or do they merely connect and present ("is," "has," "features," "constitutes")?
8. Any passives? Who is the hidden actor?
9. Is "as" in it? Which sense: "because," "while," or "like"? It usually forces two things to happen at once when they don't.
10. Is "with" pretending to be a conjunction ("She left, with the door slamming")?
11. Is there unintended repetition of a word or sound?
12. Is everything next to what it should be next to? Does the opening modifier modify the subject?
13. Could it be broken into two or three sentences?
14. Can you imagine saying it aloud to someone? If not, what exactly are you trying to say?
15. What does it imply? Does the implication help, or pull against the sentence before it?

## Signals of trouble

These signals come from Klinkenborg's own worked corrections. [Klinkenborg]

- **A verb turned into a noun.** "An appreciation of," "a brief inspection," "an occasional glance." Turn it back into the verb.
- **A sentence dodging "is."** "Resides," "constitutes," "is situated." Plain "is" is usually better than a strained stand-in.
- **"It was noticed that…"** Tear the sentence down and rebuild it with the real actor as subject.
- **Frequency pinned on an object.** "The occasional car passed" makes the car occasional. Let the time word stand on its own: "Now and then a car passed."
- **Empty modifiers** ("noble," "vibrant," "robust") and summary nouns ("array," "an assortment of") hide the actual things. Name them.
- **Hyperbole reaching for humor** almost never succeeds. Save your strongest words for what really is strongest.
- **Descriptions of physical action you can't picture.** Readers take in action with their bodies too, so write each move so it can be pictured in order.

## Revise toward

Brevity, directness, simplicity, clarity, rhythm, literalness, implication, variation, silence, the names of the world, presence. [Klinkenborg]

## Two readers

Every reader is two readers. [Klinkenborg]

- The literal one takes every ambiguity the wrong way.
- The literate, curious one will follow anywhere if the prose is clear.

Check each sentence against the literal reader, and trust the curious one: don't over-narrate, over-describe, over-interpret or over-signal.

## Supporting checks

- Replace "the fact that" and its relatives: "the question as to whether" → "whether"; "the reason why is that" → "because." [S&W R17]
- Avoid a run of loose sentences, two clauses strung on "and," "but" or "which," one after another. The mechanical sameness sings the reader to sleep. [S&W R18]
- Express parallel ideas in parallel form. [S&W R19]
- Don't strengthen a statement with qualifiers ("very," "rather," "pretty," "little"). They drain it. [S&W V.8]
