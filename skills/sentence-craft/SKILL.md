# Sentence Craft

Working on prose one sentence at a time. It produces a **rewritten passage with a short note per changed sentence**: what the old one said, what the new one says, and why. On longer work, it produces a diagnostic table (see `references/diagnostic-questions.md`). Never a vague "tightened for flow."

**Important:** never quote or reproduce text from the source books. Paraphrase the concepts and work on the person's own sentences.

## The core

- Know what each sentence says, what it doesn't say, and what it implies. The hardest of the three is knowing what it actually says. [Klinkenborg]
- The writer's job is making sentences, then fixing, cutting and arranging them. Most bad writing is sentences that don't say what the writer thinks they say. [Klinkenborg]
- Work from the sentence up. Shape and structure clarify themselves once the sentences are clear. [Klinkenborg]
- Start with short sentences, short enough to feel the differences in their length. Write longer ones only when you have a real reason and they stay as clear as short ones. [Klinkenborg]
- Each sentence comes on stage, says its piece and leaves. It doesn't lean on the next sentence or comment on itself. [Klinkenborg]
- A strong long sentence is strong short sentences joined. [Klinkenborg]
  - Weak long sentences are propped up with "with" and "as."
  - They run short on verbs.
  - They trail floating phrases.
  - They forget by the end how they began.
- Short sentences only sound choppy when they all sound the same. Think in rhythm across two or three sentences, and use first and last positions for weight. Prose lives on variation. [Klinkenborg]
- Keep the space between a period and the next subject empty. No preamble, no warm-up clause. [Klinkenborg]
- Treat every word as removable until it earns its place. Take words out one at a time and listen, even "the." [Klinkenborg]
- Fewer extraneous words leave room for implication, which means saying more than the words literally say. The longer the sentence, the less it can imply. [Klinkenborg]
- No sentence can afford to be only a transition. If you know what you've said and implied, the reader won't get lost. [Klinkenborg]
- Distrust "in fact," "indeed," "therefore," "moreover," "however," "thus." They insist on logic whether or not it's there. Remove "but" and see if the turn still holds. [Klinkenborg]

## Noticing and naming

- What you notice is a sign of what matters. Authority comes from noticing and from clarity, not from the size of the subject. [Klinkenborg]
- Get the words close to the thing itself. The world is densely named, so learn the real names. Inert verbs acting on faceless nouns drain the life out of a sentence. [Klinkenborg]
- Name the exact thing and the exact motion. A spike, not a big nail; "pads across the room," not "moves slowly." [McKee ch18]

## Against the easy sentence

- Beware the volunteer sentence, the one that arrives already shaped in phrasing you've heard before. It's nearly always unacceptable. A cliché is secondhand thinking, and irony doesn't fix it. [Klinkenborg]
- A true metaphor is a sudden, forceful act of renaming, and the correspondence must be literally accurate. The less often you use metaphor, the stronger it is. Extending one into an analogy kills it. [Klinkenborg]
- "Flow" is what the reader feels, not what the writer does. "Natural" is an effect, not a method. Talking is natural; writing isn't. [Klinkenborg]
- The narrator is a role you play. Ask "who am I to this reader?" before worrying about "style" or "voice." Style emerges from the pursuit of clarity. [Klinkenborg]

## Habits of mind

- Grammar is a tool, not a threat to creativity. Knowing the parts of speech, transitive and intransitive verbs, active and passive voice, and what a pronoun refers to lets you see what a sentence is actually doing. [Klinkenborg]
- "Writer's block" is usually something else: lost confidence, not thinking, not preparing, not reading enough, haste, or fear of the audience. Name which one it is. [Klinkenborg]
- When something works, break it on purpose to find out what comes next. [Klinkenborg]

## How to use this in practice

1. **One sentence per line.** Break the passage so each sentence sits on its own line. Length and sameness become visible at once. [Klinkenborg]
2. **Interrogate each sentence** with the questions in `references/diagnostic-questions.md`: subject, verb, the distance between them, "to be," passives, "with" and "as," openers. [Klinkenborg]
3. **Read it aloud,** plainly, to an imagined listener who is you and not you. Stop wherever the ear is disturbed. That's the most useful evidence you'll get. [Klinkenborg]
4. **Rewrite. Don't rearrange.** Don't try to fix a sentence using only its existing words. Imagine it again. A better sentence often hides inside the bad one. [Klinkenborg]
5. **Compose in your head.** Imagine the whole sentence, and the next, before writing it down. Write the sentence that *arises from* the one before, not merely the one that *follows* it. [Klinkenborg]
6. **Reassemble** into paragraphs and read for rhythm across the run.
7. **Report** each change: old → new, and the reason in a few words.

## Supporting rules

- Prefer the active voice. It's usually more direct and vigorous, and making a sentence stronger usually makes it shorter. [S&W R14]
- Say what is, in positive form. "Forgot" beats "did not remember." A sentence that hedges everything has no authority. [S&W R15]
- Prefer the specific to the general, the definite to the vague, the concrete to the abstract. [S&W R16]
- Omit needless words. That doesn't mean every sentence is short. It means every word does work. [S&W R17]
- Keep related words together: a modifier beside what it modifies, a subject near its verb. [S&W R20]
- Put the emphatic word at the end of the sentence, where the stress naturally falls. [S&W R22]
- Write with nouns and verbs. No adjective can rescue a weak or wrong noun. [S&W V.4]
- A noun and a verb make a sentence. When a sentence tangles, fall back on simple ones. [King, Toolbox]
- Use the words you actually have, and use them plainly. A word that is only a cousin of the one you meant makes the meaning worse. [King, Toolbox]
- Adverbs rarely help. If the context already shows how he closed the door, "firmly" is noise. [King, Toolbox]
- Settle doubtful words with a dictionary, not a thesaurus. The definition of the word you have often points to the word you want. [McPhee, Draft No. 4]

## Where the books disagree

- **The unit of writing.** Klinkenborg treats the sentence as the unit and builds up from it. King treats the paragraph as the basic unit, the place where coherence begins. Strunk makes the paragraph the unit of composition. Resolution used here: this skill works at sentence level; paragraph shape is checked in the last step, reassembly, and in `style-audit`.
- **Draft fast, or finish each sentence?** King and McPhee say blurt the first draft and fix it later. Klinkenborg says accept no provisional sentences. Resolution: when drafting inside `story-forge`, keep moving at the level of scene and event, but write real sentences as you go. This skill is the full sentence-by-sentence pass, applied in revision.
- **Rules or experiments?** Strunk & White state rules. Klinkenborg says there are only experiments to test on your own sentences. Resolution: the rules are hypotheses to test by ear. Keep a rule-breaking sentence when reading it aloud proves it better. King keeps an adverb when it's doing real work.
- **Fragments.** Strunk warns against breaking sentences in two by accident. King allows the well-turned fragment for pace. Resolution: a fragment has to be deliberate, and justified by rhythm.
