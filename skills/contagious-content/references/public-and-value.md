# Public and Practical Value

## Making it visible

- People copy what they see others doing, especially when unsure, and assume a busy place must be good. [Berger ch4]
- Social proof can push people the wrong way too: patients refused kidneys others had already turned down. Handle visible signals with care. [Berger ch4]
- You can't imitate what you can't see. Students who privately disliked heavy drinking drank anyway because the drinking was public and the doubts were not. [Berger ch4]
- Visible products are copied and discussed more. A neighbour's car influences your next car; their toothpaste doesn't. [Berger ch4]
- Turn the logo so onlookers see it the right way up. Design for the people watching as well as the user. [Berger ch4]
- Make the private public. A month of moustaches turned a quiet donation into a walking advertisement that starts conversations. [Berger ch4]
- Build products that advertise themselves: a signature line on every email sent, a distinctive colour of earphone or sole, a shape or sound you know at once. [Berger ch4]
- Leave behavioural residue that lasts after the moment of use: a wristband, a sticker, a reusable bag, a review, an automatic post about what someone is listening to. [Berger ch4]
- Pick giveaways people use in public, not ones that stay in a drawer or a bathroom. [Berger ch4]
- Don't advertise how many people do the thing you want to stop. Saying many people steal or pirate makes it look normal; state what people should do instead. [Berger ch4]
- A logo on someone's laptop or phone is the clearest sign they feel they belong to the brand. [Schaefer ch10]
- Put the logo or slogan on the image itself, because a scrolling viewer sees the picture and not who posted it. [Vaynerchuk R3]

## Practical value

- People pass on useful information to help each other, the modern version of neighbours raising a barn together. [Berger ch5]
- A simple how-to filmed in a kitchen spread widely among older viewers because it solved a real everyday nuisance. [Berger ch5]
- A deal is shared only when it seems remarkable. Big discounts beat small ones, and the size that counts is relative to what people expected to pay. [Berger ch5]
- Expectations set the reference point. A higher "was" price makes the same sale price look better, and the word "sale" alone can lift orders. [Berger ch5]
- The same saving matters less on a larger price. Ten off a small item feels like more than ten off a big one. [Berger ch5]
- Limits on time, quantity or who qualifies make a deal seem better, while a sale that never ends resets the reference price. [Berger ch5]
- For items under a hundred, show the discount as a percentage; above a hundred, show it as an amount. [Berger ch5]
- Make savings visible to others at the moment they happen. [Berger ch5]
- Package useful knowledge short and focused, one main item and a few links, so it's easy to forward. [Berger ch5]
- Narrow content is often shared more than broad content, because it reminds the reader of one particular friend who needs it. [Berger ch5]
- Check that useful advice is true before it spreads; false tips travel as fast as true ones. [Berger ch5]
- Teaching content, like a short recipe video, gives people a way to help friends and look helpful, which is why it spreads. [Kane ch3]
- Utility is one of the three things people keep on their phones, next to people and escape, so useful content has a natural place in the feed. [Vaynerchuk R2]
- Put the price on a product image; it draws more interest than the image alone. [Vaynerchuk R5]
- Hand members things to share outside the community, such as samples, early access and experiences; sharing outside is the metric that matters most after sales. [Schaefer ch10]
- Specific numbers are believed and repeated more than round claims. [Sugarman ch19]
