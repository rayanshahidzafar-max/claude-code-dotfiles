# Social Currency

## Why people talk about some things

- A large share of conversation is about the speaker's own experiences and opinions, and disclosing them feels rewarding in itself; people will even give up a little money to do it. [Berger ch1]
- What people choose, wear and say signals who they are. They share what makes them look entertaining, clever or in the know, and keep quiet about what makes them look foolish. [Berger ch1]
- So give people a way to look good while they mention you: make the brand a prop in their own self-presentation. [Berger ch1]

## Inner remarkability

- Remarkable means worth remarking on: novel, surprising, extreme or simply interesting. Telling a remarkable thing makes the teller seem remarkable. [Berger ch1]
- Remarkable things get mentioned roughly twice as often as ordinary ones, and surprising articles and tweets get passed on more. [Berger ch1]
- Retold stories grow more remarkable with each telling, because tellers fill gaps in ways that flatter themselves. Give them a core worth inflating, not a lie to repeat. [Berger ch1]
- To find it, ask what the product does that nobody would think possible, or what result is more extreme than people imagine. [Berger ch1]
- Break a pattern people expect. A budget airline with roomy seats and free screens gets talked about because it isn't what cheap flights are like. [Berger ch1]
- Mystery and controversy invite talk, because people turn to others to settle what they can't work out alone. [Berger ch1]
- Anything can be made remarkable, even a toilet roll, if you find the angle that makes it stand out. [Berger ch1]
- A trivia fact hidden under a bottle cap gives every buyer something to tell the person next to them. [Berger ch1]
- Tie the remarkable thing to a hook point: what makes you different and why that matters to the audience, put in a line someone would stop for. [Kane ch3]

## Game mechanics

- People love visible progress and they care where they stand relative to others; many will pick less in absolute terms to be ahead of their peers. [Berger ch1]
- Track achievement where none was tracked before, as airlines did with miles, so people have something to chase and show off. [Berger ch1]
- Make the achievement public with a badge, a level or a mark others can see, because status nobody knows about is worth little. [Berger ch1]
- Keep the status system easy to read. Gold, silver and bronze are understood at a glance; a dozen gemstone tiers confuse everyone. [Berger ch1]
- Contests, awards and public votes turn entrants into marketers, since winners boast and contestants campaign for votes, naming the sponsor each time. [Berger ch1]
- Name something after a customer, feature their photo or put them on stage, and they'll tell everyone they know. [Berger ch1]
- In a community, give members status for helping, and promote the most active into visible roles. [Schaefer ch9]

## Insiders

- Scarcity (limited amount, time or place) and exclusivity (limited to people who know or are invited) make people feel like insiders, and insiders talk. [Berger ch1]
- A hidden entrance, an invitation-only sale or an item that appears and vanishes can make an ordinary product sought after. [Berger ch1]
- Make access hard but not impossible, and relax limits once demand exists. When you must refuse someone, offer an alternative in the same breath. [Berger ch1]
- Give early or exclusive access to the people most likely to talk. [Schaefer ch10]

## Motivation

- Nobody needs payment to pass on something they're proud of. Paying for referrals can replace their own reasons with a price, after which the buzz tracks the cash. [Berger ch1]
- Social rewards such as status, recognition and bragging rights last longer and cost less than cash. [Berger ch1]
- The rewards that work come as status, access, power or stuff, and they backfire when they're unrelated to what the product is for. [Ellis ch6]

## The rest of the room on social currency

- Give fans something to give their friends: make them the one who brought the laugh or touched a heart. [Kane ch5]
- Don't ask people outright to tag friends. Give them a reason, like tagging the friend who'd be most scared by the video. [Kane ch5]
- Content counts as cool when it hits an emotional centre hard enough that people must share it, and when sharing it says something about them. [Vaynerchuk R2]
- People on social platforms want entertainment, information, connection and a chance to brag. Feed one of those before you ask for anything. [Vaynerchuk R2]
- Sharing something about yourself is its own reward, so a share button on a personal moment gets used. [Eyal ch7]
- An incentive to invite works when it pays in the product's core value, as extra storage or travel credit did. [Ellis ch5]
- A surprise gesture to one stranger, like a handwritten note or a gift, can earn far more talk than a paid push. [Vaynerchuk R4]
