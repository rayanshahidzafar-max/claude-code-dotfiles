# Triggers

## Why boring things get talked about

- A breakfast cereal can out-talk a theme park, because people meet the cereal every morning and the park once a year. [Berger ch2]
- Interesting things earn a burst of talk right away but no more talk over the following weeks; things that are cued often keep getting mentioned. [Berger ch2]
- Products cued often in daily life drew roughly a sixth more conversation than rarely cued ones, and held on to it. [Berger ch2]
- Much conversation is small talk: people mention whatever is on their mind to fill the silence, not to impress. Being on their mind is half the battle. [Berger ch2]
- Cues in the environment shape action as well as talk: news about a planet lifted sales of a chocolate bar with the same name, and the country of the music playing changed which wine shoppers bought. [Berger ch2]
- A slogan tied to an everyday object beat a nicer generic slogan in changing behaviour, even though people said they disliked it. [Berger ch2]
- A song about a weekday got a surge of searches on that day every week, bad as it was. [Berger ch2]

## Choosing a trigger

- Test a slogan by asking how often the audience will meet its cue, not only how clever it is. A cave dweller is a funny image but rarely seen; a common greeting is heard every day. [Berger ch2]
- The longer the gap between seeing the message and acting, the more the message needs a trigger to bring it back. [Berger ch2]
- Frequency matters: a drink people have all year beats a drink they have only in winter, and weekends beat holidays. [Berger ch2]
- Strength matters too. A cue tied to dozens of things (like the colour red) triggers none of them strongly; pick a fresher, more specific link. [Berger ch2]
- Place the trigger near where the behaviour happens. A message seen far from where the action can be taken has to survive in memory. [Berger ch2]
- Consider the context: geography, season and time of day all change which cues people meet. [Berger ch2]
- Even a bad review can lift sales of something unknown, because it reminds people the thing exists. [Berger ch2]

## Growing the habitat

- Every product has a habitat of cues that bring it to mind. Grow it by pairing the product again and again with a common cue, the way a chocolate bar was paired with a coffee break. [Berger ch2]
- Pairing a lunch restaurant with dinner raised talk among people who had thought of it only for lunch. [Berger ch2]
- A rival's message can become your trigger if you parody it, so every time people see theirs they think of yours. [Berger ch2]
- Ride what people are already talking about: a trending topic, a holiday, a big event. Link to it through an unexpected but honest connection. [Vaynerchuk R4]
- Linking a new product to something familiar lowers the effort of understanding it, as calling a smoke alarm a nose on the ceiling did. [Sugarman ch19]
- Time an external cue to the moment the audience already feels the need, so the product arrives with the itch. [Eyal ch2]
- Map the moments in the audience's day when the need comes up, and put the cue there. [Eyal ch2]
- Owned triggers that people opt into (an app icon, a newsletter, a notification) bring them back again and again, while paid and press triggers fade fast. [Eyal ch2]
- Load the next trigger at the end of each use, such as a reply that is waiting or a calendar link, so the cue returns by itself. [Eyal ch5]
- Talk about what the audience is talking about, and switch topics when they switch. [Vaynerchuk R2]
- Entering a fad early can carry a product, but the link fades when the fad does. [Sugarman ch19]
