# Arcs Across Books

Design an arc that spans books the way you'd design a novel's main plot, with a beginning, a middle, an end, turning points and stakes. Then cut it into installments. [Tomlinson ch9]

## Kinds of throughline

- A subplot can run across two books (develop it to a turning point, finish it in the next), across a season, or across the whole series. The longer it runs, the more substantial it must be, with real turning points and high stakes. A minor thread can be plotted like a short story. [Tomlinson ch9]
- Throughlines that work: a series villain whose henchmen fall while the mastermind escapes; a romance stretched over three or more books; justice or revenge for an old wrong; a search for redemption; saving a business, a family firm or a way of life; a conspiracy uncovered one piece at a time. [Tomlinson ch9]
- Arcs for the lead to travel: romance, healing, self-knowledge, atonement, revenge, justice, truth, discovery, a fall from grace, growing up, forgiveness. [Rosett, Arcs]
- Subplots that link books: a love story (a triangle pulls hard but stalls easily), revenge, a town's economic revival, a new business, an environmental fight, a mentor who may save or betray, a hidden council pulling strings. [Rosett, Arcs]
- A season can have one big villain, plus smaller villains for single books whose defeats clear the way to the season's final confrontation. [Tomlinson ch9]

## Building an arc across books

1. **Choose the arc's plot,** at full novel scale or novella scale. [Tomlinson ch9]
2. **Choose its length.** Three or six books make good building blocks: readers get closure regularly, and you can stop early if the series struggles. [Tomlinson ch9]
3. **Place the beginning, middle and end.** Over three books, open the arc in book one, develop the middle in book two, and climax in book three, just before that book's own finale or inside it. The middle is usually the longest part and can span several books. Each book carries one episode of the arc with its own shape, and ends on something that turns: a revelation, a betrayal, a discovery. Keep a question about the arc alive in the reader's mind. [Tomlinson ch9]
4. **Recap as little as you can.** Years pass between books for you; a binge reader may finish them in a week. [Tomlinson ch9]

- For an arc that runs longer than three books, show its inciting incident in a full scene, even as a flashback once the response is underway. The trigger itself can be the mystery that carries the early books. [Tomlinson ch9]
- An arc has to earn its place. It must complicate the main plot, contradict its meaning, echo it, or set up a later main plot. A thread that merely runs alongside splits the book. [McKee ch9]
- When several threads climax in the same book, resolve the subplots inside the main climax if you can; otherwise resolve the least important first. [McKee ch13]

## The recurring antagonist

- The antagonist isn't in the way for fun. They want something of their own, and their goal excludes the lead's, so a step forward for one is a setback for the other. [Tomlinson ch12]
- Readers know the lead will probably win in the end; what they come for is the struggle. [Tomlinson ch12]
- **Serial approach.** The fight with one antagonist runs through every book as a major subplot. Each book moves it forward and some set the lead back. The villain may be only a shadow in the background for a book or two, but is always there. [Tomlinson ch12]
- **Episodic approach.** Standalone books in which the lead foils the antagonist's plan and catches a henchman while the mastermind escapes. Each book's new balance is temporary, because the real enemy is still out there. Book one can let the lead mistake the henchman for the top. [Tomlinson ch12]
- A recurring organization lets the stakes climb level by level, up to the mastermind. [Rosett, Types]
- An antagonist who mirrors the lead's own dark side lets the lead win through understanding. Seeing how the enemy thinks, the lead wins with a selfless move that a selfish enemy can't anticipate. [Tomlinson ch12]
- A lead and their story can only be as compelling as the forces against them. Scale the antagonism to the length of the arc. [McKee ch14]
- An enemy who was once a friend or family member hits harder than a stranger: shared history, knowledge of each other's weak points, old resentments that finally come out. [Tomlinson ch15]
- Revenge can run a series by working up the chain of everyone who took part in an old wrong, one per book, to the person who ordered it. Show that lawful justice failed, make the wrong grave enough to warrant it, and never let the punishment exceed the crime. [Tomlinson ch15]

## Relationship arcs

- A romance across books moves through recognizable stages: awkward first contact, learning about each other, falling in love, bonding as a couple, doubt, a power struggle, intimacy, commitment. A failing one drifts, stagnates, avoids, and ends. Decide how far along that list each book goes. [Tomlinson ch13]
- Progress isn't steady. Outside events and inner flaws stall or reverse it, and a relationship stalled at the flirting stage can supply tension for a long time without developing fully. [Tomlinson ch13]
- Outside the romance genre you can bring a couple together, break them apart, keep them apart for several books, and reunite them, or not. The genre's promise of a happy ending doesn't bind a subplot. [Tomlinson ch13]
- An odd-couple or buddy arc runs through antagonism, a thaw, common ground, trust, a setback, forced teamwork, a final test (often a betrayal, real or apparent), and friendship proven by sacrifice. It fits three to six books, not a whole long series. [Tomlinson ch14]
- Pacing a buddy arc across six books: book one brings them together (often only at the end); book two is the feud, with a thaw at the close; book three makes them grudging companions; book four builds trust until a betrayal breaks it; book five heals it; the finale proves it. [Tomlinson ch14]
- In a long series every relationship should change: enemies become allies, friends become rivals, families split, new members arrive. [Scheuerer ch11]
- Answering a will-they question kills the tension it supplied. If the series depends on that tension, have an equally strong source ready: a new relationship, a disruptive newcomer, a new question. [Tomlinson, Intro]

## Character development across books

- Not every lead needs an arc. A lead who doesn't change can run indefinitely and in any reading order, but risks feeling static now that readers expect change. A middle path is small moments of growth, with occasional backsliding. [Tomlinson ch11]
- When the lead doesn't change, a secondary character can carry the change. Keep the lead remarkable enough not to be upstaged. [Tomlinson ch11]
- In a flat arc, the lead already knows the truth and changes the people and the world around them. The secondary characters do the changing. [Rosett, Types]
- A full development arc runs from showing the flaw, through denial, admission, a failed attempt to change and a relapse with consequences, to integration. In a series you reach the end of it late, if at all. [Tomlinson ch11]
- Spread the growth across books, with missteps and relapses, the way real change happens. Show it in a subplot beneath each book's main plot, usually a slow-burn relationship, and let outside events push it both ways: some promote growth, others knock the lead back a stage so old behavior returns. [Tomlinson ch11]
- Ask the key question: what happens to the series if the lead changes? If the pleasure is watching a flawed hero win despite the flaw, full growth may end the series, and it might even leave the lead unable to do the job. Some series hit reset. The lead loses, or gives up, the relationship that was healing them, and readers keep reading in hope of a way back. [Tomlinson ch11]
- When two partners both have arcs, plot each one separately, then weave them together. Never let them move in step. One advances while the other slips, or one fears losing the other and sabotages the change. [Tomlinson ch11]
- A development arc has an end. Once it completes, the series needs new sources of tension. [Tomlinson ch11]
- Reveal backstory a piece at a time. Hint at a wound or a secret early and save the larger revelations for later books, and make each reveal raise a new question. The longer something is withheld, the bigger its payoff must be. [Tomlinson ch10]
- Plant any long-lost relative, old flame or hidden past before it walks on stage. One that appears with no earlier trace feels like a cheat. [Tomlinson ch10]
- If a buried trauma will power a dark moment late in the series, show enough of it earlier for that moment to feel set up. [Tomlinson ch10]
- True character shows in choices made under pressure, so the pressure must rise from book to book, or readers learn nothing new about the lead. [McKee ch5]
- Escalation has more than one direction. A series can widen outward, until the lead's choices shake a city or a state, or drive inward into the lead's closest relationships and private life. Alternate the two, so rising stakes never mean only bigger explosions. [McKee ch12]
- Know exactly when and why the lead's view of the world was knocked out of true. Every book's events should press on that spot. [Cron ch5]
