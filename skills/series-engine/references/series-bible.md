# The Series Bible

The series bible is the living record of a series as it's being written, the canon every later book is checked against. Start it from the series outline saved under a new name, and update it whenever a book changes something. [Tomlinson ch17]

## What goes in

A series bible holds the premise line, genre, tone and style, the backstory of the ongoing situation, the story world, the continuing characters, the locations, the themes, the engine, the hook, and the arcs and throughlines with their status. [Tomlinson ch17]

```
PREMISE LINE
GENRE, TONE & STYLE      viewpoint and tense, held across the series
BACKSTORY                how the ongoing situation came about
STORY WORLD              written for a stranger who wants to set a story here
CONTINUING CHARACTERS    profiles (below)
LOCATIONS                standing sets, maps, floor plans
THEMES                   the thematic argument, and the others
ENGINE                   what about the regular task generates each new book
HOOK
ARCS & THROUGHLINES      each with its status: opened (book), now at, pays off (book)
TIMELINE                 (below)
STYLE SHEET              spellings, invented terms, capitals, punctuation choices
```

- Keep it searchable with headings and a table of contents, keep it open beside the draft, and back it up. [Scheuerer ch10]
- Note which book introduced each detail ("eyes grey, book 1"). It saves hours of hunting later. [Scheuerer ch10]
- Keep a style sheet in the bible: spellings, invented words and how they're hyphenated, how thoughts are set, your dash and ellipsis choices. Give it to the editor so deliberate choices don't get "corrected". [Scheuerer ch10]
- Don't overbuild it before writing. Detailing things the series will never use is procrastination, and the bible can start almost empty. [Scheuerer ch10]
- Record every name the world uses (trades, tools, streets, titles, slang) and use them exactly, book after book. A world feels real when it is densely and consistently named. [Klinkenborg]
- The minimum for any series: names, descriptions, relationships, locations, and the rules of the world (magic, government, language). [Rosett, Troubleshooting]
- Already published, but no bible? Build one now: search and reread the books, or hand the job to an assistant or a devoted reader. Reread the earlier books before you start the next one. [Rosett, Troubleshooting]

## Tracking characters

- Keep one profile for every character who might appear in more than one book: names (full, nicknames, former names); appearance; personality and dominant impression; speech patterns and verbal tics; gestures; relationships (who has met whom, and how they get on in public and in private); backstory; significant dates; milestones (check that a character is old enough for their experience); wound, flaw and arc stage; and world-specific details such as powers, vehicle or rank. [Tomlinson ch17]
- Log the small encounters too. Two characters who once shared a train compartment can't meet as strangers six books later. After each book, search the manuscript for every name and record each encounter on both profiles. [Tomlinson ch17]

## Tracking places

- Track settings at every scale: the world map; the street plan, with where things are and how long it takes to get between them; floor plans of recurring buildings; décor; the mood at different times of day and in different seasons; each place's history and legends; and which characters are tied to which places. [Tomlinson ch17]
- Draw a simple map from the start, and add every invented street and shop as it appears. Twenty books in, that map will save you. Readers notice slips, and some know your town better than you do. [Tomlinson ch7]

## Timelines

- Track what happens when, including flashbacks, books that overlap in time, prequels whose publication order differs from story order, and spin-offs running in parallel. [Tomlinson ch17]
- Give every draft a reality check. Which events fall on which day? Nobody lives a forty-eight-hour day, and no character refers to something that hasn't happened yet, or that nobody has told them. [Tomlinson ch17]
- Track how long consequences last: healing times, grief, travel distances, recoveries that go in stages. When events depend on each other, keep their order intact as you move scenes around. [Tomlinson ch17]
- Keep three timelines: what really happened, what each major character believes happened, and what the reader believes at each point. [Cron ch12]
- For distant history in early books, prefer eras to exact dates. "The age of collapse" commits you to far less than "nine hundred and fifty-four years ago". [Scheuerer ch26]
- Check every fact the way a fact-checker would. Anything doubtful that can't be confirmed against the canon gets fixed or comes out. [McPhee, Checkpoints]

## Tracking open storylines

- Plot each thread separately, then mark where it crosses the main plot and the other threads. [Tomlinson ch17]
- Don't run too many open threads at once. Readers lose the plot, and so do you, and every book should still close something. [Tomlinson ch17]

## The series folder

```
stories/series/<series-slug>/
  series-outline.md   the pitch: premise line, engines, units, the first six books
  series-bible.md     the living canon above; every fact tagged with the book that set it
  loop-ledger.md      every plant and open question, with its payoff book (sequel-craft)
  reader-path.md      reading order, entry points, prequels, spin-offs, hooks (series-doctor)
stories/NNN-slug/     each installment, built by story-forge, with a SERIES block in 00-brief.md
```

- Keep one searchable file holding the full text of every finished book, so you can find a detail without rereading the series. [Tomlinson ch17]
- Back up bible files in plain, open formats. You may come back to the series in ten or twenty years. [Tomlinson ch17]
