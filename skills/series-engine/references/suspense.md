# Renewable Suspense

A series needs suspense at three scales: in every scene, in every book, and across books, so that readers wonder what the next book holds. Suspense comes from an unfinished sequence of events whose outcome could go either way. [Tomlinson ch16]

## Eight ways to renew it

1. **Leave something unresolved.** Open a situation in one book and close it in the next, then do it again. That chain of small links suits even an episodic series. Or run one situation across three books: set-up, complications, resolution. [Tomlinson ch16]
   - Readers recognize story patterns, and once a familiar pattern starts they wait in suspense for it to complete. Start one they know. [Tomlinson ch16]
2. **Keep the outcome uncertain.** Readers should picture both a hoped-for outcome and a dreaded one. Across books you can swing between them: make one look certain, then let an unexpected event knock its odds close to zero. [Tomlinson ch16]
   - An open question survives as long as it never reaches a crisis that forces a decision. A love triangle between two equally worthy suitors can run for decades; push toward a choice and it ends. [Tomlinson ch16]
3. **Tilt the odds.** Make the bad outcome likely and the good one improbable. Grow the threads readers respond to most, then make their hoped-for outcome look impossible. [Tomlinson ch16]
4. **Delay the outcome, then deliver it.** A promised confrontation must happen on stage. It is the obligatory scene, and skipping it betrays the promise. [McKee ch8]
   - When a long series question finally closes, plant its replacement first and let it take root before the old one resolves. [Tomlinson ch16]
5. **Make readers care.** Readers fear for people they like, and a series has years to build that bond. Sometimes readers should root against what the lead wants: a flawed hero who wants the wrong thing, or the wrong partner. With an ensemble, cut between two suspenseful threads and leave each at a turn. [Tomlinson ch16]
6. **Use dramatic irony.** Let readers know what the characters don't, like the bomb under the table or the lie that will surface. Third person allows it; first person makes it hard. Choose the viewpoint with that in mind, and if you switch viewpoint between books, switch early in the series. [Tomlinson ch16]
7. **Leave readers helpless.** Show a collision coming over several books, so readers watch it approach and can't warn anyone. [Tomlinson ch16]
8. **Raise the stakes that matter.** Across books, the stakes are mostly relationships. Bigger explosions and nastier deaths don't raise them. What raises them is someone readers care about at risk of losing what they love. At the climax of a season or a series, make the outward action stand for the inner stakes. [Tomlinson ch16]
   - If an arc won't resolve until the end of a season or the whole series, know its stakes and its answer before you start. You can't plant clues for an answer you don't have. [Tomlinson ch16]

## Supporting rules

- A reader can stand in three positions. In mystery they know less than the characters and feel curiosity; in suspense they know as much and feel curiosity and concern; in dramatic irony they know more and feel dread. Mix all three inside an overall design of suspense. [McKee ch16]
- Don't fake it. A false mystery (a fact withheld only to tease) and a cheap shock (surprise with no insight behind it) both cheat the reader. [McKee ch16]
- Every book has to make readers curious from its opening lines. The books before it can't do that work for it. [Cron ch1]
- Mention something briefly, mention it again later, and later still add a little more, until readers are desperate for the full story. Then deliver it in full. Across books, this stretches a single curiosity over a season. [McPhee, Structure]
- Every setup readers notice is a promise, and a visible trail of dots must lead from it to its payoff. [Cron ch10]
- What readers resent isn't the cliffhanger itself. It's the long wait, a payoff that doesn't honor the wait, and a series abandoned with the cliffhanger unresolved. Tie a cliffhanger to a new situation, never to the current book's main plot. [Tomlinson ch18]
- A book's final page is where readers decide about the next one. A read-through hook placed after the resolution gives a glimpse of the next adventure, and it only works if you know enough about that adventure to make the glimpse compelling. [Rosett, Hooks]
