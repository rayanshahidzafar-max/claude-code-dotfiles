# The Series Outline

The outline is the series' pitch document: the whole engine on a few pages, written before book one. It works as a pitch to a publisher and as a checklist for an independent author. When drafting starts, it becomes the series bible. [Tomlinson ch5]

## The seven engines

Most series run on one of seven engines, and the lasting ones often run on two. [Tomlinson ch4]

| Engine | How it generates books | Watch for |
|---|---|---|
| Case of the week | A world where new problems arrive routinely: a crime, a patient, a monster, a planet. | Closed books need a few open threads (a romance, a recurring villain) to pull readers on. |
| Extraordinary character, ordinary world | A larger-than-life outsider, or someone with a gift, disrupts an ordinary place. | The friction of not fitting in has to renew; don't let the outsider settle in too soon. |
| Ordinary character, extraordinary world | Someone ordinary lands in a strange world and must learn its rules. An underdog to root for. | Once they've mastered the world, find them a new edge to stand on. |
| A plan or quest | A tangible goal, and each book a step closer: escape, rescue, revenge, the way home. | A quest that succeeds ends the engine. Know what comes after. |
| Trapped | A situation nobody can leave: a family business, a duty, an island, an apocalypse. | The longing to get out is the pathos. Don't open the door all the way. |
| Relationships | Opposites bound together: lovers, an odd couple, a clashing ensemble, a long feud between equals. | Interest drops once the will-they question is answered. Have an equally strong replacement ready. |
| Character arc | A flawed lead whose growth unfolds slowly across books. | Readers may stop caring once the underdog has grown past a point. Growth must show in outward action. |

- If the premise runs on one engine, add a second: one to drive each book's main plot, and one to drive a relationship that runs through the series. [Tomlinson ch5]
- Combining two familiar ideas into a new hybrid is an old and reliable way to find a fresh premise. [Tomlinson ch4]

## The premise line

- A story's log line runs from beginning to end: when something happens, the lead does something, and something unexpected follows. A series premise line describes an ongoing situation instead: the regular task, and what keeps it difficult. [Tomlinson ch4]
- The strongest premise lines pair an open-ended situation with a character who doesn't fit it. That mismatch is the series' hook, and it is where the theme shows. [Tomlinson ch4]
- If you can't get the premise into a line or two, you don't have hold of the engine yet. A fifty-page bible is easy; a one-line premise is hard. [Tomlinson ch4]

Two invented examples of the shape:

- *A salvage pilot who can't afford to leave the frontier takes the jobs no licensed crew will touch, while the bounty on her head grows with every one she pulls off.* (Case of the week plus trapped.)
- *A forensic reader of dead machines settles cases under three overlapping legal codes, while the truth about her own rebuild keeps surfacing in the evidence.* (Case of the week plus character arc, with a mystery throughline.)

## The outline, section by section

```
SERIES TITLE:          a metaphor for the premise or theme, if you can find one
PREMISE LINE:
GENRE / SUB-GENRE:     and what its readers expect
TONE & STYLE:          mood, viewpoint, tense (held across the series)
SETTING:               the world, its social order, the 3–5 standing sets
ICONOGRAPHY:           what readers see in their heads, and on the covers
THEMATIC ARGUMENT:     value + action → consequence, and the counter-argument
ENGINE(S) & TYPE:      which engines; episodic, serial or hybrid
SERIES QUESTION:       if the series runs toward an end, the question and its answer
CAST:                  the core four to six: role, flaw, secret, series potential
HOOK:                  what no rival series has
EMOTIONAL STAKES:      what the lead stands to lose, tied to their deepest fear
AUDIENCE:              who reads this genre, at what stage of life, expecting what
WHY YOU:               your connection to this material
BOOKS:                 at least the first six: working title, log line, A-story,
                       B-story, where the long arcs turn
UNITS / SEASONS:       how the books group, and the arc that closes each group
EXITS:                 where the series could end cleanly if it had to
```

- A genre's conventions are promises. Know them before you design, keep them, and find a fresh way to deliver each one. [McKee ch4]
- Tone and iconography are genre conventions too. Readers know at once when a book doesn't sound or look like its genre. [Tomlinson ch5]
- Sketch the first six books before you write the first one: a log line for each, its A-story (the outward plot) and its B-story (a relationship or growth thread), and where the turning points of any long arc fall. Knowing later books is what lets you plant clues in earlier ones. [Tomlinson ch5]
- Know the audience. Genre and theme decide who reads the series, and their stage of life shapes what they expect from it. [Tomlinson ch5]
- Write down why you should be the one to write this series: a link between your life and the premise, the fear it touches, what excites you about it. A series takes years, readers can feel when the passion has gone, and without that connection you'll stall. [Tomlinson ch5]
- Choose the kind of story you love to read, not the one you think you ought to write. You'll be living in it for years. [King, On Writing]
- Name the emotional stakes: what drives the lead, why the task matters to them, and what they lose if they fail. Tie that loss to their deepest fear, then find the worst thing that could happen and make them face it. [Tomlinson ch5]
- Find the hook: a fresh element (a world, a trait, a mix of characters) that changes how the lead does the job, often a mismatch between personality and task. Work it into every book so it never feels like a gimmick bolted on. [Tomlinson ch5]
- Choose a series title that captures the premise or the theme, ideally as a metaphor. [Tomlinson ch5]
- Plan book titles early, to a pattern loose enough to stretch if the series grows. [Scheuerer ch27]

## Units and seasons

- An open-ended series needs a strong start and a middle that can run indefinitely. Plan where the major arcs start, end and overlap, and whether one thread runs through everything. A series of fixed length needs a beginning, middle and end that build to a finale. [Tomlinson ch5]
- Group the books into seasons of three or six, each with its own arc and payoff. A season break is a natural place to freshen the series with a new location or a new overarching subplot. Change too much, though, and readers who loved the formula feel betrayed. [Tomlinson ch5]
- Seed the next season's arc before the current season ends. If a season ends on a cliffhanger, have the next book ready soon, and know how the characters get out before you strand them. [Tomlinson ch5]
- Check for built-in limits. A premise tied to a fixed set (twelve signs, seven sins) runs out of stories, and so does a single escape that ends once it succeeds. Make sure the engine can run past its first season. [Tomlinson ch5]
- Know where the series ends before much of the beginning is written. The ending tells you what the early books have to lay down. [McPhee, Structure]
- Choose a design for the series and hold to it: foresee its shape and pursue that shape, and change the design only on purpose, never by drift. [S&W R12]
- Linked trilogies can carry a long series. Each trio has its own arc, an overarching arc runs across them, and the series can close cleanly after any trio. [Rosett, Patterns]
- For a long series, outline the first unit in detail and the later units as dot points (at least each book's inciting incident, midpoint and climax), so you don't sink months into a plan that will change. [Scheuerer ch7]
- Lay the series out as a blueprint: the series question across the top, one column per book, and notes for plot points, subplots and arcs, color-coded by thread. Step back and look for gaps, and for any book that's obviously weaker than the rest. [Scheuerer ch7]
