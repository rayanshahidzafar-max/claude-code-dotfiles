# Series Engine

A series is designed one level above a story. This skill builds the machine that keeps producing books. Its main output is a **series outline** (`references/series-outline.md`), which becomes the living **series bible** (`references/series-bible.md`) the day drafting starts. It also plans arcs across books (`references/arcs.md`) and keeps suspense renewing from book to book (`references/suspense.md`). It doesn't write the books: `sequel-craft` plans each installment, and `story-forge` runs the stations.

**Important:** never quote or reproduce text from the source books. Paraphrase the ideas and apply them to the person's own series, world and characters.

## The core ideas

- Think of a series as a machine for stories, a story engine, not one story: a situation built to keep producing them. Test it by asking whether it can produce at least three. [Tomlinson ch1]
- You are designing a beginning that opens onto many self-contained middles. They lean toward an ending without reaching it, until you decide to write the last book. [Tomlinson ch1]
- The craft inside each novel doesn't change, but the emphasis does. In a single story, plot and character carry the most weight. In a series, the world and the themes rise in importance, and what you design is an engine that produces plots, not a single plot. [Tomlinson ch5]
- The engine is a continuing character with a regular task, plus opposition that repeats in endlessly varied forms. It runs on conflict between characters, between characters and their world, and inside each book's plot, so a premise with no built-in friction runs dry. [Tomlinson ch4]
- A series premise is a situation that can be run again and again. Start, as with any story, from a what-if, put people in it, and watch how they try to get free. [King, On Writing]
- The well running dry is the main risk: the same story told again until readers notice. Stock the series generously from the start: more than one genre's pleasures, a broad world, many places to play. [Tomlinson, Intro]
- The other risk is characters frozen in place: all their development packed into one book, then a new problem each time that changes nobody. Across a long series, stakes rise and characters grow, slip back and deepen. [Tomlinson, Intro]
- Three books is the smallest series, because three makes a pattern. Even long series plan well in units of three, so a series that stops at three or six still feels whole. [Tomlinson, Intro]
- Each book must satisfy on its own, and threads from the larger story must still pull readers forward. Every design decision serves both demands. [Rosett, Why]

## How to use this in practice

1. **Write the premise line.** One or two sentences that describe the ongoing situation: who, their regular task, and the friction that keeps it alive. A premise line names no ending. That's what separates it from a story's log line, which runs from beginning to end. Write a log line for each planned book too. [Tomlinson ch4]
2. **Name the engine.** Pick from the seven in `references/series-outline.md`. If the premise runs on only one, combine it with a second; most lasting series do. [Tomlinson ch4]
3. **Choose the series type.** Episodic (every book closed), serial (every book a chapter of one story), or hybrid. Default to hybrid: each novel complete, with a few threads, usually relationships, running across books. Lean further toward serial only if the genre expects it and the books will come out close together. [Tomlinson ch2]
4. **Ask the series question.** If the series runs toward an end, state the one question the whole series answers, know the answer before book one, and test whether the question can carry this many books at the genre's usual length. [Scheuerer ch6]
5. **Cut it into units.** Plan in groups of three, or seasons of six, each with its own arc and payoff under the series arc. Seed the next unit's arc before the current one closes. [Tomlinson ch9]
6. **Map the arcs** across books with `references/arcs.md`: one or two long arcs (a series villain, a slow-burn relationship, a mystery), shorter arcs that close inside a unit, and each lead's development arc with its setbacks.
7. **Plan the suspense** with `references/suspense.md`: which questions stay open across which books, and what replaces each one when it closes.
8. **Build for series potential:** the cast, world and theme sections below.
9. **Decide the pilot.** Book one is the pilot episode (section below).
10. **Open the bible.** On the day drafting starts, the outline becomes the living document in `references/series-bible.md`. Log every new fact as you write. [Tomlinson ch17]

## Casting for the long haul

- Start with a core group of four to six characters and let the cast grow, but keep it to six or eight major characters early on. Three already gives you a triangle of loyalties and grudges, and every new regular multiplies the relationships you must track. One or two will catch fire, with you and with readers; give them more room. [Tomlinson ch10]
- A character has series potential if they can generate conflict with others and with their world, form every kind of relationship (friendship, romance, rivalry, feud), mentor someone, and step into a supporting role in someone else's subplot. The more jobs a character can do, the more books they can carry. [Tomlinson ch10]
- Build contrast into the cast on purpose. Different outlooks make conflict unavoidable; a cast that agrees has nothing to fight about. Tie each character's outlook to one side of the series' thematic argument. [Tomlinson ch10]
- Let the personality decide the voice. A fighter speaks short and direct, a carer reads the room, a thinker builds long sentences full of facts. Give every recurring character a tag of speech, look or habit that identifies them each time they come back. [Tomlinson ch10]
- The lead's two key relationships do the inner work. The antagonist embodies what the lead fears in themselves, and the partner (lover or friend) values what the lead is ashamed of. Cast both with that in mind. [Tomlinson ch10]
- Plant the secondary characters that later books will need, even if they barely matter now: a future partner, a future villain, a witness for book five. If you don't know yet, people the world with a varied pool and draw on it later. [Tomlinson ch10]
- Readers judge importance by the space a character gets, how often they appear, how others watch them, and whether people talk about them when they're absent. Use that to build a reputation over several books before a character finally walks on stage. [Tomlinson ch10]
- First appearances should leave one dominant impression. Introduce the lead alone and in action, and avoid résumés and police-file descriptions. [Tomlinson ch10]
- Don't introduce the whole cast at once; later books can add characters once readers know the core. [Scheuerer ch11]
- Every significant character needs a secret and a motive of their own: what they hope to gain and what they stand to lose. Secrets get found out, and that turns conflicts. [Scheuerer ch11]
- Each book still needs a lead with a clear goal. Without one, readers have no puzzle to work on and no direction to travel, however rich the series around it. [Cron ch4]

## A world with series potential

- The setting is part of the engine, so it has to keep producing stories. [Tomlinson ch7]
- Choose a setting with conflict built in: a hierarchy, insiders and outsiders, barriers that people feel trapped behind or need to defend. [Tomlinson ch5]
- Give the series three to five standing sets, the places readers come back to like a familiar holiday spot. Add more as the series grows, and damage a beloved one when you need a big moment. [Tomlinson ch7]
- Keep familiar places fresh with the time of day, the season, weather, festivals, visitors and threats to the place itself, like a closing factory, a flood, or a newcomer with money. [Tomlinson ch7]
- A confined setting needs a renewable supply of new people (a boomtown, a college, a resort, a port), or a steady run of crimes and crises starts to look absurd. [Rosett, Types]
- Make the setting specific, but not so confining that the series can't expand later. [Tomlinson ch7]
- Know the world better than any reader will. Clichés come from not knowing it, and a world you know completely can answer any question a later book asks. [McKee ch3]
- In later books, revisit known landmarks and hint at what happened there before, without spoiling earlier books for readers who come in out of order. [Tomlinson ch7]
- Plant small mysteries in the world: the house empty for decades, the ruin nobody rebuilt. They pull readers back, and they give later books something to open. [Tomlinson ch7]
- When a later season or a second series moves to a new region, consider a sibling culture: the same founders, a different environment, generations apart. Readers get a built-in comparison. [Diamond ch2]

## Theme as the series' spine

- In a series, decide the thematic argument while designing the premise, before any book is written. A standalone can let its theme surface in the draft, but in a series the theme is part of the engine. [Tomlinson ch8]
- State it as an argument (a value, an action, and the consequence it leads to), then design stories that test both sides. [Tomlinson ch8]
- The theme decides what's possible in the series' universe: whether the world is kind, indifferent or cruel. Fix that once, and every book obeys it. [Cron ch2]
- Setting and theme make separate books feel like one series, and characters are how you explore both. A strong setting and theme can survive a departing lead, and they make spin-offs possible. [Tomlinson ch8]
- Prefer dilemmas to easy choices. Don't resolve the lead's central dilemma happily in mid-series, because the engine needs it. Let each book force a new sacrifice, and hint that a resolution might come someday. [Tomlinson ch8]
- Prove the argument twice. The antagonist shows the vice at full size, and a subplot character shows a smaller version of it and pays the price. [Tomlinson ch8]
- Keep the genre's usual themes and add yours beside them, not in their place. Those themes have already proven they can carry a series. [Tomlinson ch8]
- The series' meaning is dramatized, never announced. One sentence names it in the bible, and it stays off the page. [McKee ch6]

## Book one is the pilot

- Plan the series before you plan book one: a world worth living in, characters worth following and an engine that works come first, and the first book's plot follows. Keep the premise line where you can see it while drafting. [Tomlinson ch18]
- Choose the pilot type. A premise (origin) story shows how the situation came about, often through a newcomer entering the world. A typical story starts mid-situation and sets everything up on the fly. A hybrid drops a newcomer into a typical adventure. The closer book one is to the series' usual flavor, the better. [Tomlinson ch18]
- An origin story can be too different from what follows. A dazzling origin makes the regular books feel flat, or the hero spends half the book becoming the hero. One fix is to open with the hero already in action and weave the origin in as a flashback. [Tomlinson ch18]
- The more serial the series, the more set-up book one needs; lean toward a premise or origin-heavy pilot when several long threads must start. [Tomlinson ch18]
- Book one must show the world, the themes, the engine, the main characters and the conflict-rich pairings, and it must start any long threads. Don't hold vital set-up back for book two; the engine will supply plenty of later plots. [Tomlinson ch18]
- Don't spend the whole series in book one. Put in what the set-up needs, then stop; an opener packed with events and subplots buries readers before the series starts. [Scheuerer ch20]
- The first book carries everything after it. Every later book loses some readers, and nobody reaches book two through a weak book one, so make it the best book you can, even if it will be free. [Tomlinson, Intro]
- Book one has to tell a complete story; it is the reader's proof that you can finish one. [Scheuerer ch9]

## The vocabulary of continuation

Use these terms exactly, so that plans and the canon stay unambiguous. [Tomlinson ch21]

- **Sequel:** continues the original directly, usually with the same characters. Canon.
- **Prequel:** written later and set earlier; its events are the original's backstory. Canon.
- **Sidequel (side story):** runs alongside the original and crosses it now and then. It holds what would have been a digression in the main book.
- **Spin-off:** the same world with a different focus, through new characters or a secondary character promoted to lead.
- **Crossover:** a character from one series appears in another.
- **Reboot:** a fresh start that discards earlier continuity. **Remake:** a faithful new version.
- **Canon:** the authorized continuity. Pastiche and fan fiction sit outside it.

## Where the books disagree

- **Plan the series or discover it?** Tomlinson designs the engine and the first six books' log lines up front, yet admits his best twist and a favorite character arrived mid-series, and he wished he had planted them earlier. Scheuerer outlines heavily, King discovers, and Rosett warns against planning so long you never write book one. Resolution used here: design the engine and the load-bearing facts up front. Those are the premise line, the series question, the stakes and answer of any arc longer than one book, and who must be planted early. Discover everything else in the drafts, and write ahead or batch-write so that late discoveries can be planted back into earlier books.
- **Theme first or theme found?** Tomlinson tells you to name a series' theme before writing, the opposite of his advice for standalones. King and McKee find meaning in the draft. Resolution: name the series' thematic argument in the bible, because it is part of the engine's design. Let each book's particular angle on it emerge, and sharpen that angle in the book's second draft.
- **Change the lead or keep them?** Tomlinson's leads who never change can run forever, but risk feeling static. Rosett offers a flat arc (the lead changes others) or a robust arc (the lead changes, toward an endpoint). Cron and McKee say story is change. Resolution: every book must change something for good: a relationship, a secondary character, the world, or the lead. Decide in the bible what changes in which unit, and ask what the series loses if the lead changes completely.
