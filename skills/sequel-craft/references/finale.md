# The Final Book

## Knowing when to end

- If you know it's time (you've reached the planned count, or the ending has arrived), trust that and end the series while you still love it. [Scheuerer ch16]
- If you're unsure because of fatigue, life, or poor sales, wrap up the current arc and move on for now. Linked trilogies make that possible. [Scheuerer ch16]

## Four ways to ruin a finale

1. **Rushed:** everything wraps up too fast, and readers get no time to feel it. Measure the final act against the first two. If it's much shorter, expand it, so the arcs resolve at a steady pace. [Scheuerer ch16]
2. **Abrupt:** no wind-down at all. An open or ambiguous ending is a choice; an incomplete one is a cheat. [Scheuerer ch16]
3. **Never-ending:** the writer can't let go, and the ending drags on until it dilutes everything the series built. [Scheuerer ch16]
4. **Convenient:** a rescue from nowhere. Foreshadowing, breadcrumbs and planning prevent it. [Scheuerer ch16]

- An ending decided by coincidence, or by a rescue from outside, tells readers that the characters' choices never mattered, and they resent it. [McKee ch16]

## Seven moves for a finale

1. **Meet the genre's ending conventions.** Know what the last books in your genre deliver (the happy ending, the final battle, justice done), and deliver it. [Scheuerer ch16]
2. **Close the loops.** Pay off the foreshadowing, finish the series arc and the subplots, answer the questions you raised, and give readers final moments with each character. Give readers' favorite a last scene, and stage the reunion they've been waiting for. [Scheuerer ch16]
3. **Leave something unsaid.** Don't cram everything into the last chapter. Leave readers room to imagine the characters' lives beyond the page. [Scheuerer ch16]
4. **Leave the door ajar.** Leave some ambiguity, an adventure still waiting, a secondary character who has grown, or corners of the world nobody has seen yet. More than one finished series has come back years later through a door its finale left open. [Scheuerer ch16]
5. **Make it emotive.** Let the final scenes carry the series' themes and finish the character arcs, with a glimmer of hope even in a sad ending. [Scheuerer ch16]
6. **Mirror the beginning.** End where it all started, or echo an early line or image, with the characters transformed. [Scheuerer ch16]
7. **Consider an epilogue.** Jump ahead to show the aftermath, a natural partner to a prologue, if your genre uses epilogues. [Scheuerer ch16]

## Getting it right

- Ask beta readers specific questions about the ending. Which questions are still unanswered? What feeling lingers? Is it satisfying without being too neat? [Scheuerer ch16]
- Put the most design effort into the series climax. Everything else in the series exists to serve that moment. [McKee ch5]
- Climax the subplots inside the main climax where you can. Then show how the climax ripples through the cast, and give readers a slow curtain. [McKee ch13]
- The last two big turning points should swing in opposite directions. A triumphant finish needs a dark turn just before it, and a tragic one needs a moment of hope just before the fall. [McKee ch9]
- A promise made across books, such as the confrontation or the reunion, must happen on the page. Readers waited years for it. [Tomlinson ch16]
- A finale can gather characters from earlier books through a ritual (a wedding, a funeral, a festival) and stage its climax there. [Tomlinson ch10]
- If the planned ending won't work, look back upstream. The ending may already be in the material, and the series may have finished before you thought. [McPhee, Structure]
- What you leave out, knowing it fully, strengthens what remains. Trust readers with the unwritten. [McPhee, Omission]

## Exit and expansion, planned from the start

- Plan for two outcomes from the beginning: a series that flops, and one that takes off. Build in a way out and a way to keep going. [Scheuerer ch15]
- Exits: arcs built as linked trilogies or duologies; books that are each a complete quest, so the series can stop after any of them; a small cast and few subplots, grown only if the series succeeds. [Scheuerer ch15]
- Expansion: weave in enough open loops and breadcrumbs to pick up later. When readers ask for more, a new threat, a new obstacle for a couple, a secondary character's own arc, or a jump of several years can restart the series. [Scheuerer ch15]
- Never abandon a series. If sales collapse, end it as well as you can in the next book. An unfinished series costs readers' trust in everything you write. [Scheuerer ch3]
- An exit isn't a failure. It gives you room to pivot and keep writing. [Scheuerer ch15]
