# Prequels

A prequel is written after the original and set before it. Its events are the original's backstory, and the original has already fixed much of what can happen.

## Choosing the moment

- The prequels readers want most tell the big events that the main books mention but never explain: a death, a failed mission, a terrible discovery, a villain's origin, the moment a lifelong dream began. [Scheuerer ch19]
- Choose life-changing moments, the ones that shaped the person readers meet in book one: a hated gift used once to save a friend, or a mentor's words the lead later recalls bitterly. [Scheuerer ch19]
- Decide how many prequels you'll write before you write the first. Knowing the number lets you choose the most pivotal moments, not the first ones that come to mind. [Scheuerer ch19]
- Plant the prequel in the main series. Book one mentions an event without telling it, and the prequel tells it. [Rosett, Marketing]
- Prequels can introduce the legends, places and people that later books need: the overarching threat seen up close for the first time, an antagonist's first appearance, a relationship already shifting before book one opens. [Scheuerer ch19]

## Whose story

- Feature the main series' lead where you can. A prequel centered on someone else can leave readers wanting that character's full story, and expecting the main series to be about them. [Scheuerer ch19]
- In a series with several viewpoint characters, each viewpoint lead can have a prequel of their own. [Scheuerer ch19]

## Making it work

- A prequel must be a complete story with a beginning, middle and end, never a sample chapter. It proves you can finish a story. [Scheuerer ch19]
- Match the main books' tone, viewpoint, tense and quality. A prequel makes a promise about the books it leads into. [Scheuerer ch19]
- Keep a set of prequels to consistent lengths. Longer pieces read as more valuable. [Scheuerer ch19]
- Plan the prequels against the canon. Build a timeline of the main series and of everything before it, then place each prequel on it. [Scheuerer ch19]
- When readers already know the outcome, the suspense comes from how and why: dread, and compassion for people who can't see what's coming. [McKee ch16]
- Nobody in a prequel can know what only later books reveal, and nobody can remember an event that hasn't happened yet. [Tomlinson ch23]
- People inside a chain of causes rarely see where it ends. Write prequel characters with the full confidence of people who don't know the outcome. [Diamond, Epilogue]
- A free "book zero" meant to draw readers into a series is still an ambassador for it. Give it the full quality of the series, because a free book that disappoints loses the whole series. [Tomlinson ch18]
- An origin doesn't always need its own book. It can sit inside book one as an extended flashback after an opening that shows the hero already in action. [Tomlinson ch18]
- A flashback works as its own small drama with a turning point, brought in only once readers want to know. A prequel follows the same rule at book length. [McKee ch15]

## Linking with prequels

- A set of prequels can carry readers between two series set in the same world, by spotlighting a place or a people from one series' finale in stories that lead into the next series. [Scheuerer ch19]
- Small threads can cross between series. A detail mentioned in passing in a prequel returns in the main series as part of a key memory, then takes a bigger role in the next series. [Scheuerer ch19]
