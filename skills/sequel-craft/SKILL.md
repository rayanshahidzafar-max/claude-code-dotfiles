# Sequel Craft

One installment at a time. This skill plans a single book in a series (a sequel, a middle book, a prequel or the finale) and keeps the series' promises in a **loop ledger**: every plant, clue and open question, with the book where it pays off. Its outputs are the installment plan (`references/installment-plan.md`), which goes into the story folder's brief, and the ledger (`references/loops.md`). `series-engine` designs the series around the book, and `story-forge` drafts it.

**Important:** never quote or reproduce text from the source books. Paraphrase the ideas and apply them to the person's own books.

## The core ideas

- What keeps a reader turning pages is a question they need answered. The writer's job is to keep supplying questions, clues and hints of what's coming. [Scheuerer ch12]
- Every book gives a complete experience of its own, with its own inciting incident, midpoint and climax, while it moves the series arc forward. [Scheuerer ch14]
- Each book settles a major question and, before its last page, raises the next one. [Scheuerer ch7]
- A series needs two kinds of arc: the series arc, resolved in the last book, and a book arc, resolved in each one. [Scheuerer ch6]
- To plant foreshadowing, breadcrumbs and open loops, you have to know what's coming. That matters most when each book is published before the next is written, because the earlier books are then fixed. [Scheuerer ch6]
- Start from the ending. Knowing where the series lands tells you what to lay down at the start and along the way. [Scheuerer ch6]
- A good installment leaves readers content with this book and impatient for the next. [Scheuerer ch9]
- Answer the book's main story question inside the book, and let subplots and series threads carry the pull to the next. [Rosett, Patterns]
- Each installment must still change something for good. Its climax turns a value absolutely and irreversibly, even while the series runs on. [McKee ch2]

## How to use this in practice

1. **Find the book on the blueprint.** Read the series outline and the bible, then write down the series question, the question this book answers, and the question it asks to lead into the next. [Scheuerer ch7]
2. **Read the ledger.** List what's due to pay off in this book, what stays open, and what new plants later books will need. [Scheuerer ch14]
3. **Ask what readers loved,** from reviews, beta readers, or your own best scenes in the earlier books. Bring it back bigger, with a twist that changes it. [Scheuerer ch14]
4. **Give the book its own spine:** an inciting incident, a midpoint and a climax. A book that only carries the series arc forward is the middle book everyone skips. [Scheuerer ch14]
5. **Raise the stakes, or swap them for different ones.** If they can't go higher, put something else at risk. [Scheuerer ch14]
6. **Break one of your own patterns.** Take a formula readers now expect and turn it at least once. [Scheuerer ch14]
7. **Move the arcs.** Decide where each character and relationship stands at the start of this book and at its end, who arrives, and who leaves. [Scheuerer ch14]
8. **Decide where to start and what to recap,** with "Opening a sequel" below.
9. **Choose the ending:** a soft cliffhanger by default (`references/loops.md`).
10. **Write the plan** into the brief's SERIES block (`references/installment-plan.md`), draft with `story-forge`, then update the ledger and the bible. [Scheuerer ch10]

## Opening a sequel

- Two common failures: pages of summary before anything happens, or an opening so far past the last book that readers feel lost. Start with an inciting incident, balance action against explanation, and make clear how much time has passed. [Scheuerer ch14]
- There are three ways to recap: a short "previously" page before chapter one, a cast list that says where each character stands now, or reminders woven into the opening chapters. Woven reminders are the most common. The longer the gap between releases, the more help readers need. [Scheuerer ch14]
- A woven reminder does several jobs at once. One sentence can say where the characters are, how much time has passed and what the last book cost them, and then the story moves on. [Scheuerer ch14]
- Reintroduce characters and past events through the lead's present situation and attitude: just enough, and no spoilers. [Rosett, Troubleshooting]
- Never reuse the last book's wording for a recap, a reintroduction or a familiar place, because binge readers hear the echo. For a longer recap, retell events through a different character's view (biased, mistaken, even lying), or let two characters argue over what happened. [Tomlinson ch9]
- Never have characters tell each other what both already know just to brief the reader. A plain sentence of narration beats a staged conversation. [Tomlinson ch3]
- Make backstory do work in the scene. Characters use what they know as weapons in the present conflict, and nothing gets explained unless the reader would be confused without it. [McKee ch15]
- Bring the past in gracefully, as a gesture or a habit in the present, never as a lecture. Every character has a past, and most of it doesn't belong in the book. [King, On Writing]
- A recap sentence gets the same care as any other sentence. One that only reports, in phrasing borrowed from the last book, is a volunteer sentence; rewrite it until it says something new. [Klinkenborg]
- The sequel's own inciting incident belongs on the page, as early as it can land with full force. [McKee ch8]
- Some readers start in the middle of a series. Every book must make sense to a newcomer and reward a returning reader. [Tomlinson ch3]

## The sequel checklist

- **A complete arc again.** A later book needs its own inciting incident, midpoint and climax, or it falls into middle-book syndrome. [Scheuerer ch14]
- **What readers loved, transformed.** Bring it back in a form they haven't seen. If book one's set piece was a deadly contest, the sequel's contest changes its rules or its contestants. [Scheuerer ch14]
- **Challenge expectations.** Break a pattern your own series set up. A formula of one victory per book is exactly the one to break, with a failure in mid-series. [Scheuerer ch14]
- **Overturn book one.** Turn earlier ideas upside down: the villain who turns out to be right, the hero who falls. [Scheuerer ch14]
- **Progress the arcs.** Later books show who the characters become: new obstacles, changed relationships, revealed truths. Give minor characters new jobs, and give characters who've lost their purpose a reason to go. [Scheuerer ch14]
- **Raise or change the stakes.** Every action has consequences, victories included. Ask what each challenge can win or cost, add a ticking clock, and go back to what happens if the characters fail. [Scheuerer ch14]
- **New faces.** New characters shake up the cast, and readers' favorites often arrive late in a series. Characters can also leave and come back. [Scheuerer ch14]
- **Payoffs.** Pay off earlier foreshadowing, or give it a partial payoff on the way to the finale. Close some loops and open new ones. [Scheuerer ch14]
- **Deaths that mean something.** A death lands only when readers have reason to care, so build the investment first. [Scheuerer ch14]
- **More of the world.** A sequel can open new territory: the next region, the next city, the next planet. [Scheuerer ch14]
- **Never retreat in magnitude.** Each book's central conflict must go beyond the last one's, in scale or in personal cost. Recycling an old size of trouble makes the middle of a series sag. [McKee ch9]
- **Trouble for the lead.** Whatever can go wrong for the lead should, and each attempt to fix it should make things worse. [Cron ch9]

## Endings, prequels and finales

- Default to a soft cliffhanger: the book's main arc closes, then a new loop or inciting incident opens on the last pages. The full rules, and the ledger, are in `references/loops.md`. [Scheuerer ch13]
- The prequels readers want most tell the big events the main books mention but never explain. When readers already know the outcome, suspense comes from how and why, not what. Method in `references/prequels.md`. [Scheuerer ch19]
- A finale closes the loops, resolves the foreshadowing and finishes the series arc, then leaves a little unsaid and the door open a crack. Method in `references/finale.md`. [Scheuerer ch16]

## Characters across installments

- In the first draft, nail down the logistics: where everyone goes, and why. Then layer in character detail in a dedicated pass, noting what later books must reference and which character arcs remain open. [Scheuerer ch11]
- Know each lead's arc type. It may be transformation (a false belief meets the truth), growth (they overcome something without becoming someone else) or a fall (a slide caused by bad choices). Map it across the books. [Scheuerer ch11]
- Give characters flaws and old wounds. A lead who never errs can't hold readers through three books. [Scheuerer ch11]
- Keep the lead driving the series. Writers fall for a secondary character, and leads drift into letting things happen to them. [Scheuerer ch11]
- Give every major character a part in the series ending. Ask what each one contributes to the final payoff, and cut anyone who contributes nothing. [Scheuerer ch11]
- Show growth through callbacks: a lesson from an earlier book that changes how the lead acts now. [Rosett, Troubleshooting]

## Where the books disagree

- **Cliffhangers.** Scheuerer allows hard cliffhangers when releases come fast, and at chapter ends. Tomlinson allows them with six months or less between books, as long as they're tied to a new situation. Rosett says to use them with caution, and McKee's climax must be absolute and irreversible. Resolution used here: every book answers its own main question with a real climax. The pull to the next book comes from a new question opened after that answer, a soft cliffhanger or read-through hook. A hard cliffhanger appears only at a chapter end, or when the next book is already written.
- **Outline or discover?** Scheuerer outlines every book down to the chapter so she can plant early. Her discovery writers plant by instinct and fix things by drafting ahead, and King discovers. Resolution: plan the load-bearing plants, anything a later reveal depends on. Don't publish an installment until the next one is drafted far enough to show what the earlier book must plant.
- **How much recap?** Scheuerer offers a recap page or a cast list; Tomlinson recaps as little as possible, in fresh words; McKee and Cron give readers only what they need, when they need it. Resolution: woven reminders by default, each doing double duty. Use a front-matter recap only after a long gap or with a big cast, and never paste it from the last book.
