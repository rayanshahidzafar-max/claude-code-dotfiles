# Title Craft

This skill titles things people read: books and their subtitles, chapters, series, articles, posts, videos, emails and listings. It produces the **title sheet** (`references/title-sheet.md`):
- the reader and their problems, questions, roadblocks and results;
- twenty or more candidates;
- a shortlist with reasons;
- the chosen title (with subtitle);
- the chapter titles;
- the series pattern, if there is one.

For names of businesses, products, characters and places, use `name-craft`. Its SMILE and SCRATCH tests also apply to titles.

**Important:** never quote or reproduce text from the source books. Paraphrase the ideas, use your own examples, and apply them to the person's work.

## The core ideas

- The title is the most important words you will write. Most people read only the title, and a strong title over ordinary content beats great content under a weak one, because it gets read at all. [Edwards S6]
- A title has one job: stop the right people and get them to start reading or watching. Nothing else can work until it does. [Edwards S6]
- Great titles connect emotionally with the ideal reader, usually through something they fear or something they want. [Edwards S6]
- Aim at the right reader and let the wrong ones pass. Fewer, better readers beat a crowd that was never going to care. [Edwards S6]
- Write from the reader's side. A title about the author's own journey sold nothing; the same product sold at once under a title promising the reader an advantage. [Edwards S4]
- People don't act without a reason why. Tie the title to something they care about: saving time, avoiding effort, escaping pain, being admired. Don't make it all about money. [Edwards S3]
- Titles follow shapes you can model. Keep a swipe file of titles that grabbed you, from any field, and adapt their structure, never their words. [Edwards S6]
- Spend real time on it, sometimes half the time the whole piece takes. The title is never an afterthought. [Edwards S6]
- Curiosity pulls people in, but it must be about the real content. Curiosity unrelated to what follows is bait and switch, and it makes people angry. [Edwards, Q&A]
- To title something is to name it. Point at something solid in the work (a character, a place, the subject, the theme or the genre), and the best titles point at more than one. [McKee ch18]
- A title, like a lead, is a promise and a flashlight down into the piece. It must never promise what doesn't follow. [McPhee, Structure]
- A title plants a preconception that colours the whole reading. Use that on purpose. [Uncanny, McKnight Hardy]

## How to use this in practice

1. **Name the job:** book title, subtitle, chapter titles, series name and pattern, article or post title, subject line or listing. Is it fiction or nonfiction?
2. **Find the one reader.** Write down their problems, questions, roadblocks and hoped-for results, in their own words. [Edwards S8]
3. **Research what already works:** the bestselling titles in the category, their tables of contents, their back covers and their reviews. [Edwards S12]
4. **Match their awareness.** Readers who know only the problem need the problem; readers looking for a solution need the solution; readers who already know you need the thing by name. [Edwards S7]
5. **Generate twenty or more.** Use templates, the promise formula, hooks and the swipe file. The good ones arrive after the obvious ones, so write past them. [Edwards S9]
6. **Cut hard.** Is there a clear benefit or promise that draws the right reader? Is it about them? Is it true? Then run `name-craft`'s SMILE and SCRATCH for memorability. [Edwards S23]
7. **Pair the title and subtitle** for nonfiction. → `references/promise-titles.md` [Edwards S22]
8. **Title the chapters** as curiosity bullets (nonfiction) or by a pattern that holds (fiction). → `references/chapter-titles.md` [Edwards S9]
9. **Read the skim path.** Title, subtitle and chapter titles alone should carry the whole pitch to someone who reads nothing else. [Edwards S31]
10. **Test where the platform allows:** ads, emails, listing subtitles. Change one thing at a time. [Edwards S11]
11. **Fill in the title sheet** and record why the winner won.

## Nonfiction: titles that promise

- The promise is what sells a book or product most, and it usually lives in the title and subtitle. It has four parts (the hurdle, the prize, the timing and the eliminator), set out in `references/promise-titles.md`. [Edwards S22]
- Verbs make pictures. "How to plant and harvest a kitchen garden" is easier to see than "how to become a gardener". [Edwards, Q&A]
- Odd numbers (3, 5, 7) tend to feel more credible in list titles. [Edwards S6]
- Don't overstate. One inflated superlative makes the reader doubt everything else on the cover. [S&W V.7]

## Fiction: titles that name something solid

- A fiction title names something real in the story (a character, a place, an object, the theme) rather than selling a result. → `references/fiction-titles.md` [McKee ch18]
- A title positions its audience. It tells them what kind of story to expect, and a title that promises the wrong genre leaves readers waiting for something that never comes. [McKee ch4]

## Series titles

- Decide the series name and the book-title pattern early, and choose a pattern that can stretch. → `references/series-titles.md` [Scheuerer ch27]

## Where the books disagree

- **Hype or plain?** Edwards writes promises in capitals with "outrageously" and exclamation marks. S&W say never overstate, and the plain-style books agree. Resolution used here: keep Edwards' structure (hurdle, prize, timing, eliminator) and drop the hype words. A promise must be specific and supportable, never inflated.
- **Curiosity or clarity?** Edwards says curiosity drives the click and a hook exists only to make people curious. Watkins warns against mysterious names, and McKee rejects literary non-titles that hide what a film is. Resolution: a title may withhold the how, never the what. The reader must be able to say what the book is about, even if they don't yet know the secret.
- **Promise or name?** Edwards' formulas are for nonfiction, where the title sells a result. McKee, McPhee and the fiction writers name something solid in the story. Resolution: nonfiction carries the promise (often in the subtitle, with a short, vivid main title); fiction names a character, place, object, theme or genre, and leaves the hook for the tagline and blurb.
- **Test or judge?** Edwards tests every headline. Watkins says never poll people about a name. Resolution: test what's cheap to change (ads, emails, listing subtitles, post titles); judge a book title or series name against the brief, then commit.
- **Puns.** Watkins builds names from puns, and McPhee gave them up as too easy. Resolution: a pun in a title must say something true about the book. In fiction, ration them unless the book is comic.
- **Decide early or late?** Scheuerer now decides series and book titles at the outlining stage. King lets a working title stand until a better one turns up in the draft, and Edwards writes the sales copy before the product exists. Resolution: for nonfiction and series, draft the title and subtitle first as the blueprint, then revisit. For a single novel or story, keep a working title and choose the real one at the final polish.
