# Chapter Titles

## Nonfiction: chapters as bullets

- A list of chapter titles is a list of bullets, and bullets are the workhorses that build curiosity and carry the case after the headline has done its job. [Edwards S9]
- A feature names what something is; a benefit says what it does for the reader. "Templates" is a feature; "Templates that write your first draft for you" is a benefit. [Edwards S9]
- Go one step further to meaning: it does this, so you can do that, which means this good thing happens in your life. The meaning is where the emotion lives. [Edwards S9]
- A curiosity bullet can promise the payoff without giving away the answer, using the reader's own words for their problem. [Edwards S8]
- Write more than you need. For a dozen chapters, draft twenty-five titles and keep the best. The later ones come out stronger, as with any warmed-up muscle. [Edwards S9]
- Get a rough list down fast. A list on the page is far easier to improve than a blank one. [Edwards S19]
- A few excellent titles beat many dull ones. Cut or merge chapters whose titles can't be made to promise anything. [Edwards S9]
- Myths, misconceptions and mistakes make chapter titles that promise real value without spending the answer, and mistakes pull hardest, since everyone dreads making one. [Edwards S20]
- A contents list of benefit titles sells the book on its own. One sales letter listed what was in the book with page numbers so the contents felt solid. [Edwards S9]
- Read the tables of contents and indexes of the bestsellers in your field before you write your own. They show the questions readers bring. [Edwards S8]
- Turn the reader's real questions, the ones they type into search boxes and forums, into chapter titles that answer them. [Edwards S8]
- Short questions make strong titles, the way the best subject lines are short questions the right reader answers yes to. [Edwards S18]
- The contents are the book's skim path. A browser who reads only the title and the chapter list should still get the whole pitch. [Edwards S31]
- Keep the language consistent. The words of the title should reappear in the chapters, so the whole thing reads as one conversation. [Edwards, Q&A]

## Fiction: chapter titles that hold a pattern

- Plain numbers are clear and never wrong. Themed titles are more fun, but the theme must hold for every chapter. [Watkins ch1]
- Chapter titles can foreshadow, hinting at what's coming without saying how, the way one fantasy series titles its chapters. [Scheuerer ch12]
- A book can be built as an inventory, each chapter named for an object that carries part of the story. [Uncanny, Roberts]
- While outlining, title each sequence by its purpose (from no job to job, say) so you know why it's there. These working labels are for you, not the reader. [McKee ch2]
- Pick a pattern with room to grow, so a later chapter never breaks it. [Watkins ch2]

## Form

- Set book titles in italics and article or chapter titles in quotation marks in running text, and drop an initial "A" or "The" before a possessive. [S&W III]
