# Nonfiction Titles and Subtitles

## The promise formula

- **Hurdle:** the action the reader sees as hard, in their own verbs. The exact verb matters (drive, hit and strike a ball are different promises), and action words put a moving picture in the head. [Edwards S22]
- **Prize:** the result they want, in the words they use for it, including whoever they'd recognize as the standard. [Edwards S22]
- **Timing:** when they get it. Make it almost unbelievable but possible, so if they don't get there it's on them. It can be the time the reader needs to do it, or the time you need to teach it. [Edwards S22]
- **Eliminator:** take away the excuse with "even if..." or "without...". Never suggest the reader is to blame for not having the prize yet. [Edwards S22]
- **Superchargers:** a tight timeframe, or a cost comparison against something everyday. Use money promises with great care. [Edwards S22]
- The four parts don't have to come in order, and not every title needs all four, but each one you add answers a question the reader would otherwise ask. [Edwards S22]
- The shape: how to [hurdle] your [prize] in [timing] even if [eliminator]. [Edwards S22]

## Template families

- **How to:** how to get [result]; how to [result] in as little as [believable time]; and the same with "...even if [obstacle]". [Edwards S6]
- **How every [reader] can [result]:** names the reader and the result together. [Edwards S6]
- **Ways:** [odd number] quick ways to [result]; [number] ways to get [result] and avoid [pain]. List titles arouse curiosity about which ways they are. [Edwards S6]
- **Mistakes:** which of these [topic] mistakes will you make?; [topic] mistakes every [reader] needs to avoid. People learned at school to dread mistakes. [Edwards S6]
- **Warning:** what every [reader] needs to know about [topic]. A warning catches everyone's attention, so use it rarely and only when it's real, or it's the boy who cried wolf. [Edwards S6]
- **Solution and method:** the solution if you want [result]; my [named] method to [result]. [Edwards S6]
- Magazine covers at the checkout are a free school of title shapes. Study the structure and ignore the subject. [Edwards S6]
- **Questions** work when the reader would answer yes: one about a [pain] they're sick of, a [result] they'd like, or a [dream] they've long had. [Edwards S28]

## The cover as a headline package

- A headline package has a short line above that names the audience, the headline, and a subheadline that completes it. On a book cover that's the series line or tagline, the title and the subtitle. [Edwards S17]
- Let the main title be short and vivid and the subtitle carry the full promise. A meaningful long subtitle beats a short vague one. [Watkins ch1]
- See the title in context (thumbnail, spine, listing, a friend saying it aloud) before you decide. [Watkins ch6]

## Make it about them

- Find the reasons why: earning or keeping money, saving time, sparing effort, escaping pain, comfort, health, praise, love, status. Tie the title to the strongest one for this reader, and the subtitle to a second. [Edwards S3]
- Ask "so what?" of every feature until you reach what it means to the reader. Meaning carries the emotion, and the emotion sells. [Edwards S10]
- Readers buy the thing they want and rarely the thing they need. The title promises the want; the book delivers the need along with it. [Edwards S8]
- Picture the reader across a desk, step into their head, and ask what they would call a book that solved this. Titles often fall out of their answers. [Edwards S27]
- Mine competitors' reviews for your angle. If readers complain that the leading book is too academic, your title can promise the conversational version. [Edwards S12]
- A title can name the after-state, the life once the problem is gone, the way the before-after-bridge formula paints it. [Edwards S15]
- A buzzword lets readers off the hook. Go a level or two more concrete, from the category word to the specific struggle and its cost. [Edwards, Q&A]
- Go negative the right way. Meet readers inside the problem they already worry about; never attack a named person or company, only a method or a myth. [Edwards S24]
- Take a position. A title with a clear stand pulls its people close; one that tries to please everyone moves no one. [Edwards S21]

## Hooks

- A hook is an angle that creates intense curiosity. It doesn't sell or explain; it pulls people in and makes the book memorable. [Edwards S29]
- A hook is not a point of difference. The point of difference says why this book is different; the hook is a quick story that makes people curious about that difference. [Edwards S29]
- Look for the hidden story you take for granted: the unlikely author, the odd method, the surprising source. [Edwards S29]
- **Hook shapes:**
  - a one-sentence story;
  - an unlikely character plus a timeframe plus a result;
  - a result plus a timeframe minus the pain.
  - The story must be true. [Edwards S29]

## Keep it honest and clear

- Tell the truth and be ready to back every claim. A title that overpromises turns into a refund, a bad review or worse. [Edwards S17]
- Write the title and promise before the book. The copy becomes the blueprint, and you'll build the book to keep the promise instead of hedging it. [Edwards, Q&A]
- A confused mind says no. Use the reader's words, not your field's jargon, and test clarity on someone outside the field: can they say what the book is about from the title alone? [Edwards S13]
- The best titles don't feel like selling. They sound like a friend telling you something useful, in familiar words. [Edwards S1]
- An editor may refuse a bare subject as a title. A one-word topic is a label, not a title, and a small added phrase can turn the label into a place. [McPhee, Editors & Publisher]
