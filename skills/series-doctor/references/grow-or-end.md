# Grow or End

## Carry on: extending a series

- When a robust arc completes, the series needs a relaunch: a new arc, and often a new premise. A job offer, a new partnership or a reversal can flip the setup, so the hunted become the hunters. [Rosett, Extending]
- Find the logical extension: a bigger antagonist behind the first one, or a challenge that uses the lead's new skills. Don't loop the old arc. [Rosett, Extending]
- Weave in a new subplot: a romance, a secret identity, a quest, a career, an estranged relative, a deepening friendship. [Rosett, Extending]
- Wiping the slate is risky. Resurrecting the dead annoys readers who grieved, so bring back a character who was only off the page instead, and use memory loss carefully. A transformation or a life change (a reformed thief, a retired detective, a baby, a divorce) can reset a series honestly. [Rosett, Extending]
- Never take away what readers come for. A small-town series that moves to the city has lost its reason to exist. [Rosett, Extending]
- If the finished story gives the lead a usual task, the engine is still there. If it doesn't, hand the lead a fresh task: a new post, a mission, a quest. [Tomlinson ch20]
- Cornered with the original lead? Promote the most fascinating, strangest or most mysterious secondary character, as a hero, a partner, a villain, or a villain revealed as a hero. Or bring a new hero up against the old villain. [Tomlinson ch20]
- Keep the door open whenever the story allows. Leave a mystery in a character's past, a secondary character's fate unsettled, or doubt about whether the villain is really gone. [Tomlinson ch20]
- A longer series rides its own momentum. Many series find their readers just as a short run ends, and every new release sends readers back to book one. [Scheuerer ch17]
- Before starting a second series, study the first: what readers loved, what reviews keep complaining about, how its length landed, what it's compared to. Build on what worked instead of veering away from it. [Scheuerer ch18]

## Close it: ending a series

- Series have natural endpoints: a robust arc completes, or a life transition arrives, like a graduation, a wedding or a retirement. [Rosett, Ending]
- Signs it's time: you're bored, the ideas are too small to fill a novel, read-through keeps falling, and reviews say the series isn't what it used to be. [Rosett, Ending]
- Most fears about ending are mindset. A finished series is backlist that sells as a complete set, and loyalty to characters is real but survivable. Readers who want more are a sign of success, so give them somewhere to go next. [Rosett, Ending]
- Tell readers quietly (shift your focus, then confirm it in your newsletter) or openly, using the final book to hand readers on to the next series, for example through a crossover or a dual timeline. [Rosett, Ending]
- End with a book that closes the big open arcs. Readers burned by an abandoned series won't start your next one. [Tomlinson ch19]
- Give a series about three books before you judge it. Many readers wait until several books are out, and a later hit can send readers back to your earlier work. [Tomlinson ch19]
- Don't promise a twelve-book epic until the books are written. Keep the freedom to stop early, or to run past twelve. [Tomlinson ch19]

## Branch out: connecting series

- Linked series lower the barrier to trying your new work. You link groups of books, not single titles. [Rosett, Connecting]
- **Spin-off:** a secondary character gets their own book or series. [Rosett, Connecting]
- **Shared universe:** several series set in one world, often with secondary characters stepping up to become viewpoint characters. It gives readers several ways in, so nobody has to start with your weakest book. [Rosett, Connecting]
- Linking by place works across eras. A village can tie a present-day series to a historical one through a character who grew up there. [Rosett, Connecting]
- **Companions:** guides, short stories and interviews that sit alongside a series. [Rosett, Connecting]
- **Retellings:** a familiar story from a new angle, or from a revisionist point of view. Use only public-domain sources, bring a take nobody else has, and expect both a built-in audience and heavy competition. [Rosett, Connecting]
- **Crossovers:** the leads of two series meet, and both series gain readers. [Rosett, Marketing]
- Spin off once the original is established, at its end or between seasons, and tell existing readers when their series will return. [Tomlinson ch21]
- Plant the new series in the old one's last book or two, say with a character leaving on a new adventure, without stealing focus from the old series' climax. Or open the new series with the old characters handing over the baton. [Tomlinson ch21]
- The spin-off's own first book still needs every pilot element, because new readers may never have read the original. [Tomlinson ch21]
- Keep what readers loved most. If the spin-off changes tone, carry over a familiar element (a character, the world) to bridge the two. [Tomlinson ch21]
- A second-generation series hands the lead role to the original cast's grown children, with the originals as supporting characters. [Scheuerer ch21]
- Lead readers between series from inside the books: maps whose names leave room to grow, cameos by recurring characters, and the next series' lead appearing in this series' last book. Add a reading list and an author's note in the back matter. [Scheuerer ch23]
- One founding culture split across two environments and given time grows into two societies that mirror each other. Linked series in one world can use that built-in comparison. [Diamond ch2]
