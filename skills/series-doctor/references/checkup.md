# The Series Checkup

Run it on any series, planned or published. Every problem found gets a diagnosis and one prescribed fix.

```
SERIES:                  TYPE:                 PATTERN:
BOOKS SO FAR:            PLANNED:              GAP BETWEEN RELEASES:
LINKS:                   the arcs and subplots that cross books
OWN QUESTION ANSWERED?   book by book
LAST PAGES:              cliffhanger / read-through hook / closed, book by book
PROBLEMS → FIXES:
FUTURE:                  carry on / extend / end / branch out
```

## Technical problems

**Reintroductions.** New readers need to know who people are, and returning readers have forgotten. [Rosett, Troubleshooting]
- Give just enough, threaded through the lead's present situation and mood, never dropped in as a lump, and spoil nothing from earlier books. [Rosett, Troubleshooting]
- Don't reintroduce a recurring character in the same words twice; binge readers notice. [Tomlinson ch3]

**A cast that keeps growing.** [Rosett, Troubleshooting]
- Focus each book on a few characters. Not everyone needs to appear every time. [Rosett, Troubleshooting]
- Send characters off the page for a good reason (a job, college, travel, another planet), and bring them back when a story needs them. [Rosett, Troubleshooting]
- Give characters who've lost their purpose a reason to leave, and give minor characters new roles. [Scheuerer ch14]

**Facts that can't change.** [Rosett, Troubleshooting]
- Use generalities where specifics don't matter: "early twenties", "a few months back". [Rosett, Troubleshooting]
- Hold the world's rules fixed, and never break one for a plot point. [Rosett, Troubleshooting]
- Avoid technology details that will date the series. [Rosett, Troubleshooting]
- Think ahead about pets, children and siblings. Who walks the dog while the lead is away? Who minds the kids? Will readers want the sister to get her own book? [Rosett, Troubleshooting]
- When something does change (a move, a new job, a different home), explain it on the page. [Rosett, Troubleshooting]
- A rule invented early without a reason can make later books unrealistic. Question every firm rule before you put it in print. [Scheuerer ch26]
- An established rule binds the writer. The fantastic gets one leap away from reality, and after that must obey strict probability. [McKee ch2]
- References to current fashions, products and celebrities date quickly. Choose ones that will last. [McPhee, Frame of Reference]

**Losing track.** Open or rebuild the series bible with `series-engine`, which sets out the minimum contents and how to build a bible late. Batch writing also helps: drafting several books before releasing any keeps the world in your head and lets you fix earlier books before they're fixed in print. [Rosett, Batch Writing]

**A stuck lead.** [Rosett, Troubleshooting]
- Launch a new arc or subplot, or give the lead small arcs: a goal, a relationship, a change of status, a return to normal or a new normal, a transition. [Rosett, Troubleshooting]
- Call back to lessons from earlier books to show growth, like a lead who is more careful now after once trusting the wrong person. [Rosett, Troubleshooting]
- A small framing quest, like the search for the right gift, can carry a whole short installment. [Rosett, Troubleshooting]
- Ask what happens if the lead truly changes. If the series' pleasure is a flawed hero winning despite the flaw, complete growth may end it, so stage the change and let it slip back; growth with setbacks can run for many books. [Tomlinson ch11]

**A love triangle going nowhere.** Waffling forever bleeds readers, and choosing alienates one camp. [Rosett, Troubleshooting]
- Foreshadow the choice so it feels inevitable when it comes, or let readers vote. Either way, the choice can launch a new arc. [Rosett, Troubleshooting]
- A triangle can run for decades only if both suitors stay equally worthy and the story never forces a decision. Once you push toward a choice, you have to make it. [Tomlinson ch16]

**A weak book one.** [Rosett, Troubleshooting]
- You have three options. Rewrite it and republish it as a new edition. Or promote book two to the new book one, and turn the old book one into a prequel or bonus story. Or start a new series. [Rosett, Troubleshooting]
- A shared world gives readers other ways in, so they don't have to start with your weakest book. [Scheuerer ch22]

## Mindset problems

- Boredom is the top problem. Within what the genre expects, fight it with:
  - a new character with a twist, like a rival forced into partnership, never a recycled replacement for a departed favorite; [Rosett, Troubleshooting]
  - a secondary character's backstory, such as why the grump is always angry; [Rosett, Troubleshooting]
  - a theme book (a holiday, a trope you haven't used) or a twist on a classic trope; a tight limit often forces invention; [Rosett, Troubleshooting]
  - a new style or structure, such as a different tense, a different viewpoint, or the body on page one. [Rosett, Troubleshooting]
- Take stock of the characters and the plot. Have they gone flat? Have the breadcrumbs and open questions run out? Read the draft in a different format to see it fresh. [Scheuerer ch24]
- Check the outline. Has the book drifted off course, or is the outline itself strangling it? Pause the draft and rework the outline. [Scheuerer ch24]
- Stop forcing a stuck plot. Walk away and let the problem sit; one drastic event can untangle a knotted story. [King, On Writing]
- When the size of the job freezes you, finish book one and decide the rest afterward. Don't plan your way out of writing. [Rosett, Overwhelm]

## Mistakes worth checking for

- A middle book with no arc of its own can't stand up outside the series. Fix it in the structural edit, and plan to prevent it next time. [Scheuerer ch26]
- Hopping to a new genre or series mid-series splits your focus and frustrates the readers who are waiting. [Scheuerer ch26]
- A series stands or falls by its opposition. If the middle sags, look at the antagonism before you look at the hero. [McKee ch14]
