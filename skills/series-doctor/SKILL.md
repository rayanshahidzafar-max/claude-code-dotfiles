# Series Doctor

A checkup for a series that's planned, half written or long running. The doctor names what kind of series it is and how it's patterned, finds what's stuck and prescribes a fix, then decides what happens next: carry on, relaunch onto a new arc, end, or branch out into spin-offs, prequels and companions. It produces a **series checkup** (`references/checkup.md`) and the **reader path** (`references/reader-path.md`), the map of how readers move through everything set in this world.

**Important:** never quote or reproduce text from the source books. Paraphrase the ideas and apply them to the person's own series.

## The core ideas

- Every book must be complete and satisfying on its own, keeping its genre's promise about how stories end, while threads from a bigger story pull readers into the next one. That tension is the heart of series writing. [Rosett, Why]
- Readers come back for a promise they already enjoyed, delivered fresh each time. [Rosett, Why]
- Once a book is published, its facts are fixed, so every early detail is a commitment the later books must honor. [Rosett, Why]
- Think ahead about how the lead's life plays out over many books: children who grow up, jobs, homes, the friends who'd have to move whenever the lead moves. Choices that feel free in book one can be cages by book six. [Rosett, Introduction]
- Plan flexibly. With no plan, early accidents trap you, and a rigid plan breaks once the books are underway. Make a plan you're allowed to change. [Rosett, Introduction]
- A small, fixed setting piles up implausibility. A quiet village with a murder every month strains belief, so build in a reason for all that trouble, or move the story on. [Rosett, Introduction]
- The first book carries the series, even though later books are usually better written. [Rosett, Why]
- A series can overstay its welcome, and readers who love it won't automatically follow you to the next one. Plan both the ending and the handover. [Rosett, Why]
- Boredom is the series writer's main danger, and readers can feel it in the prose. [Rosett, Troubleshooting]

## How to use this in practice

1. **Name the type,** from the section below, and its built-in weakness. [Rosett, Types]
2. **Name the pattern:** duet, trilogy, linked trilogies, or open-ended. [Rosett, Patterns]
3. **Check the links between books.** Arcs and subplots tie the books together, so list them. Then check that each book still answers its own main question. [Rosett, Arcs]
4. **Check every last page.** Does it end on a cliffhanger or a read-through hook, and is the next book ready to honor it? [Rosett, Hooks]
5. **Run the troubleshooting list** in `references/checkup.md`, and prescribe one fix per problem.
6. **Decide the future** with `references/grow-or-end.md`: carry on, extend onto a new arc, end, or branch out.
7. **Draw the reader path** in `references/reader-path.md`: reading order, entry points, prequels, companions, hooks, bundles.

## The three types of series

**Multi-protagonist.** A new lead (or couple) in each book. The books are linked by family, a group of friends, a workplace, a place, or a shared theme or trope, and each stands alone in any order. [Rosett, Types]
- Secondary characters step up to lead later books. That keeps the series fresh, and readers can revisit earlier leads as supporting cast. [Rosett, Types]
- The premise can cap the number of books (three sisters, three books) unless you widen the circle with a long-lost sibling, a cousin, or the parents. [Rosett, Types]
- Subplots usually follow future leads or whatever binds the books together, and a few run through the whole series. [Rosett, Types]
- Each book costs more work, because each needs a new lead and a new arc. The type suits romance better than genres whose readers want one recurring hero. [Rosett, Types]

**Single lead, flat arc.** The lead is a catalyst who already knows the truth. They change the people and the world around them, and stay essentially the same. [Rosett, Types]
- The antagonist can be new in every book, or recurring. A recurring organization lets the lead climb its ranks book by book, all the way up to the mastermind. [Rosett, Types]
- The secondary characters do the changing, and readers relate to the series through them. [Rosett, Types]
- It suits long, open-ended series. The risks are a static feel, and a secondary character or the villain outshining the lead, so give the lead small arcs and challenges. [Rosett, Types]

**Single lead, robust arc.** The lead changes step by step across the books, each book a stepping stone, often against a nemesis who runs through the whole series. [Rosett, Types]
- Secondary characters get arcs of their own and serve as foils and mirrors. The subplots layer the series arc, the lead's arc, the secondaries' arcs, and the mysteries. [Rosett, Types]
- The arc sets the series' length. When it completes, the series has reached its natural end, and going further takes a new arc. [Rosett, Types]

Choose the type by what your genre expects, what appeals to you, and which drawbacks you can live with. [Rosett, Types]

## Patterns

- A duet tests an idea, a trilogy is the most popular shape, and an open-ended series runs as long as readers keep coming. [Rosett, Patterns]
- Answer every book's main story question inside that book, and let the subplots and series arcs carry the pull forward. [Rosett, Patterns]
- Linked trilogies give a long series checkpoints. Each trio closes its own arc under an overarching one, and the whole series can wrap up after any trio. [Rosett, Patterns]
- If you aren't sure you'll write the next book, close every loop. Many readers won't start a series until it's finished, and an abandoned thread breaks a promise. [Rosett, Patterns]

## Links and last pages

- Arcs and subplots are the strongest links between books. Nest a complete arc inside each book, within the larger arc of the series. [Rosett, Arcs]
- A robust arc can spread one hero's journey across the books: the call, the mentor, the trials, the abyss, the transformation, the final battle and the return. [Rosett, Arcs]
- In a flat-arc series the arcs belong to the secondary characters, and the lead gets small challenges so they never feel stuck. [Rosett, Arcs]
- A book's final page is where readers decide about the next one, so treat it as the most important page for the series. [Rosett, Hooks]
- Readers complain about cliffhangers and keep reading anyway. Use them carefully, and preferably when the next book is already out, on preorder, or close behind. [Rosett, Hooks]
- A read-through hook resolves the plot and then offers a taste of the next book: a letter, a job offer, a discovery. You need to know enough about the next book to make that taste compelling. [Rosett, Hooks]
- Whatever ends a book, the next must open with the same momentum. A spectacular ending followed by a flat opening wastes it. [Scheuerer ch13]

## Where the books disagree

- **Vague facts or concrete ones?** Rosett and Scheuerer advise keeping ages, dates and durations general, so later books stay free. Strunk & White, Klinkenborg and Cron demand the specific. Resolution used here: be specific in the scene and flexible in the canon. A sentence can be exact about what's in the room while staying loose on any fact a later book may need to move.
- **When to start the next thing.** Tomlinson waits to spin off until the original is established, at its end or between seasons. Scheuerer warns against hopping to a new series or genre mid-series. Rosett sees linked series as the best way to carry readers along. Resolution: finish or close the current unit first, then branch out, with the overlap planted in the old series' last book.
- **Should the lead change?** Rosett makes it a choice of type, flat or robust. Tomlinson warns that a fully changed lead may end the series, and Scheuerer says even unchanging leads must be tested. Resolution: choose the type on purpose. If a robust arc has run out, treat any further books as a relaunch with a new arc, not more of the same.
