# The Unnamed Thing and the Imminent Revelation

## Keep the revelation imminent

- The aesthetic heart of the best supernatural stories is a revelation that is always about to arrive and never does. At their centre lies an unknown that can't be sounded, edged with a hint of death. [Ligotti, Thinking Horror notes]
- A god that might exist is alive with mystery. A god declared to exist becomes a plastic idol. Treat the thing in your story the same way. [Ligotti, Thinking Horror notes]
- A writer needn't give the horror a fixed address or a name. Suggest a malignity that joins human corruption to the corruption of the world itself. [Ligotti, Supernaturalism]
- Pull at the leash of realism. Let a realistic story creep up to the border of the supernatural, and keep the reader unsure whether the man at its centre is merely wicked or has seen into a wicked world. [Ligotti, Supernaturalism]
- The horror sits where the human mind collides with the universe, not in a particular jungle or cellar. [Ligotti, Supernaturalism]

## The model story

- **Lovecraft's "The Music of Erich Zann" is the model.** A frightened narrator's mind and an unnamed thing outside it blur together. The musician holds the thing off with his playing. No explanation is ever offered that would imply a hidden order. [Ligotti, Supernaturalism]
- **The contrast.** A rival classic lets an unimaginative character explain the events as a sound from the fourth dimension. Even that much explanation gestures at a world where everything makes sense, and it weakens the horror. [Ligotti, Supernaturalism]
- **"Weird notes" only prose can play.** Impossible harmonies and colours that film can't reproduce belong to the page. Use them. [Ligotti, Supernaturalism]

## Let the supernatural pervade

- In the stronger of two great tragedies, a supernatural order colours every incident and reduces the characters to puppets. In the weaker one, the ghost only starts the plot and could be swapped for any other device. Make your supernatural the first kind. [Ligotti, Consuming Horror notes]
- Treat the possession, the haunting or the summoning as a means to ends eons in the making, not as the point. The person is a configuration the forces are using. [Ligotti, Style]
- Everyone is killable, and some characters destroy themselves just ahead of something worse. Jeopardy is permanent. It can be discovered but never defeated. [Ligotti, Style]

## Plotting dread

- **The accident model:**
  1. A firm grip on the day.
  2. A slide no one planned.
  3. Pure strangeness ("this can't be happening").
  4. The whisper that no place is safe and no rule still holds.
  5. Something set in motion that changes everything. [Ligotti, Plot]
- **Death as a visitor.** When death comes near, everything quivers with something unseen. Shapes form just out of sight, and the pieces arrange themselves into a design no one should learn. [Ligotti, Plot]
- **The face that is too much.** Escalate a familiar thing past its natural proportion: a smile that keeps widening until it is a doorway, and then the swallowing. [Ligotti, Plot]
- **The skeptical witness.** A convinced disbeliever is the ideal witness, because the reader travels with him into dreadful belief. In a world of miracles a ghost is merely natural. [Uncanny, Ward]
- **Leave the machinery dark.** Fix the time and place precisely, supply plenty of plain, factual detail, then, at the climax, keep the reader a little in the dark about how the supernatural works. [Uncanny, Ward]
- **Terror without a being.** A story can build supernatural dread without one supernatural being in it, by escalating slightly wrong, very specific details until rationalizing becomes impossible. [Uncanny, Dyson]
- **Reality itself unstable.** The strangest classic tales outlast their era because their weirdness can't be confined to one character's disordered mind. It seeps out and contaminates reality, and the characters turn out to be puppets of forces they can't see. [Uncanny, Jarvis]
- **Start from a what-if.** What if the town's dead kept their appointments? Put ordinary people in the predicament and watch them try to work free; don't plot the escape in advance. [King, On Writing]
- **The iceberg.** Know far more about the thing than you show. Whatever you know and leave out still weighs on the page. [McPhee, Omission]
- **Clichés lose their power.** Once a trope can be summarized in a sentence (the cursed book, the tentacled god, the hostile villagers), it stops frightening. Re-weird it, or find your own. [Uncanny, Budden]
