# Febrile, Concrete Prose

## Style is consciousness

- Style isn't the surface of the language. It is the writer's consciousness, exposed where the handling of the subject meets the handling of words. [Ligotti, Style]
- **Formula against vision:**
  - The formula novel dresses characters for doom or for survival.
  - It gives its holy men good deaths and kills an unpleasant drunk for thrills.
  - It ends with good winning, in plain, reportorial prose.
  - The visionary novel tells readers nothing reassuring and doesn't set them back down safely at the end.
  - Choose which you are writing. [Ligotti, Style]
- Lovecraft and Poe are attacked for "bad writing": emotional intensity and self-disclosure that break the rules of professional detachment. That possessed quality is why they are still read. [Ligotti, Sickness]

## The lyric devices

- Poetry, more than prose, has always been the natural home of the morbid and the fantastic. Carry poetry's tools into the story: rhythm, diction, imagery, tone. [Ligotti, Sickness]
- Metaphor is the most potent of those tools. It can call the fantastic into being. Chimneys that sprout overnight in a winter wind can become a devil's organ, and the world turns delirious. [Ligotti, Sickness]
- Febrile is the right word for nightmare literature. It names a fever, not only a manner. [Ligotti, Sickness]
- In healthy speech, metaphor decorates experience everyone shares. For someone pushed outside that frame, it is the only bridge across, so horror's metaphors carry experiences most readers have never had. [Ligotti, Sickness]
- When all the news is bad, the writing has to be beautiful: a silver pen for a black message. [Ligotti, Escaping]
- Even the most fevered writing runs on things the senses can touch: the smell of rotting metal, not "a smell of decay." [Ligotti, Living Horror notes]
- A story can state the misery outright in its first line, when the writer means it. [Ligotti, Sickness]

## Discipline from the other books

- Fresh, startling figures make the familiar unfamiliar; clichés are overfamiliar by definition. Set a homely image against a deadly scene (weapons stacked like party snacks), and set two images side by side so the reader makes the connection. [Uncanny, McKnight Hardy]
- A deliberately repeated adjective can thicken the dark: a blurred moon that sheds a blurred light. [Uncanny, McKnight Hardy]
- **Write it like you mean it.** Announcing that death was absolute, or telling the reader the sea was frightening, makes nobody shiver. [Uncanny, Royle]
- Use definite, specific, concrete language. Give the significant details, not all of them. [S&W R16]
- Every metaphor must be graspable at reading speed. A figure the reader has to decode stops the dread cold. [Cron ch6]
- A sentence that implies more than it says draws the reader into the dark with it. [Klinkenborg]

## Banned and allowed

- **Banned:** vague horror adjectives that stand in for an image: indescribable, unspeakable, eldritch, nameless (unless the scene shows a name failing), unholy, cyclopean (as decoration).
- **Allowed:** "unnamable" at the exact point where the prose has tried to name the thing and broken down, and the breakdown is on the page.
- **Audit:** `style-audit` keeps figurative intensity in horror and weird fiction when it is concrete and controlled.
