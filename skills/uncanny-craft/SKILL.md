# Uncanny Craft

This skill writes the strange that grows out of the familiar: the home, the face, the suburb, the beach, the self. It produces the **uncanny sheet** (`references/uncanny-sheet.md`), which goes into the story folder's design file:
- the familiar thing and how it turns;
- what stays dark and what must stay clear;
- the place, named exactly;
- point of view, tense and title;
- the escalation of slightly wrong details;
- the ending.

For cosmic dread (an indifferent universe, knowledge that can't be unlearned, the self as a puppet), pair it with `cosmic-horror`. If the story is also a crime or a chase, pair it with `suspense-craft`. `story-design` turns the sheet into structure, and `story-forge` drafts it.

**Important:** never quote or reproduce text from the source books. Paraphrase the ideas, use your own examples, and apply them to the person's story.

**About the source:** *Writing the Uncanny* collects essays by working writers, so its tags name the essayist: the word Uncanny, then the essayist's surname, or Intro for the editors.

## The core ideas

- The uncanny isn't simply the unknown. It is the familiar turned strange: something hidden or repressed has come into the open, and the fear leads back to what we have long known. [Uncanny, Intro]
- It is a mode, not a genre. A little unease can tighten any story, or unsettling the reader can be the whole point. [Uncanny, Intro]
- Negative space does the work: missing information, no explanation, no exposition. Readers fill the gaps with their own fears, and an absence can unnerve more than a sinister presence. [Uncanny, McKnight Hardy]
- The two strongest triggers are not knowing exactly where the fear comes from and not being able to say when the familiar dissolved. Good uncanny fiction raises questions and then declines to answer them. [Uncanny, McKnight Hardy]
- Keep the setting ordinary and reveal its strangeness. The mundane made frightening is the uncanny's home ground. [Uncanny, Moore]
- Accumulate rather than twist. Readers see twists coming, but a slow gathering of slightly wrong details makes the end shocking and inevitable at once. [Uncanny, McKnight Hardy]
- The strange needs a real world to stand on. Bring magic into an otherwise realistic story and the uncanny appears; hold the rational and supernatural readings open, and the strangeness leaks out of one character's head into the world. [Uncanny, Jarvis]
- The more absurd the material, the more it needs true emotion to anchor it. Readers must care. [Uncanny, Shearman]
- Meaning lives on the brink of knowing. Pin the strange thing down as a fact and it dies. [Ligotti, Thinking Horror notes]
- Know far more than you show, and step back so the reader does the building. What you leave out still weighs on the page. [McPhee, Omission]
- Let page one hint that something is off, and give enough of the normal world to measure the wrongness against. [Cron ch1]

## How to use this in practice

1. **Name the familiar thing** and how it turns, in one line: the home that rejects its owner, the mother with a stranger's face, the beach that is nearly the same beach. [Uncanny, Intro]
2. **Choose the mode** with the table below. [Uncanny, Budden]
3. **Decide what stays dark and what stays clear.** The nature of the strange may stay unexplained. What the people do and suffer, and why, must stay traceable. → `references/negative-space.md` [Cron ch8]
4. **Ground it in a real place,** named exactly, and let the place generate the story. → `references/place-and-lore.md` [Uncanny, Budden]
5. **Choose point of view, tense and title** for unease. → `references/negative-space.md` [Uncanny, McKnight Hardy]
6. **Plan the escalation:** precise, slightly odd detail before the first turn, then episodes that each build on the last, up to a throwaway moment no rational account survives. [Uncanny, Dyson]
7. **If there's a ghost, give it a job:** a purpose, rules, and a reason it shows itself now. → `references/ghosts.md` [Uncanny, Ashworth]
8. **If there's a double, a doll or a coincidence,** decide which of Freud's two kinds you're using. → `references/doubles-and-dolls.md` [Uncanny, Jarvis]
9. **If it's funny,** let the jokes and the horror rise together. → `references/comedy-and-dread.md` [Uncanny, Shearman]
10. **Design the ending:** close the human outcome, leave the strange open, and make the story feel as if it goes on past the page. [Uncanny, McKnight Hardy]
11. **Fill in the uncanny sheet** and paste it into `02-design.md` under `UNCANNY SHEET`.

## Choosing the mode

| Mode | What it is | Reach for it when |
|---|---|---|
| Uncanny | the familiar turned strange; the repressed coming back | the home, the family, the body, the face, the self |
| Eerie | signs of people and no people; something there that shouldn't be, or missing that should be | empty streets, ruins, edgelands, an out-of-season beach |
| Weird | an intrusion from outside; the thing in the wrong place | an object or creature that breaks the world's rules |
| Fantastic hesitation | the reader can't decide whether the cause is natural or supernatural | both readings must survive to the last page |
| Fairy tale | a wonder nobody in the story questions | retellings; set it in a real place with real psychology to make it uncanny |

- The weird is the thing that doesn't belong. The eerie belongs to places, and its sign is evidence of people who aren't there. [Uncanny, Budden]
- The first line chooses the mode. A once-upon-a-time opening makes marvels normal, a plain realist opening makes them delusion, and a wild-eyed start in mid-action makes them comic. Hoffmann opened with letters, one of them sent to the wrong person, to keep both readings alive. [Uncanny, Jarvis]
- A fairy tale is a wonder nobody questions, and explaining it ends the fairy tale. To make it uncanny, move from no explanation to several held open at once, and add a real place and real psychology. [Uncanny, Dean]
- McKee's horror subgenres line up with the table: a rational source, a supernatural one, and the super-uncanny, which never lets the audience settle between them. [McKee ch4]
- Pull at the leash of realism. Let a realistic story creep up to the border of the supernatural, and keep the reader unsure whether the trouble lies in a person or in the world. [Ligotti, Supernaturalism]

## Detail and description

- Innocent details, chosen and set side by side, can add up to something sinister. [Uncanny, Moore]
- Go beyond show-don't-tell: hint, suggest, imply, and treat silence as a positive form rather than a gap. [Uncanny, McKnight Hardy]
- Overwrought prose and over-explained thoughts kill the strange. So does announcing the fear: telling the reader the sea was frightening makes nobody shiver. [Uncanny, Royle]
- Description is started by the writer and finished by the reader. A few well-chosen details stand for the rest. [King, On Writing]
- Show the mood through behaviour. If you have to name it, you've lost it. [King, On Writing]
- The less a sentence carries, the more it can imply. Trust the reader: don't over-narrate, over-describe or over-interpret. [Klinkenborg]
- Don't explain too much. Telling everything is rarely the right call. [S&W V]
- Leave some states of mind unexplained. The explanations a writer leaves out can be a book's best parts. [Highsmith ch7]
- Explaining every ghost away shrinks a story. An early Gothic novelist did it, and her heroines were left facing nothing worse than a bad marriage. [Ligotti, Atmosphere]

## Endings

- Leave a sense of loss, an understanding not quite grasped, and a story that seems to go on past the page. Steer between total confusion and a neat bow, and resist the explanation that says "I told you so." [Uncanny, McKnight Hardy]
- Drop the expected happy ending or bloody revenge. A story left unsettled keeps playing in the reader's head. [Uncanny, Dean]
- Offer the rational explanation, then undercut it: it was only a likeness, until it comes back. [Uncanny, Ward]
- A short story can pull the reader to the cliff edge and stop. Abruptness and a refusal to explain can be the point. [Uncanny, Shearman]
- The climax must still change something absolutely and irreversibly for the people, even while the strange stays open. [McKee ch13]
- Every payoff needs its setup. Lay the slightly wrong details as dots the reader can look back along, so the end feels inevitable rather than arbitrary. [Cron ch10]
- In a series, each book still needs a complete arc and a definite outcome. Leave the strange open, not the story. [Scheuerer ch9]

## Where the books disagree

- **How much to explain?** The essayists (McKnight Hardy, Ward on M. R. James, Dean, Jarvis on Todorov) raise questions they refuse to answer and keep the machinery dark. McKee wants holes closed and no deus ex machina, Cron wants cause and effect, and Highsmith calls arbitrary withholding an unfair gimmick. Resolution used here: withhold the nature of the strange, never the cause and effect of what the characters do and suffer. Negative space is an absence whose shape the reader can feel. False mystery is hiding what the viewpoint character plainly knows. Allow the first, never the second.
- **Show or imply?** King and Cron say show. McKnight Hardy says neither show nor tell: hint. Klinkenborg's implication sides with her. Resolution: imply through a few concrete, selected, slightly wrong details, never through vagueness.
- **How much description?** McKnight Hardy withholds description to make readers work. Highsmith paints the main faces with care, King keeps description lean, and S&W want concrete detail. Resolution: they agree on a few precise details, chosen so that one of them is off.
- **Open or closed ending?** The uncanny leaves loss and disorientation, not a bow. McKee wants an ending with a charge, and Scheuerer wants each book in a series complete. Resolution: close the human outcome and leave the strange open, and get there by accumulation, not a twist.
- **Build the world or distort it?** Shearman says short stories distort worlds rather than build them. Diamond derives worlds from causes. Resolution: build the ordinary world fully and causally, so its distortion registers, and leave the strange element underived.
- **Breaking point-of-view rules.** Ashworth lets ghosts break point-of-view rules. Highsmith and McKee say an unwarranted viewpoint break weakens a story. Resolution: declare the new rule early and keep it.
- **Should the lead react?** Cron wants the protagonist to react visibly so the reader knows how to feel. Dyson's Aickman has the lead not react at all to the impossible. Resolution: the lead reacts to ordinary things in ways the reader understands, so a non-reaction to the impossible reads as a signal. It only works against that baseline.
- **Awe or dread?** Dyson's Aickman shows honest strangeness readmitting awe, even hope. Ligotti finds existence malignantly useless. Resolution: the uncanny can end in awe, loss or dread, and cosmic horror ends in dread. Choose per story and keep the opening's promise.
- **Coincidence.** McKee allows coincidence early, where it gains meaning, and never at the end. Freud makes uncanny coincidence a legitimate device. Resolution: uncanny coincidence accumulates meaning and never resolves the plot.
- **Fairy tale or realism?** Freud says the animistic uncanny needs common reality, and Dean adds real place and psychology to tale elements. They agree: ground the marvel in the real.
