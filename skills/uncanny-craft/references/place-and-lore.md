# Place, Land and Lore

## Setting as the story

- Stories grow from their environment, and a setting's dull incidental detail is as much the story as the plot. These stories couldn't happen anywhere else. Use that for real issues: housing-crisis horror can rise from a canal boat whose water turns against the couple who fled there. [Uncanny, Budden]
- The humdrum is already weird, always about to spill some deep truth. Reweirding a lived-in place gives everyday life weight and layers. [Uncanny, Budden]
- Name places exactly (not a one-way system but that one) and let the opening line put the reader on the actual streets. [Uncanny, Budden]
- The most mysterious places now are the ones nobody looks at: outer suburbs, tired shopping centres, underpasses, flyovers, quiet residential streets, and the edgelands of depots and slip roads at a town's rim. [Uncanny, Budden]
- The method: notice everyday oddness (things at the corner of the eye, a presence or an absence on a walk, the mood of utilitarian places), pin down exactly what frightened you, and don't brush it aside for the consensus. [Uncanny, Budden]
- Tropes you can sum up in a sentence (hostile villagers, the field that hides a secret, the dug-up object that dooms the curious) have lost their power. Reweird instead. [Uncanny, Budden]
- A true story can't be moved: it belongs to one place and one time. Keep the world small enough to know completely. [McKee ch3]
- Build the ordinary place from its causes (why the estate is half empty, why the shops shut) so its wrongness has something solid to push against. Behind each obvious cause, ask for the deeper one. [Diamond, Prologue]
- The same place changes with the hour, the season and the point of view, and it can stand for a character's inner state or fear. [Tomlinson ch7]
- Build atmosphere through every sense, and a sixth: the smell of a room, a cough upstairs, the colour of the wallpaper. [Highsmith ch7]
- Atmosphere comes from inside the writer (sensations, memories, moods), not from a predecessor's furniture. [Ligotti, Atmosphere]

## Houses

- Forbidden spaces feed the imagination: the stair you may not climb, the corridor that turns into darkness. [Uncanny, Roberts]
- A house can stand for a body and then a mind, its rooms busy with unseen forces, where exploring brings pleasure and danger together. The truly uncanny house is one where real violence happened, and a whole country can be haunted by what it tried to forget. [Uncanny, Roberts]
- Strong emotion can spill into a place. A house can shake with its heroine's disorder, and a woman never wanted anywhere can seem wanted by it. [Uncanny, Moore]
- Make the dreadful place physically comfortable, and the soft bed becomes the site of horror. Dread can sit in a picnic on a lawn. [Uncanny, Moore]
- Enclose by stages (country, gate, garden, house, then the forbidden closet) and put the horror in the innermost box. [Uncanny, Ward]
- A brand-new house can feel haunted, not by a presence but by the gloom of what is going to happen there. [Highsmith ch7]
- Choose a house whose fate is bound to its people, so its decay mirrors theirs. [Ligotti, Atmosphere]
- In a series, give readers a few standing sets they come to know, including a forbidden place or one with a history. Those are the places the uncanny should turn. [Tomlinson ch7]

## Coasts and edges

- The beach is a go-between: land and sea, there and gone, carrying messages to and from the deep. Dunes belong to neither, and anything can happen there. [Uncanny, Royle]
- A scene that repeats exactly, where some marks wash away and others never do, feels sealed off from the world. The loop is the uncanny. [Uncanny, Royle]
- Let the setting invade: sand in the hotel room, in the mirror, in the bed. [Uncanny, Royle]
- The replaced place: after a high tide it is nearly the same beach, as if rebuilt from memory by someone who once knew it, and the character becomes a stranger at home. [Uncanny, Royle]

## Folklore and culture

- Adults reason childhood dread away (the sense of something watching from the corner). But why is the cat staring at that corner? Start there. [Uncanny, Emelumadu]
- Write what you know, at least at first. A culture where the supernatural is part of daily life is a deep source, and modern against traditional is a strong axis: a scoffing clerk who still falls flat before the night spirit, and an ending that won't say whether it was spirit, imagination or fate. [Uncanny, Emelumadu]
- A mundane motive can walk a character into horror, like a caterer worried about rent who learns what the job really wants her to cook. Flip the power, too: take it from whoever usually holds it, and treat the extraordinary as everyday. The uncanny frightens in daylight too. [Uncanny, Emelumadu]
- Prefer a society's lasting fears (failed harvests, disease, barrenness, betrayal) to the topical. Borrow from ballads and legends without plagiarizing, and mine dreams for ideas. [Uncanny, Emelumadu]
- Read "write what you know" broadly. The heart and the imagination know things too. [King, On Writing]
- Choose allusions that will last. Topical references date fast; old tales keep. [McPhee, Frame of Reference]

## Fairy tales

- Tales move into our places: a troll under every bridge, a forest in any scrap of edgeland. They have no owners and their patterns repeat with small changes, so they feel familiar and unfamiliar at once. [Uncanny, Dean]
- Hunt the stories of a place: local-history shelves, old newspaper forums, a charm hung in a window, an object pulled from a well. Traditions crust onto places; dig and you reach the old forest. [Uncanny, Dean]
- Fairy tale's symbolic language (glass, forest, needle, blade, well, tunnel) strikes the body. Make those sensations subtly strange and the dread is felt with the whole body. Invent new tales in that language that feel remembered from childhood. [Uncanny, Dean]
- Readers accept a story's people only while they are sure their own world is more real. Shake that certainty and the tale gets out of the book. [Ligotti, Creating Horror notes]
