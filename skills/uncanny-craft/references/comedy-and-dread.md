# Comedy and Dread

- In an absurd world the comedy lives in the context. A punchline would shrink it to one moment, so use comedy as an ingredient: the ironic beat, the pratfall inside a tragedy. [Uncanny, Shearman]
- Take a figure of speech literally and follow the consequences, then escalate by degrees. The man who was beside himself now has to share his chair. Offer a false escape before the last image shows what the escape cost. [Uncanny, Shearman]
- There's no reset button. A sitcom resets at the credits; a dark tale leaves its people permanently changed. Decline to describe the worst and the reader imagines worse, then feels guilty for laughing. [Uncanny, Shearman]
- Explaining the monster redeems it and kills the comedy. Ghastliness dressed as a happy ending is funnier and worse. [Uncanny, Shearman]
- The kit is deadpan matter-of-factness, a naive innocent, lurching structure, blood breaking through grey calm, and a teller who isn't on the reader's side. Use the audience's fear of missing the point: stay ahead and pull the rug. [Uncanny, Shearman]
- Fire joke after joke at the audience while slowly revealing a horrific context, starting with small hints of wrongness. The content gets sicker as the jokes get funnier, and the audience's complicity becomes the subject. [Uncanny, Shearman]
- Great short stories pose as anecdotes, amiable and easy, then echo far beyond their size. A novel lets its hero win; a short story has time only to watch him drown, best when he believes he has won. [Uncanny, Shearman]
- Magnify an absurd, banal activity until the reader recoils and sees what matters underneath, such as a broken bond between father and son. A first-person teller who keeps insisting things are fine exposes his own emptiness. [Uncanny, Shearman]
- Comedy in short fiction distorts reality so we see it, and see that its "normal" is a choice. Laughing and screaming are both things the body does. Implied violence works only while it stays implied, and if your comedy doesn't make you laugh, stop. [Uncanny, Shearman]
- A dry, droll tone for absurd things, with sharp skewering of the character's snobbery, lets the comedy give way to unease. [Uncanny, Dyson]
- The comic writer is an angry idealist. Ask what you're angry about, and give the comic character an obsession he can't see. [McKee ch16]
- Comedy also paces dread. A lighter beat after a climax lets the reader gather breath for the next turn. [McKee ch12]
